# The Bell Below

_The eastern mountain · A fourth movement of The Borrowed River_

## 22 · The Forest Above the Road

_The fern hollow · An unsounded place_

The ground rang under Iria's sounding pole. Brío put his weight back before she did.

Below the trail, the fern hollow seemed to end in a wall of green. She parted the fronds with the pole. There was no wall. A drop fell past the tip and vanished into a roof of leaves so broad that a person could have slept on one. Far below, a second roof caught the water. Beyond it a third. The entire forest hung inside the cleft, fed by rain collected on the high plateau and released a terrace at a time.

Something crossed the first leaf. Six legs, a shell like folded bark, a forelimb long enough to bridge the gap to the next. It lowered its head into a little basin at the leaf's root. As it drank, the leaf dipped. Water spilled into the basin below and the creature ran uphill, impossibly quick for its size.

Iria pulled back. Brío had already chosen a ledge wide enough for both of them.

Tami appeared on the opposite rim with a climbing line over one shoulder. She saw Iria, then the space beneath her feet, and stopped smiling.

“The old map puts a road there.”

“Cael's map leaves it blank.”

“I prefer his.”

Tami had spent two days following the high swifts. They had abandoned the eastern updraft, not because the wind had changed but because a band of warm air now rose from the wrong side of the ravine. She pointed out a pale strip around the exposed stone. An old waterline, three body lengths above the leaves.

“The whole cleft used to fill?” Iria asked.

“In the long rain. Now the water's going under it.”

Below them the shellback drank again. A small one followed, its legs slipping. Iria had taken the movement for a hunting pair. The larger creature caught the smaller against its side before the leaf tipped. She thought of the jaguar at the broken bridge and withdrew the certainty before it reached her mouth.

They left Brío on firm ground with Tami's mare and took the old service steps along the rim. Every third tread was cut from black stone; the others were newer, poured around the roots. People had repaired this road for centuries without agreeing on what lay under it. The oldest spans curved toward a structure visible only when the clouds thinned: an inverted tower, its point driven deep into the cliff and its broad roof hidden by the forest. Seven enormous ribs carried the weight of the road above. Along each rib hung a chain of counterweights, some high as houses. A city could have stood on the roof. Perhaps one had.

Tami lay flat and drew the tower in her route book. “If the water reaches that rim, the shells can climb out. If the road fails first, they can't.”

“Do you know what they eat?”

“No.” Tami turned a page. “I'll ask the people who live here.”

At the next stair a bell rang from within the cliff. Not the river's warning bell. This one had no metal tongue. The stone itself sounded, and each counterweight shifted a finger's width. A hairline opened through the mortar at Tami's feet.

Iria caught her sleeve. The tread fell away. It took the loose end of the climbing line with it and struck the first leaf below. The shellbacks vanished into the canopy. Somewhere under the roots, a second bell answered.

Tami stared at the missing step. “That was the road I used this morning.”

Iria found a dry patch for a message flare and marked it with three stones so a courier would see it from the upper trail. She sent Brío back with a written account tied to his saddle, not because he could find the Academy but because he knew Mireya's station and the track was sound as far as the last ford. He resisted leaving her. She walked him to the bend herself, waited until he chose the descent, and watched his white star disappear.

The bell sounded again. This time both women were on firm rock.

## 23 · The City That Had Kept Its Receipts

_The Bell Below · First chamber_

Luz reached the hollow before the Academy did. She brought two keepers, a pack mule, and the name of the woman who still worked the old tower.

“Her name is Oren,” she said. “She has been asking for a second operator for eleven years.”

Oren met them at a door cut into the mountain's underside. She was small, with one arm held close against an old injury. She carried a ring of keys and a brass comb whose teeth were worn to different lengths. She pushed it into a seam in the wall. Three teeth hummed. One did not.

“The lower bearing is dry,” she said. “Someone has been drawing the reserve toward the river circuit. I shut the first compartment. The other two won't answer the keys.”

“Who built it?” Tami asked.

“The Oriván made the first pressure vault. My mother's crew made the part you can walk through. Neither trusted a single hand with the water.” Oren looked at the comb. “We did, later.”

Inside, the first chamber opened around a shaft wider than the Academy courtyard. Stone galleries crossed it at different heights; on the far side, a line of suspended cisterns climbed into darkness. When water moved through a channel, the entire space changed pitch. The sound travelled under Iria's boots, up the walls, out through the bell-shaped vents above the forest. The shellbacks had followed those pulses into the hanging leaves for generations. People in the valley used the same sound to judge the next day's river.

Modern copper wire had been stapled across an older carving. The carving showed three hands on three separate wheels. Below each, a narrow tile had been fitted into the stone. Its surface carried columns of tiny notches, a record of permitted draws, floods, repairs, and names. There were hundreds. Iria thought of Elvia's tile in Miralumbre: another system built so a draw had to bear a name. This was a different watershed, with its own keepers. At the end of the row, someone had cut a fourth notch and left the operator column blank. Oren asked for a rubbing before anyone moved the tile again.

“They left us their measurements,” Oren said. “We called the whole place a ruin because we stopped reading them.”

Iva arrived with the upper water figures, soaked from the climb. She took one look at the three carved hands and began copying the columns. A courier from Ema followed with an order to keep the chamber closed pending inquiry.

“It is closed,” Oren said. “The bearing is still losing water.”

The courier did not try to arrest a leak with the paper. He helped Iva carry her measuring gear down the gallery.

The second chamber held a pressure gate three stories tall. Behind it, water pressed against smoked glass. Pale organisms travelled through it in long coiling ribbons, each body no wider than a finger but the shoal broad as a street. The bell sounded. The shoal folded toward the gate before the water did.

“They know before the gauge,” Iria said.

“They feel the pressure change,” Iva said. “So do you, through the floor. Give me the number.”

The needle on Oren's gauge rose. Iva called the mark. Luz ran it to the keeper above, who signalled the families camped near the lower road. Tami took the cliff path to the roost signal point. Aru could carry a message in clear air, Nara had warned her, but there was no clear air tonight. Tami would have to climb.

At the third gallery a man in a guard coat blocked the way. His badge was covered with blue wax. He held a coil of copper wire in one hand and a cutter in the other.

“Leave the lower key,” he told Oren. “The road stays up until the convoy gets through.”

Oren knew him. “Varek. Your children live beneath that road.”

“That's why I'm here.”

He had been paid to keep the convoy road stable for two days, he said. The lower farms could manage a little less water. The contract did not say how much. Neither had the instrument. When he learned the cisterns were nearly empty, he had cut the listening line; the bell was making people stop the carts.

Iva put the gauge in front of him. “It won't last two days.”

He looked at the needle, then past them toward the gate. It rose another mark.

The stone bell rang hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling. Varek dropped the cutter. The gate's middle pin shot across the gallery, struck the rail, and fell into the shaft. The water held for one breath, then found the gap.

## 24 · Three Hands on the Wheel

_The Bell Below · The third chamber_

Iria went down under the first spray. It struck her shoulder like a thrown bag of gravel.

Oren dragged the lower key from her ring and threw it to Iva. “Blue wheel. Three turns against the flow. No fourth.”

Varek ran toward the fallen pin. He was not going to put it back; even at the rail he could see it was gone. He reached the third wheel and shouted that the convoy was still on the upper road.

“Then signal them off,” Luz said.

“They can't turn the wagons.”

“Unload them. People can walk.”

She shoved her pack into his hands, its signal flags tied across the top. Varek went. He had made this emergency and knew the quickest way to reach the road.

Iva worked the blue wheel with one keeper. Iria and Oren reached the lower gallery, where the old Oriván ribs met the newer stone. The third wheel was not there. Its housing had been stripped, a replacement copper band wound tight around the shaft. That band was drawing from the cisterns toward the river system, holding the road in place by emptying the forest's reserve.

“If we cut it?” Iria asked.

“The road settles. If we don't, the gate bursts and takes the farms.”

Oren set the comb against the housing. Only two teeth sang. Iria could see a pale seam where the fastener bit into living root, but the faultglass had equalized and the seam gave her no map of the wall beyond. She put her hand on the stone. Foundation practice let her steady one small piece while Oren loosened its pin. Pain climbed her forearm. She stopped before her fingers locked.

“Again,” Oren said.

“In a minute.”

Oren looked up, surprised. Iria had once mistaken stopping for a failure of nerve. Here it made room for the keeper to take her place. They traded positions. The keeper held the pin while Iria used Varek's cutter to sever the band.

Upstairs the blue wheel turned. Water struck the glass gate. The pale shoal flowed away from it in one long silver twist. Through the shaft came Tami's signal, passed down from the roost in the pattern they had practised with Nara: the convoy was off the road; the lower homes were emptying.

“Second compartment clear,” Iva called. “Oren, can you close the third by hand?”

“With the balance weight released. I'll lose the upper road.”

“You will lose its surface,” Iva said. “The ribs stay if we leave the flood bed open.”

Oren had spent eleven years protecting that road. Iria watched her measure the distance to the release lever. No light around her offered an answer. Oren handed the comb to the keeper so someone else could check her work and pulled.

Above them the great weights fell one after another. The road cracked with a sound that seemed to come from every direction. The gallery pitched. Iria hit the rail; Oren caught her coat. For a moment both hung over the shaft while water rose in the dark beneath them. The keeper pulled them back, tearing the coat at its seam.

Then the bell stopped.

Silence made the place seem deeper. A thin stream crossed the cut band and found the old channel to the hanging forest. It would take days to refill the cisterns, months to rebuild the road, and longer to learn which contracts had relied on a reserve nobody had consented to spend. None of that could be accomplished by holding a lever for one heroic minute.

Outside, the lower valley was a scatter of moving lamps. People and pack animals climbed toward Luz's marked shelters. Half the high road had slumped into the fern hollow. The broad leaves had folded under a rain of stones, but the far canopy still held. From it came a small clicking sound, then another.

Iria sat on the threshold with her torn coat over her knees. Tami arrived at dawn, mud to her waist, furious that Iria had gone below without her. Iva came out behind them and told Tami exactly which task had been hers. Neither apologized quickly.

Oren brought the witness tiles into daylight. Varek came down from the convoy road and gave Ema the name of the contractor who had paid him. Ema wrote it beside his own. The contractor's seal did not match the one on the coastal marker; the inquiry had a lead, not an ending.

Cael's reply to Iria's message reached the valley that afternoon. He had opened his old schematics to the keepers and found an error in the way the bands estimated stored water. He would correct it under their review. He had not asked when she would come back.

At Mireya's station, Brío had refused two attempts to send him home and waited under the dry awning. Iria walked the final mile to him. He sniffed the torn seam of her coat and turned toward the feed bin. She laughed, exhausted, and fetched him water first.

Beyond the stable, the mountain gave one low note as the restored channel filled. The sound was no promise. It was a measurement the valley could hear again.
