## Scene seed

At the hour when the village stopped pretending dusk was only dusk, Mira found the cup beneath the sombraluz tree.

It was a small cup, chipped at the rim, with a blue line painted around its middle. It belonged to Lucía. Everyone knew it belonged to Lucía because Lucía had once thrown it at a wall after an argument with someone she no longer spoke to, and the cup had missed the wall, landed in the plaza dirt, and survived with only the chip. The story had become part of the village, as things did when no one was trying to make them important.

Mira picked it up because it was upside down and rain might come later.

The cup was warm.

Not warm from the day. The stones had already begun giving their heat back to the air, and the leaves above her moved with the soft, unhurried sound of people deciding not to interrupt. This warmth was held inside the clay. Along the blue line, a thin light gathered and went out, gathered and went out.

Mira knew better than to call for everyone.

She had learned, in the weeks since Bela began walking with her at dusk, that a destello was not an announcement. Sometimes it was only a person being a person with more of the edges visible.

Still, she crouched there with the cup in both hands, listening.

From the bakery came Don Emilio's three notes. Not a song, exactly. He hummed them while he swept, the same way he breathed. The floración azul beneath his window had opened so fully that the small blue petals looked like pieces of sky that had forgotten where to go.

At the far side of the plaza, Lucía sat on the low wall beside the fountain. Her guitar lay across her knees, untouched. She was staring at the water as if she had asked it a question and it had been rude enough to answer honestly.

Bela arrived without making the plaza feel smaller. She leaned her walking stick against the tree and stood beside Mira.

—¿Lo ves? Mira asked.

Bela looked at the cup. Her face did not change.

—Veo que encontraste una taza.

Mira lowered it a little. The light had almost gone now, leaving only the ordinary blue paint and the chip near the rim.

—Pero estaba brillando.

—Tal vez.

—¿No quieres saber por qué?

Bela sat on the root that rose from the ground like the back of a sleeping animal. For a while she said nothing. The plaza had room for nothing. It did not need to be filled.

—Querer saber y tener derecho a saber are different hungers, she said at last.

Mira turned the cup in her hands. There was a thumbprint baked into its underside. It made the cup seem suddenly less like a thing and more like somebody's small mistake that had decided to stay.

—But if I can see it—

—Then you can see it.

—That is all?

Bela nodded toward Lucía, who had begun to tune one string without looking at it.

—When the river shows you a stone at the bottom, little one, does that mean you pull it out?

Mira thought of Río Claro after rain: the water pale and quick over the rounded piedra viva, every stone visible until the current changed its mind. She thought of the fish that old men sometimes held too long, hoping the silver at their scales would tell them something useful.

—No, she said.

—Why not?

—Because it belongs where it is.

Bela smiled once, but gently, as if smiling too hard might frighten the answer away.

The blue line on the cup brightened. Mira saw—not a picture, not a voice, not a secret—but the shape of a held-back song. It sat behind Lucía's ribs like a bird refusing a hand. Mira felt the old pull in herself: the wish to be useful by knowing first, the wish to run across the plaza and say the exact right thing.

Lucía looked up.

Her eyes found Mira. Then the cup.

Mira stood. Her knees were dusty. She walked to the fountain slowly enough that Lucía could have stopped her if she wanted.

—You left this, she said.

Lucía took the cup. Her fingers closed around the chip.

—Thank you.

The answer was ordinary. It was also, Mira understood, complete.

For one breath the cup's blue line shone against Lucía's palm. Lucía saw it too. Her mouth changed—not into a smile, not away from one—and she set the cup beside her guitar.

—Do you know a song for when someone has been angry a very long time? Lucía asked.

Mira did. Everybody did. It was the one Don Emilio hummed while he swept, though no one had ever said where he had learned it.

Mira began with the first note. Lucía followed on the second. From the bakery, the third arrived as if it had been waiting for permission.

The plaza did not become a temple. No one knelt. The sombraluz leaves did not applaud.

But the shade beneath them grew a little less cold.
