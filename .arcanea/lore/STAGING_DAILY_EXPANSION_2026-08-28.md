## Scene: The Light That Did Not Choose

The courier came to the Crown Gate after sunset, carrying two letters in the same hand.

The first had been sealed in green wax. Its paper smelled faintly of rain. The second bore no seal at all; someone had folded it so many times that the creases had worn thin as cloth.

The courier set both letters on the stone before Aiyami.

“Tell me which one is right,” they said.

Beyond the threshold, the court held the last warmth of the day. The upper sky was still pale, though the paths below the hill had already become indistinct. Darkness had gathered under the trees and along the low walls, quiet and ordinary. It did not threaten the light. It simply waited where the light had not reached.

Aiyami did not touch the letters.

“Read them,” she said.

The courier opened the green seal first.

“‘The north sluice has split. If the gate is opened before dawn, the lower rooms will take water. If it stays closed, the fields beyond the ridge will dry before the next rain. Send an order. There is no time to gather every household.’”

They placed the letter down and unfolded the second.

“‘The lower rooms hold the sick and the very young. The ridge fields hold the season's grain. We have moved what we can. The people below say open the gate; the people above say keep it shut. Send an order. There is no time to argue.’”

The two pieces of paper lay between them like opposed hands.

Aiyami looked at the courier. “What do you know?”

“The sluice has split.”

“What else?”

“The lower rooms may flood if the gate opens. The fields may fail if it stays closed.”

“May?”

The courier's mouth tightened. “The reports use the word.”

“Then keep it.”

The courier looked down.

They had crossed the hill with the letters pressed against their ribs, trying to make the word disappear. *May* was too thin a thing to carry into a decision that would fill rooms with water or leave a season bare. They had wanted Aiyami to replace it with a harder word—*will*, perhaps, or *must*.

“What do you think?” Aiyami asked.

“The lower rooms should be saved.”

“Why?”

“Because people are in them.”

“And the fields?”

“The fields are not people.”

Aiyami waited.

The courier felt the answer turn in their chest. It was not wrong. It was not enough.

“The fields feed people,” they said at last.

“Yes.”

“Then I should keep the gate shut.”

“Why?”

“Because hunger lasts longer than water.”

Aiyami waited again.

The courier closed their eyes.

“Water takes people now,” they said.

The court did not grow brighter. No voice rose from the threshold to reward the answer. Somewhere above the court, Sol answered the silence once. The sound held no verdict in it.

“I cannot make both losses vanish,” the courier said.

“No,” Aiyami replied. “You cannot.”

The words were not cruel. That made them harder to resist.

The courier had imagined enlightenment as a height from which all roads became visible and all conflict became a failure of lesser sight. They had imagined that someone who saw far enough could point to one road and leave no shadow beneath the other.

Instead, Aiyami was looking at them as if the two letters had not come to a goddess at all. As if they had come to the person carrying them.

“Then tell me what to do,” the courier said. “That is why I came.”

“Is it?”

The courier stared at her.

Aiyami picked up the unsealed letter. “You came because you do not want the decision to belong to you.”

“That is not fair.”

“No. It is accurate.”

The courier's hand went to the strap across their shoulder, where the weight of the message case had rubbed a red line into their skin. “I am only the courier.”

“Then carry the truth of the letters without pretending they are orders.”

“They asked for an order.”

“They asked for someone else to carry the part of the choice that frightens them.”

The courier felt anger rise, clean and immediate. “They are frightened because there is no good answer.”

“Yes.”

“Then why should I bring them more uncertainty?”

Aiyami turned toward the dim paths below the hill.

“Do not call uncertainty a failure because it will not flatter your need to be finished,” she said. “Nero's darkness is not evil. It is the part of a thing not yet formed, not yet known. Shadow is corruption—Void without Spirit. Do not give Shadow's name to a question merely because the question asks you to remain responsible.”

The courier said nothing.

Aiyami's voice remained calm.

“Light does not become wiser by claiming it shines where it does not.”

The first letter stirred in the evening air. The green seal had been broken cleanly, but a thin rim of wax still held the impression of the hand that had pressed it.

“What can I give them?” the courier asked.

“Begin with what is true.”

“The sluice has split.”

“And?”

“The lower rooms and the ridge fields cannot both be protected by the same act before dawn.”

“And?”

The courier looked at the letters for a long time.

“No one outside the valley can decide which loss their hands will live with.”

Aiyami nodded once.

It was not permission to leave. It was not absolution. It was a shape the courier could carry.

They took both letters back and turned them over. On the blank side of the green-sealed one, they wrote:

> The sluice has split. Opening it may flood the lower rooms. Keeping it closed may cost the ridge fields. These are the facts I can carry without borrowing the future. Gather at the gate before dawn. Bring those who know the rooms, the fields, the sick, the stores, and the water. Choose in sight of one another. Do not let the loudest voice turn another household's loss invisible.

The courier read the words once, then looked up.

“And if they ask me to choose anyway?”

“Then tell them what you would protect first,” Aiyami said. “Tell them why. Let your judgment be a gift they may weigh, not a crown you place on your own head to avoid hearing theirs.”

The courier folded the letters together, though they did not fit neatly now.

At the edge of the court, Sol moved somewhere beyond the reach of the remaining light. The courier could not see the Godbeast clearly. They could only hear the small shift of presence and understand that being watched was not the same as being rescued.

They started down the hill.

The paths were dark enough that the courier had to walk slowly. More than once, they wished for the old answer to return: one command, one clean line, one person to blame when the water came or did not come.

It never did.

By the time the first houses appeared below, the courier still did not know which choice the valley would make.

But the choice no longer had the shelter of a false certainty.

And that was enough light to begin.
