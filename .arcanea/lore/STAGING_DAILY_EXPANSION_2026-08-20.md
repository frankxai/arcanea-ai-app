## Packet purpose

A contained **STAGING** teaching parable. A seeker wants to turn iron into a knife after failing to protect someone they love. A broken water-cart needs the same iron before nightfall. Draconia refuses the easier false choice: neither fear nor practical care is treated as cowardice. The scene asks what protection becomes when the need to act is real but the desired weapon is not the only possible answer.

## Scene: The Iron That Did Not Become a Knife

The seeker brought a length of iron to the forge before the sun had cleared the ridge.

It was not fine iron. Its surface had been scraped clean in one place and left black in another; a twist near one end showed where it had once belonged to something built for weight rather than war. The seeker set it on the anvil and did not remove their gloves.

Draconia was standing at the bellows.

She looked at the iron, then at the seeker.

“Knife?”

The seeker nodded.

“Why?”

The question came too quickly. The seeker had expected instruction: heat here, strike there, do not let the edge cool before it learned what it was for.

“Because I needed one yesterday.”

Draconia drew the bellows once. The coals answered with a low red breath.

“Yesterday is over.”

“It is not over for the person I left behind.”

That made Draconia still.

The seeker had not meant to say it. They had meant to say that a crowd had formed near the market gate, that someone had shoved their friend to the ground, that hands had appeared too fast and too many to count. They had meant to say that they ran for help and returned after the crowd had gone. They had meant to say that an empty hand was a kind of failure.

Instead they said, “I could have stopped it.”

“No,” Draconia said. “You might have changed it. Those are not the same claim.”

The seeker looked away.

The forge was open on one side. Beyond it, the morning held its ordinary shadows under carts and eaves. Nothing in them moved wrong. Nothing asked to be blamed for being dark.

Draconia picked up the iron with tongs and held it above the coals.

“A knife can be useful,” she said. “It can also make a frightened hand feel certain before that hand has learned what it is touching.”

“So I should do nothing?”

“No.” The word struck harder than a hammer. “You came here to do something. Do not insult that hunger by calling it only rage.”

Before the seeker could answer, a cart rattled into the yard.

One wheel dragged. The cart's water barrel was lashed down with rope, but the iron brace beneath it had split along the bolt-hole. A woman walking beside the cart held the reins in one hand and the cracked brace in the other. Two small vessels hung empty from the rail at her hip.

She saw the forge and exhaled.

“Can you mend it?”

Draconia did not look at the seeker.

The seeker looked at the brace. The split was narrow, but the metal had thinned around it. A patch would fail. A new piece could be shaped from the length on the anvil.

The same length that could become a knife.

The woman followed the seeker's gaze.

“I can come back,” she said, though the barrel rocked each time the cart settled. “There are other wells.”

“How far?” the seeker asked.

“Far enough that the water will be warm when I return.”

Draconia set the tongs down.

“There,” she said. “Now you have a choice worth your time.”

The seeker felt the first clean heat of anger.

“That is not fair.”

“Of course it is not.” Draconia's mouth turned, not into a smile. Into recognition. “Fair would be finding enough iron for both. We have what we have.”

The woman began to apologize. Draconia raised one hand, and the apology stopped.

The seeker imagined the knife: narrow, bright, undeniable. They imagined returning to the market gate with it at their side. They imagined the next crowd seeing the blade before anyone they loved hit the ground.

Then they imagined the barrel without its brace. The cart leaning. The water spilled into dust before it reached the people waiting for it.

Neither image made the other disappear.

“Make the brace,” the seeker said.

Their own voice surprised them.

Draconia lifted the iron from the anvil.

“Say the cost.”

“I will not have the knife.”

“Again.”

“I will not have the knife when I want it.”

The Fire Gate Goddess nodded once.

“Good. Now say what you are choosing.”

The seeker watched the iron enter the coals. Its black surface took the color slowly, then all at once.

“I am choosing that someone gets home with water.”

“Not instead of protection,” Draconia said. “As protection.”

The hammer was heavier than the seeker expected. Draconia did not take it from them. She corrected no grip, softened no blow. When the metal bent too sharply, the seeker had to heat it again. When the bolt-hole drifted, the seeker had to widen it and start the measurement over.

The work took longer than a knife would have taken. That was part of the cost too.

At the open side of the forge, something answered from beyond the yard: a single sound from Draconis, too deep to mistake for approval and too clear to mistake for threat. The coals brightened. Draconia did not turn toward it.

When the brace was finished, the seeker carried it to the cart with both hands.

The woman fitted it beneath the barrel. The wheel no longer dragged.

She reached into her pocket.

“No,” said the seeker.

She paused.

“I did not do it for payment.”

“Then why did you do it?”

The seeker looked back at the forge. Draconia had returned to the bellows. The anvil held only scale and ash where the knife had been imagined.

“Because I was afraid,” the seeker said. “And because that was not the only thing I could make.”

The woman considered this, then climbed onto the cart.

When she had gone, the yard felt larger and less forgiving.

The seeker stood beside the cold anvil.

“What do I do when it happens again?” they asked.

Draconia turned the bellows once. A new breath rose from the coals.

“Go toward the person you would protect,” she said. “Call for help. Learn whose hands can stand with yours. If a blade is truly needed, learn what it costs before you ask it to make you brave.”

The seeker looked at their empty hands.

They were still empty.

But they were no longer waiting to be told what that meant.
