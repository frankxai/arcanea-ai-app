# El Vado del Murmullo — The Measure That Was Not Taken

## Packet purpose

A contained **STAGING** Veldorian scene in which Mira and Señor Vidal disagree honestly about what can be said before it is known. Mira wants her perception to count. Vidal wants the village safe without turning a child's sight into public proof. Neither is dismissed; their incompatible needs create the scene's pressure.

**Placement:** an unplaced ordinary afternoon during Mira's eleventh year. No chapter order, lasting incident, or novel chronology is asserted.

## Scene seed

Señor Vidal had put three stones on the riverbank in a row, which meant he was worried.

Mira knew this because the first stone was pale, the second had a black seam, and the third was the size of a loaf of bread. They were not useful together. A person who wanted to build something chose stones for their shape. A person who wanted to remember chose them for the order in which they had been found.

Marisol stood beside her with one paw lifted.

The Vado del Murmullo lay below the willowless bend of Río Claro, shallow enough that grown people crossed it with their trousers rolled to the knee. The water moved over the rounded piedra viva with its usual low note, a sound the village heard without listening. Above it, Señor Vidal crouched beside the narrow plank rail that kept carts from slipping at the edge of the ford.

He did not look up when Mira arrived.

—You should not stand there, he said.

Mira looked at the stones. Then at the ground beneath her sandals.

—There is nothing here.

—That is what makes standing there dangerous.

He pulled a small iron square from his pocket and pressed it against the rail. The wood had split near one nail. Mira had walked across that rail with Tomás only two days before, Tomás running ahead with both arms out because he believed the river needed protecting from him.

—Is it going to break? she asked.

—Everything breaks.

—That is not an answer.

Now Señor Vidal looked at her. His face was not angry. It was worse than angry. It had the patient look adults wore when they had decided a child was asking for more than a child could carry.

—It is the answer I have.

The oscuro around him was dark at the edges, as it always was, but not empty. Mira had once seen a deep pool under the roots of a sombraluz after rain. It had held the sky without becoming the sky. Señor Vidal's light was like that. It made her want to keep looking, which was how she knew to look away.

He tapped the pale stone with one finger.

—This was on the far bank this morning.

He tapped the black-seamed one.

—This was in the middle yesterday.

Then the bread-sized stone.

—And this one was under the rail at dawn.

—The river moved them.

—Yes.

—So the rail will break.

—Perhaps.

Mira felt heat climb into her face. Perhaps. Adults were always allowed perhaps. Children got told not to make things up.

—You asked me to come.

That made him still.

—Your mother said you were by the bakery.

—You sent Marisol.

Marisol lowered her paw and sneezed. Señor Vidal looked at the dog with the expression of a man betrayed by a witness who could not be questioned.

—She was already walking this way.

—You knew I would follow her.

—Yes.

The word landed between them without decoration.

Mira moved closer to the river. The current went over the stones in small clear folds. On the far side, the bank was damp farther up than it should have been. She could see that with her eyes. Below it, where the water turned beneath the plank rail, something quieter gathered: not a voice, not a hand reaching, only the feeling of a sentence that had stopped before its last word.

She pressed her toes into the dust.

—It does not want anyone to cross today.

Señor Vidal's mouth tightened.

—The river did not tell you that.

—No.

—Then do not say that it did.

Mira turned toward him so quickly that Marisol stepped back.

—I did not say it told me.

—You said it did not want.

—It is the same thing.

—It is not.

For a moment the only sound was the ford. The low note rose through Mira's soles and into her knees. She hated that it still sounded ordinary.

Señor Vidal sat back on his heels. His hands were pale with sawdust in the creases. Mira remembered that he had built the shelf above Remedios's oven after the old one bowed under flour jars. He had measured the wall three times, then asked Remedios where she wanted the jars, not where he thought they should go.

—Listen to me, Mira, he said. —I believe you see things I do not see. I do not have a ruler for them. That does not make them nothing.

The heat in her face became something else. She did not know what to do with it.

—Then close the ford.

—If I close it because you said the river did not want crossing, the next time someone is frightened they will bring you to the plaza and ask you what the stones mean. They will make your eyes into a bell they can ring whenever they are afraid.

Mira looked down at the water.

—But someone could fall.

—Someone could. That is why I am here.

He stood and took the hammer from his belt. He did not touch the rail. Instead, he walked to the ford's entrance and dragged two loose cart boards across it, slow enough that the sound carried up the path. It was not much of a barrier. A person could step around it. But a child running at full speed would see it first.

—What will you tell them? Mira asked.

He set the third stone on top of the boards.

—That the bank is soft, the rail is split, and I am repairing it before evening.

—And if they ask why now?

—Because I found a crack.

Mira waited for him to say more. He did not.

A woman with a basket came along the path from the orchard. She saw the boards and lifted her chin.

—Vidal, are you making work for yourself again?

—Usually, he said.

She laughed once, not kindly and not unkindly, then turned toward the longer path by the fields.

Mira watched her go. She had wanted Señor Vidal to explain the river so everyone would know Mira had been right. Instead he had told the smallest truth that could keep someone from falling.

—That is not fair, she said.

—No.

He picked up the pale stone and held it out to her.

—But it is yours.

Mira took it. It was warm from his hand, and ordinary as a stone could be.

—What am I supposed to do with it?

—Keep it until you know.

—Know what?

Señor Vidal lifted the hammer. The first blow rang hard against the rail and disappeared into the river's old murmur.

—Whether it is a measure, or only a stone.

Marisol came close enough that Mira could rest her fingers between the dog's ears. Together they stood at the edge of the closed ford while Señor Vidal worked. The water kept moving. It did not explain itself.
