# Chapter Four — El Hilo

*The Thread*

---

On the third dawn in the wilds Selene woke before the others and saw, for the first time, the light on a world that had no people in it at all, and understood that she had been wrong her whole life about what her gift was for.

She had thought it was for reading people. That was what it had cost her — the doctor, the careful faces, the eleven silent years — and so that was what she had assumed it was: a thing that happened between her and other human beings, a private mistranslation. But there were no human beings here. There was a hollow of pale grass under a sky going from grey to the colour of the inside of a shell, and a stand of trees she had no name for whose leaves turned their pale undersides to the coming light, and a stream working its way over stones; and all of it was *lit.* The grass had a low even green at its roots, the green of Sight, faint but everywhere, the way a held note fills a room. The trees breathed a slow amber-rose, Foundation, the patient light of things that have decided to stay where they are. The stream ran silver-blue, Flow, brightening at the little falls. Even the stones had something — the barest banked warmth, the destello of things that have been sat near and loved by the living for a very long time.

The whole world had a destello. The whole world had always had one. She had been so busy bracing against the lights of people that she had never once, in nineteen years, simply looked at the light of *everything else* — and the light of everything else asked nothing of her, accused her of nothing, wanted nothing from her but to be seen.

She sat in the wet grass with her arms around her knees and let the dawn come up through the living land, and for a while she did not brace against anything at all.

— You're awake — said Iolen, behind her, not loudly.

— I didn't want to miss it — Selene said.

The old man lowered himself down onto a stone near her with the deliberation of a body that negotiates with every movement. His banked light, out here away from the Academy, was a little less shuttered than it had been; she could see more of the uncountable chord of him, and it was the most restful thing she had ever looked at. — Most never see it — he said. — The light on the unpeopled world. You have to stop needing the light to be *about* you before it will show you that. It took me thirty years. — He looked at her sidelong. — It took you three days and a Foundation Trial. I keep having to revise downward my sense of how long things will take you, and it is making me feel my age.

---

They were five on the road, and the fifth was a thread of warmth Selene could not stop being grateful for.

Tobias had met them at the last waystation before the wilds — Pyros House patrolled this border, and a Master walking a prismatic *retornante* toward the place where something old was stirring did not go without an escort who could, if it came to it, breathe fire. Brasa rode in her sling, bigger already than she had been a week ago, awake more often, and when she was awake she watched Selene with the fire-on-iron eyes of a creature deciding something slowly and in her own time. Yael walked when she could and rode the spare *quílmera* when the path allowed, and talked, and her three-coloured light had steadied since Crystalpeak into something Selene had started to lean toward the way you lean toward a window.

And Niebla went at Selene's heel, and went still whenever the land's destello surged, which Selene was learning to read as a kind of instrument: when the silver cat stopped, something was about to be worth seeing.

The wilds were worth seeing.

On the second evening a herd of *venados de luna* had crossed the ridge above them at dusk — moon-stags, Yael breathed, *venados de luna,* the antlers branched like winter trees and the antlers *shedding light,* a cool pale silver that fell on the grass and did not quite reach the ground, so that the whole herd moved in a low private dawn of its own making. Selene had watched until the last of them went over the ridge. Their destello was a green so clear it was almost no colour, the green of Sight, and they had turned, all of them, at the top of the ridge, and looked down — not at Tobias, not at the *quílmeras* — at her, the way the woman with the basket had looked up the hill on her first morning, the way things in this world kept looking at her, and then they were gone.

— They don't do that — Tobias had said, low, the way he had said it at the crossroads. He had not elaborated. He did not need to. The land was noticing her. Everyone had stopped pretending it wasn't.

There were mist-weavers in the mornings — *tejedoras de niebla,* small as a thumbnail, spinning webs out of the night's condensed Anima, so that at the Heart-dawn, the rose hour just before the sun, every web in the hollow caught the resonance and glowed a soft heart-rose, a thousand small wheels of light strung between the grass-stems, and walked through, broke, and were rebuilt by evening. There were dusk-geese whose crying, when a skein of them crossed overhead, moved through Selene's chest and shifted something in it she did not have a word for, a loosening, a leaning-toward — *that's Heart,* Yael said, *that's 417, they carry it in their voices, nobody knows why* — and after the geese passed Selene found her eyes wet again and did not know, this time, what she was grieving.

It was the most beautiful place she had ever been. It frightened her, how beautiful it was. She had learned, with the colours, not to reach for things — and the wilds were nothing but things she wanted to reach for.

---

Iolen began to teach her on the fourth night, by the fire, while Yael slept and Tobias kept the first watch with Brasa awake on his shoulder.

— You see the destello — he said. — That is the whole of what you have done so far. Seeing. It is rarer than you can imagine and it has cost you more than you should have paid, and it is also only the doorway. — He fed a stick into the fire. The flames found it. — A *retornante* who only sees is a reader of a book she may never write in. The Academy exists to teach you to write in it. To *act* in the light, not only watch it. That begins, for everyone, at the Heart. Foundation gives you the right to exist as you are. Heart gives you the bond. Everything after is built on those two.

— Act how — Selene said.

— Look at Tobias — Iolen said. — Not at him. At the air between him and the small dragon.

Selene looked. And there it was — she had seen it for days and not named it, the way she had not named the rope over the domino table until Renata gave her the word — a fine line of light running between Tobias's chest and Brasa's sleeping body, heart-rose, taut, alive, thinning where they drew apart and brightening when the hatchling stirred toward him.

— A *hilo* — Selene said. — Renata told me. A thread.

— A thread — Iolen agreed. — Love leaves it. The Heart of one reaches and takes hold of the Heart of another, and what is left is real and lasting and *findable.* Most who see destellos never see a hilo — it is a Heart-sight, it is deep. You have been seeing them since before you had the word. — He looked at her steadily across the fire. — That is the second time the world has shown me you skipping a flight of stairs I climbed one step at a time. I have stopped being surprised. I have started being careful.

— Why careful — Selene said.

The old man was quiet for a moment. The fire worked. Out in the dark a *venado* called, far off, and another answered.

— Because to act at the Heart, child, you must use a hilo — he said. — And to use a hilo you must be willing to feel it. All of it. Including the ones that hurt. And I have been walking beside you for six days, and I have watched you read the thread between a man and his dragon, and the threads between four strangers over a game, and the thread that still runs from your friend Yael to a grandmother three years in the ground — yes, it is still there, death does not cut a hilo, it only turns it toward the violet, you will see that for yourself before this is over — — he let that sit, gently — — you have read every thread in the wilds. And I have not once seen you look down at your own.

Selene went very still.

— Everyone has them — Iolen said. — Running out of the chest, toward whoever they love. Yours are there. I cannot see destellos the way you do, I am Foundation, I see the ground under a thing — but I can see the ground under *you,* and the ground under you is a girl standing very carefully on top of a thread she has decided not to look at, because the last time she looked at it, it went grey.

The fire was loud in the silence. Selene's hand had gone to her satchel without her deciding it, to the folded photograph, the way it had in the café in Avilara.

— My father's light was the only one I could never read — she said. Her voice came out low. — Everyone else, I knew. I always knew. I knew the woman next door was sick before she did. I knew my grandfather was dying before the machines. But my father — his light was always *moving,* always just out of — I could never hold it. And then on the last night it went grey-green and I thought — — she stopped. — In the chamber I learned what the grey-green was. That he was dying here, that he left to live. I learned he didn't leave because of me. But knowing it and — *feeling* it — —

— Are two different Gates — Iolen said. — Foundation is knowing. Heart is feeling. You have passed the first. The second is still in front of you, and it is the harder one, and it cannot be passed in a chamber under a safe mountain, which is why we are out here.

Selene looked at the photograph in her hands, her father at the Plaza Francia, laughing at something outside the frame, the one light she had never been able to hold.

— Is the thread still there — she asked. — To him. Even now. Even across — whatever's between here and where he is.

— A hilo does not break with distance — Iolen said, and it was the third time she had heard the sentence, from Renata and from him and now from her own memory of Renata, and she understood it was not a saying. It was a law. — It stretches. It grows faint and costly to follow. But it holds. Yours has been running out of your chest toward your father for seven years, child, the whole time you were refusing to look at it. You did not cut it. You could not have. Only the one who loves can cut a hilo, and only by stopping — and you never stopped. You only looked away. — He rose, slowly, the negotiation with his knees. — The Heart Trial will ask you to look back. Not yet. When you are ready. But you should know what it is going to ask, so that you are not ambushed by it the way Foundation ambushed you.

He left her at the fire with the photograph and the sleeping wilds and the silver cat warm against her ankle.

Selene did not try to find the thread that night. She was not ready, and she knew it, and being able to know it — being able to feel the edge of a thing without flinging herself over it — was new, and was, she suspected, its own kind of Foundation.

But she took the *piedra de los velos* out of its box and held it against her chest, where her father's photograph also lay, and she felt the stone go warm, warmer than her body, the way it always did, as though it were not taking heat from her but giving it back from somewhere far away.

---

In the grey before the fifth dawn, Selene made a decision, and it was the first large decision of her life that she made by reaching toward something instead of away.

She found Iolen already awake, sitting where she had sat on the third morning, watching the unpeopled light come up.

— I need to go to Avilara — she said. — Before the Trial. To the *casa de paso.* — She had thought it through in the dark and it had come out whole. — My grandmother sent me here with nothing but a stone and a name. She doesn't know if I crossed safely. She doesn't know any of this happened. She's eighty-one and she's been waiting sixty years and she let me go because she'd rather lose me to a living world than keep me in a kitchen, and I will not walk into a Trial that might not let me come back without sending her *one word* across the only place a word can cross. — Her voice did not shake. — Doña Renata holds the corridor. If a message can go to my grandmother, it goes from there.

Iolen looked at her for a long moment, and something in his weathered face eased.

— Good — he said. — That is a Heart reason, and you arrived at it on your own, and you put your grandmother before your own Trial. — He got to his feet. — There is a thing waiting for you in Avilara that you do not know about, and I had wondered how to put you on the road to it without taking the choosing out of your hands. You have saved me the trouble. We go by the highland road. It bends through the *pueblos del cerro* and adds two days, and it will be two days well spent, because — — he paused, and looked west, toward the blue line where the wilds deepened toward the old border, and his banked light did a slow uneasy thing — — because what we are walking toward will keep. It has kept eight hundred years. It is not Laeylinn's way to hurry, and it is Laeylinn we are walking toward, whether or not the Academy likes me saying the name out loud.

— Laeylinn — Selene said. The word landed in her like a stone into the warm well of her chest, heart-rose, a name she did not know she had been waiting for. — Maylinn's Godbeast. The growth-deer.

— The Heart of the world, child, with antlers — Iolen said. — She has not let herself be seen since a Luminor named Sereth walked into the Shadowfen eight hundred years ago and did not walk out, and Laeylinn lay down in the wilds and would not rise. And two nights after a girl crossed a closed corridor speaking the old tongue by sundown, the *venados* began to move toward the old border for the first time in living memory, and the *tejedoras* began to spin again on the eastern slopes where they had not spun in a century, and an old Foundation Master who should know better felt the Heart of the wilds turn over in its long sleep and lift its head. — He looked at her. — Something is waking up because you are here. The Academy thinks I am taking you to a Trial. I am taking you to a *meeting.* But first — — and here, astonishingly, the old man smiled, the limestone cracking all the way into warmth — first, you are going to send your grandmother her word. Come. The highland road is beautiful at this hour, and you have not yet seen what your country looks like from the inside.

And they woke the others, and turned the *quílmeras* toward the high blue country of Avilara, toward a *casa de paso* on the fifth tier of Solavila, toward seven sealed letters in her father's hand that had been waiting for her for seven years — and Selene rode toward the place she came from with the *piedra de los velos* warm against the photograph over her heart, and for the first time in her life the warmth did not frighten her.
