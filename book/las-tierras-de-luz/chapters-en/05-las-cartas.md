# Chapter Five — Las Cartas

*The Letters*

---

Doña Renata was waiting at the gate of the fifth tier when they came up out of the highland road, and she had been crying before they arrived, which meant she had known they were coming, which meant the Library had told her, which meant — Selene understood, watching the old woman's banked gold flare and steady — that the thing Iolen had said was waiting in Avilara was waiting *with Renata,* and that Renata had been carrying it a long time.

— Mija — Renata said, and took Selene's face in both hands the way Camila used to, the way a grandmother does, and looked at her, and at her light, and made the small involuntary sound of a person confirming a hope she had not let herself fully hold. — You crossed. You passed the chamber. The whole Library is sick with you. — She laughed, wet. — And you came *back.* Most of them never look back at us once they go up the mountain. You came back on your way to the wilds to — what. To send word to Mercedes.

— To send word to my grandmother — Selene said. — If a word can cross.

Renata's face did something complicated.

— Come inside — she said. — There is more to say than fits in a gateway. And there is something I have been keeping for you for longer than you have been able to read.

---

The *casa de paso* was a house of warm coral stone built around a courtyard, and Renata sat them in it — Iolen and Yael and Tobias given food and the courteous distance of people who understood that what was about to happen was not theirs — and took Selene alone into a small inner room where a single window held the whole tiered sweep of Solavila falling down to the Mar de Aurora, gone gold now in the late light.

On the table were two things.

The first was a single letter, the paper old, the ink faded, the fold worn soft as cloth. — This came forty years ago — Renata said. — Through the corridor itself, in a season when the corridor was shut. I have never been able to explain how. It was simply on the threshold one morning, addressed to me in your grandmother's hand, and inside it Mercedes told me she was alive, and married, and would not be coming back, and that one day — *one day, Renata, hold the watch* — she would send someone who could see. — Renata's voice was steady in the way Selene was learning meant the opposite. — I held the watch. Forty years on top of the twenty before it. And here you are.

Selene could not speak.

— But that is not the thing — Renata said. — That letter is mine, and I will keep it. The thing is the other.

The other was a bundle. Seven envelopes, tied with a plain cord, the paper newer than Mercedes's letter but not new — years had handled it. And on the front of each, in a hand Selene did not know with her eyes and knew instantly, completely, with the muscle of her chest, was written one word:

*Selene.*

She sat down without deciding to.

— Three years ago — Renata said, very gently, sitting across from her — a man came to this *casa.* Not through the Solavila gate. From the south. From Sol Mareo. He had crossed the small sub-corridor under the Ávila, the evacuation way, the one that stays open when the great corridor is shut — and he had crossed it *outward,* from Earth, which is a thing almost no one survives, and he arrived here grey to the lips and shaking and he asked me, in an accent that was not Avilaran, softer, longer in the vowels, whether the watcher of the Caracas corridor would carry letters to a girl, if the girl ever came. — Renata put her hand on the bundle. — I said I would. He has sent one a year since. There are three. The other four he gave me that first day, already written, in case he — — She stopped. — In case he could not send more. He is a careful man. He plans for the silence.

— My father — Selene said. It came out of her in a whisper.

— Dario Solamar — Renata said, and Selene heard her own discarded second name said aloud, in this world, in the voice of a stranger who had known her father's face, and the room tilted. — A Luminor of Sol Mareo, mija. Did you not know? — She read Selene's face. — No. You did not know. Of course you did not. He could not tell you. That was the whole shape of his life — to live beside the three of you and never tell you what he was, because what he was, on Earth, beside a corridor that was beginning to wake, was a *signal.* A Luminor's presence on Earth is a fire on a dark hill. It would have been seen. Not by anything good. He left to put the fire out before it drew the eyes it would have drawn. He left so that the corridor would settle and you and your mother and Mercedes would be small and dark and safe. — Her own banked gold was wet at the edges. — The grey-green you saw on him on the last night, child — he wrote me what you'd seen, he had seen you see it — that was his Anima failing in your air. He had stayed nineteen years past his assignment. He stayed for love and the staying was killing him and he left to keep it from killing him in front of you. — She pushed the bundle gently across the table. — These are his. I have carried them three years. They were never mine to open. Read them in whatever order your heart wants. I will be in the courtyard. Take all the time the world has.

She rose, and pressed two fingers to her own sternum, *here, over the heart,* and went out, and closed the door, and left Selene alone with seven letters in her father's hand.

---

She read them out of order, the way you eat the foods you love first when no one is watching.

She read the one that said *I have seen your light since the night you were born, Selene. You came out of your mother already lit, all ten colours at once, white before the prism, and I held you and I wept and the midwife thought it was an ordinary father weeping and it was not. I knew what you were. I knew what it would cost you in that world. And I knew I could never tell you, because to tell you was to tell you everything, and to tell you everything was to put the fire on the hill. So I hummed you songs instead. You thought they were children's songs from my hometown. They were the lullabies of Sol Mareo, mija. I was teaching you the Heart frequency every night of your infancy, the only way I was allowed to teach you anything. If you ever learn to follow a thread — and you will, you of all souls will — you will need a key of sound. Use the songs. I made sure you would have them.*

She read the one that said *Forgive me the phone calls. Each one was harder to make than the last. It was not that I stopped loving you. It was that the distance kept growing — not the distance you could measure, the other one — and a voice on a wire carries so little of a person, and I could feel myself becoming, to you, a man who was less and less there, and I could not explain that I was less and less able, not less and less willing. I let you believe the smaller, crueler thing because the true thing was a door I was forbidden to open. I have hated that forgiveness more than anything I have ever done.*

She read the one that was only four lines: *You will be told you are a cause. You are not a cause. You are a condition. The world does not wake because you make it. The world wakes because you are in it, the way a room is not warm because the fire commands it but because the fire is there. Be in it, mija. That is the whole of what you are for.*

And she read the last one, the seventh, the one written first, three years ago, on the day he had arrived grey to the lips:

*If you are reading this, you crossed, and you can see, and Renata kept her word, and I am — I do not know what I am, when you read this. I may be here in Sol Mareo waiting for the day they let me stand in front of you with a body. I may be silent for reasons neither of us chose. But I want you to know one thing more than I want you to know anything else, and I am writing it first so that if there is only one letter you ever get, it is this one:*

*I never left you. I changed the distance. The thread did not break. It cannot break — I am a Luminor of the Heart Gate among six others, mija, I would know, I have checked it every day for seven years the way a man checks a window in a storm, and it holds, it holds, it has always held. You have been at the other end of it your whole life thinking you were alone on it. You were never alone on it. I have been here. Reach, when you are ready, and you will find me reaching back. I have not stopped. I will not stop. — Tu papá.*

Selene sat with the seven letters around her and the gold light of Solavila pouring through the window and she wept the way she had wept in the chamber, fully, without managing it — but it was a different weeping, because in the chamber she had wept for what she had lost, and now she was weeping for what had been there the entire time, underneath, the way Avilara had been underneath her grandmother's bread, the way the song had been underneath the noise, the way the thread had been running out of her chest for seven years while she stood very carefully on top of it and refused to look down.

Then she stopped weeping, and she wiped her face, and she did the thing she had crossed two worlds to be able to do.

---

She did not plan it. It was the most unplanned thing she had ever done, and she understood, afterward, that that was the point — that you cannot follow a thread while bracing against it, that the whole work is the un-bracing.

She took the *piedra de los velos* out of its sandalwood box and held it against her chest, over her father's seventh letter, over the folded photograph, over the place the thread came out of her. *A key of matter.* She closed her eyes.

And she hummed.

She did not decide which song. Her body decided. It came up out of the unteachable place behind language, a small slow melody in a key she had no name for, a tune she had thought her whole life was a children's song from her father's hometown and now knew was a lullaby of Sol Mareo, sung over her crib by a Luminor of the Heart who was teaching his infant daughter the only magic he was allowed to teach her, every night, disguised as love — which it also was. *A key of sound.*

And she stopped defending against the loss.

That was the hard one, the one Iolen had warned her was the hard one. She let herself feel the whole grey-green weight of seven years, the phone calls, the *te llamo el sábado,* the gap on the wall where a photograph had hung — and she did not flinch from it, did not file it down, did not become the careful person who walked. She let it be exactly as heavy as it was. *A key of state.*

The piedra went warm against her chest. Warmer than her body. Warmer than it had ever been — not taking heat from her but giving it back, from somewhere very far away, the way it had given back the prism of light on her grandmother's wall, the way it had given back the warmth of a hand she could not see.

And the thread lit.

She saw it with her eyes closed, which should not have been possible and was: a fine line of light running out of the centre of her chest, heart-rose deepening toward gold, out through the wall of the *casa de paso,* out over the tiers of Solavila, out across the Mar de Aurora and the southern coast, thinning with distance but not breaking, *not breaking,* running on and on toward a coral-pink room she had seen once for half a second in a chamber under a mountain — and at the far end of it, where it brightened again, someone took hold.

The light around her changed. The gold of Solavila through the window dimmed, and another light came up in the room, amber and rose and prismatic over the top, the light of someone alive, and she felt — the way you feel a person sit down beside you in the dark — she felt him *there.*

She opened her eyes.

He was sitting across from her, at the table, where Renata had been, and he was not solid — she could see the window through him, faintly, the gold tiers falling to the sea — but he was *him,* seven years older, grey at the temple, the face from the photograph and the half-turn in the chamber, and his destello was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen because it was the one she had spent her whole life unable to read, and now it was open to her completely, every Gate of it, and every Gate of it was full of her.

*Selene,* her father said. He said it the way the mountain had said it. The way her grandmother said *mi vida.* The way a person says the name of someone they have been waiting a very long time to see.

*You found the thread,* he said. His voice came not through the air but through the warm line between them, low and soft, the Sol Marean vowels long. *Mija. You found it. I felt you reaching from a long way off — I have felt you reaching toward this your whole life and not knowing what you were reaching for — and tonight you reached on purpose.* His not-quite-solid hand lifted, and she understood she could not take it, that this was not the body, that this was the thing the phone calls had failed to be and not the thing a body is — and she understood, too, that it was enough, that it was more than she had had in seven years, that it was real. *I cannot stay,* he said. *This costs you, and you have a Trial, and the body has to wait for the day they let me come. But I am here. I have always been here. Hold the songs. Hold the thread. And Selene —*

The light was already beginning to thin. She felt the cost of it in her own chest, her own destello dimming with the spending, the way Iolen had said it would.

*— the thing in the wilds that is waking up,* her father said, fainter now, the room's gold coming back up around him. *Laeylinn. Do not be afraid of her. She is not waking because you are powerful. She is waking because you are a condition, the way I wrote you. She lay down eight hundred years ago because the Heart of the world broke and no one came who could be a thread between the broken and the rest. You are a thread, mija. You have always been a thread. Go and let her see you. That is the whole of the Trial. That is the whole of what you are for.*

*Papá —* Selene said. The first time she had said the word in seven years. *Don't —*

*I am not going,* her father said, very faint now, almost only warmth. *I am changing the distance. The thread does not break. Reach again when you are rested. I will be reaching back. I have not stopped. I will not stop.*

And the light thinned to a line, and the line ran on out of the room toward the southern coast, faint and golden and unbroken, and the gold of Solavila came back up full through the window, and Selene sat alone at the table with seven open letters and a cold stone going slowly warm again against her chest, weeping, smiling, spent, *held* — and for the first time in her life entirely unafraid of the warmth.

---

When she came out into the courtyard the light had gone to dusk and her friends rose at the look on her face.

— It's true — Selene said, to Iolen, because he would understand what she meant and the others would catch up. — The thread doesn't break with distance. — She wiped her face, and laughed, and the laugh was real. — I checked.

Iolen looked at her for a long moment, at her face and at the dimmed and slowly-refilling light around her that he could not see but could somehow, in his Foundation way, feel the ground of.

— You spent yourself — he said. — Reaching. That is the most expensive thing the Heart Gate does and you did it before anyone taught you, in an upstairs room, on your first try. — He shook his head, the old gesture, the revising-downward. — Rest tonight. Eat what Renata puts in front of you, which will be a great deal. Send your grandmother her word in the morning. — He looked west, out past the courtyard wall, toward the darkening blue line of the wilds and the thing lying in them with its head lifting after eight hundred years. — And then we go and let the Heart of the world look at you. — A pause. — I think, after tonight, you are ready to be looked at.

Behind them, in the doorway, Doña Renata pressed two fingers to her sternum and watched the daughter of Dario Solamar stand in an Avilaran courtyard with her own light slowly coming back, and did not say what she was thinking, which was that she had kept the watch sixty years for a great many reasons and had just, tonight, been paid for every single one.
