# Chapter Two — Crystalpeak Citadel

*La Ciudadela Pico de Cristal*

---

She had expected the Academy to be cold, and that was the night's last mistake, because when they passed beneath its gate the whole mountain exhaled.

They had ridden until the stars wheeled over, the *sombracalle* a silence at their backs that neither of them named again, and somewhere in the small hours the road had begun to climb in earnest toward a peak that made the Cerro Luminoso look like a thing one might climb before breakfast. It had grown all night against the sky — first a far black tooth, then a closer one, then a wall, and then, in the last hour, the thing it truly was: a city carved into the living body of a mountain, crowned by a single spire that drank the starlight and gave it back from within, so that the whole vast peak glowed, very faintly, the way the *piedra de los velos* glowed in her grandmother's palm. A mountain that remembered the light after the light had gone.

And it was made of the same stone. She knew it before she could have said how — the pale stone of the door in the rock, the pale stone of the lookout-crown above Solavila, the warm pale stone of the altar where the Ávila had spoken her name. All of it the same stone, all of it, she would learn, the same single crystal, grown over a thousand years by people who had opened a Gate she would not reach in her lifetime; and the gate they rode under now was two cathedrals tall, carved around its inner curve with a spiral she did not yet know how to read, and as Solana's hooves struck the first stone of the courtyard the mountain *breathed* — a long low settling of the air, neither sound nor wind but both, the exhalation of something enormous registering that someone new had come inside its skin.

Selene felt her chest do the thing it had done at her grandmother's table and had not done since: unclench.

— It does that — Tobias said quietly, watching her face. — It knows who comes in. Don't ask me how. Nobody knows how. It just does.

A woman was waiting for them in the courtyard, robed in indigo, silver hair cropped short, with the upright stillness of someone who has chosen to be awake at this hour rather than been forced to it. Her light was a deep cool blue, level as water in a covered well — and it rippled, once, when she looked at Selene, a single deep disturbance her composed face did not betray at all.

— Bienvenida — she said. — I am Proctor Esmé Vethal. Foundation Gate. — Her eyes moved over Selene, and then, deliberately, over Selene's light, and stayed there a half-second too long. — We felt the corridor open. We did not let ourselves believe what we felt. — She inclined her head, the smallest courtesy. — You will be tired in a way you do not yet have the sense to feel. Come. The reading first, and then a bed.

Tobias swung down and handed Solana's reins to a stablehand who had appeared from a side arch. Brasa stirred against his chest and did not wake. He turned to Selene and held out his hand again, the long handshake, the press.

— This is where I leave you — he said. — Brasa needs her own bed and so, between us, do I. — The grin flickered, but the careful thing was still behind it, the thing that had got onto his face at the crossroads and not entirely left. — You're in good hands. Vethal's the best of them. And Selene — — he hesitated, then said it — whatever they tell you up there tomorrow, you climbed a mountain and it said your name back. Hold onto that one. The rest is just school. — He bowed his head to her once, and was gone toward the stables, the small dragon breathing against his heart.

Niebla, who had ridden a mountain and crossed a courtyard without once being invited, fell in at Selene's heel as though she had always walked there. Proctor Vethal looked down at the silver cat, and then at Selene, and chose, visibly, to say nothing.

— Por aquí — she said, and turned, and Selene followed her into the waking Academy.

---

The reading room was round and high and empty, save for a ring of ten unlit lamps set into the curved wall at the height of a standing person, and a single flat stone in the centre of the floor, pale, worn smooth, the size of a millstone.

— A *piedra de cantos* — Vethal said. — A tuning-stone. You will stand on it, and you will breathe, and it will sound you the way a finger sounds the rim of a glass. Each lamp on the wall answers one of the Ten Gates — the ten registers a soul can sound. Most people, standing here, light one. The strong light two. A rare few, three. — She gestured to the stone. — There is nothing to do but breathe and be honest. The stone does the rest. It cannot be fooled and it cannot be flattered, which is more than I can say for the people who run this place.

A door opened on the far side of the room and a man came in, and Selene's chest, which had unclenched at the gate, unclenched a second degree, the way a body relaxes near a wall it knows will hold.

He was perhaps sixty, broad through the shoulders and broader through the chest, with grey-streaked hair and a face that had been weathered into patience. His eyes were the colour of wet limestone. His light — and here was the thing, the thing she could not stop seeing — his light was banked low, shuttered, held small on purpose, the way she had held hers her whole life; and beneath the part he was hiding it was not one register, or two, or three. It went up, and up, a chord with too many notes to count. She trusted him before he had said a word, because a powerful man who hides his own light is a man who knows what it costs to be seen.

— Master Iolen Stoneward — Vethal said. — Foundation Gate.

— I know who she is — Iolen said. His voice was slow, low, unhurried, a voice that had never once in its life needed to be raised. — Half the mountain knows who she is. A corridor opened that has been shut for sixty years and sent us a *retornante* who was speaking the old register by sundown. — He looked at Selene, and there was nothing unkind in it, and nothing soft either. — Before we make a legend of you, child, let us make a measurement. Stand on the stone.

She stood on the stone.

— Breathe — Iolen said. — Don't perform. The stone has heard every kind of performance there is.

Selene closed her eyes, and breathed, and thought — because she could not help it — of the altar on the Ávila, and the wind with its wide flat shape, and the voice that had come up out of the rock and the wind and the third thing under both and said her name as though her name were true. She breathed, and let herself, for the first time in a room full of strangers, stop bracing against the light. She let herself simply *see* — the cool blue of Vethal, the banked uncountable chord of the old man, the small bright silver of Niebla at the door — and not look away, and not apologise, and not pretend.

The stone grew warm under her feet.

She opened her eyes because the room had changed colour.

The first lamp had lit — Foundation, an amber-rose, the colour of the deep stone, the colour she would later learn was the floor under everything. And then the second, a cool water-blue, before she had finished noticing the first. And the third, fire-gold. And then they were not lighting one at a time anymore; they were lighting the way dawn lights a row of windows, the way a chord arrives — heart-rose, voice-silver, a green, a violet, a deep indigo, a white so clean it had no colour at all — until the ring was complete and the round room blazed with all ten at once, every Gate, the full spectrum standing lit on the walls around a nineteen-year-old girl who had been told by a kind man with a cool office that she had invented the colours for attention.

No one spoke. The lamps burned. Niebla's tail had gone very still.

Selene looked at Proctor Vethal and found that the proctor's level water-blue light was shaking — not her face, her *light,* rippling like a struck pond — and that the woman had pressed two fingers to her own sternum, the gesture, *here, over the heart,* without seeming to know she had done it.

And Master Iolen Stoneward stood with his weathered limestone face giving away absolutely nothing, and his banked light flaring at the edges in a slow helpless pulse he could not hide from the one person in the room built to see it, and he said, very quietly, more to the lit room than to her:

— Once in a hundred years. — A pause. — The last was Lirien of Aqualis, four hundred years ago, and there are nine books in our Library about what she became, and they agree on nothing except that the Academy was not ready for her. — He looked at Selene, and the limestone cracked, just slightly, into something that might have been awe and might have been fear and was almost certainly both. — Step off the stone, child. Before you bring the mountain down around us by *breathing.*

---

Afterward there were forms.

That was the detail no story about a magic mountain ever prepared a person for: that the singing crystal Academy, having lit all ten of its Gates to a girl from another world, then needed her to give her particulars to a clerk. A junior proctor sat her at a desk in an anteroom and asked, in patient Common, for her full name, her place of origin, the name of the *retornante* who had received her, her age, her line.

— Selene Solamar Velara — she said. — Velara is my mother's. Solamar is my father's. — She watched the proctor write it, the strange letters of Common forming under the nib, her father's discarded name set down in ink in a world he had never told her existed. — I stopped using the Solamar at fifteen.

She did not, at the time, understand why Master Iolen — who had followed them in, who had been standing at the window with his back to the room, who had said nothing for several minutes — went so completely still.

She saw it, because she saw everything. His banked light, which had been pulsing in its slow helpless way since the stone, stopped pulsing. It went, for one full breath, perfectly flat — the stillness of a man who has heard, in an ordinary sentence, in a clerk's anteroom, a word he has been waiting a very long time to hear and has long since stopped expecting. Then it resumed, carefully, deliberately, a door closed gently from the inside.

He did not turn around. He said, to the window, to the dark valley below, to no one:

— Solamar.

Just the one word. The proctor, writing, did not look up. Selene felt the back of her neck prickle, the way it had on the road when the *sombracalle* turned, the sense of being suddenly and specifically *known* — but the old man said nothing else, and after a moment the feeling passed, and the proctor asked her line, and she said *Mercedes Solar Velara, of Avilara,* and Iolen at the window made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost not, and still did not turn around, and Selene would not understand any of it for a long time, and would remember every second of it when she did.

---

Synthesis House took the ones who would not fit anywhere else.

That was how Vethal explained it, walking her up through the waking citadel in the grey before dawn — past stairs that adjusted the height of their own steps to the length of her stride, past doors that read the intent of a hand before it touched them, past a window where the central spire stood breathing slowly against the paling sky. Seven Houses, one for each of the elemental Gates and one, the seventh, for those whose light refused to settle into a single colour. Synthesis. Historically, Vethal said, the smallest House, the strangest, and the one that had produced the most of those who went on to become Luminors — because the world's hinges, she said, are rarely made by people who fit through a single door.

— You cannot be Sorted to one Gate — Vethal said. — You are all of them. So you go to the House of all of them, which is to say the House of none, which is to say you go where the misfits go, and you will find, I think, that you have never in your life been in better company.

The dormitory was small: two beds, a window that opened directly onto the breathing spire, a cold hearth, and a great deal of evidence of someone already living there — books in stacks, books open, books face-down to hold a place. On the far bed, cross-legged, with three volumes open around her in three different scripts and a fourth held against her knees, sat a young woman who looked up when the door read them in, and went still.

She was Selene's age or near it, small and quick, brown-skinned, with a great deal of dark hair coming loose from a braid that had clearly been hasty hours ago. And her light — Selene's breath caught — her light was not one colour either. It was three: a violet and a gold and a deep rose, braided slowly around one another like three currents that had agreed, a long time ago, to share a single riverbed.

The young woman looked at Selene. Then at Selene's light. Her three colours flushed bright all at once.

— Oh — she said softly. — *You're the one.* — She unfolded herself off the bed in a single motion, books sliding. — You're the corridor. The closed one. Renata's *retornante.* The Library's been insufferable about you for two days — a sealed Earth-corridor, sixty years, prismatic resonance, and half of them refusing to believe it and the other half already writing papers. — She stopped in front of Selene, near enough that the three-coloured light and the all-coloured light hung in the small room together, and she looked, openly, hungrily, the way Selene had spent nineteen years dreading and now found she did not mind at all. — Yael — she said. — Yael Mehta-Suleiman. Synthesis, second year, and apparently your roommate, which the universe arranged without consulting either of us. — The grin arrived, quick and real and conspiratorial. — I have been waiting my whole life to meet someone whose light does what mine does. Yours does so much more of it that I should probably be jealous and I have decided, in the last four seconds, not to be. Sit. You look like you crossed a world today.

— I did — Selene said.

— I know — Yael said. — That's why I said it. Sit.

---

They talked until the window went from black to grey to the first pale gold, the way Selene had not talked to anyone since she was a child, before she learned that talking led to doctors.

Yael's light was three colours because Yael was three inheritances, and she laid them out on the bed the way another person might lay out photographs of family. Her mother's people were Sephardic, keepers of the Kabbalah, of the Tree and its ten luminous *sefirot* — *ten,* Yael said, watching Selene's face, *the same ten, the Gates with older names.* Her father's people were Tamil, keepers of the Vedas, of the wheel of lights that climbs the spine. Her grandmother — *Allah yerhamha,* Yael said, and touched her own chest, and Selene's breath snagged on the gesture — her grandmother had been Sufi-trained, *dhikr* every night to the last one, the most ferocious gentleness Yael had ever known.

— Three traditions — Yael said. — Three true things. Three different hillsides in the dark, all looking at the same ten Gates and writing down what they could see from where they stood. — She showed Selene a small diagram inked in the back of one book, ten circles joined by paths; a phrase in a rounded southern script in another; three words of Arabic she breathed rather than spoke. — Up here they call them Echoes. Outer-World Echoes. Because the Academy teaches the thing *directly,* the source, and what my grandmothers found, with no school and no teacher and nothing but their own attention in the dark — that's the echo of it. And they're not wrong to call it that. But people *say* it like *echo* means *less.* Like my grandmother heard a worse version. — Her jaw set. — She heard the music with no instrument. That's harder, not easier.

And Selene, looking at the diagram of ten joined circles, said, slowly: — My grandmother burns a resin every night. In a little dish by the window. *Palo santo.* And she touches her chest before she reaches for you. And she made a bread, after I told her I could see, that tastes like — — She stopped, because her eyes had filled again. — I ate that bread today. On the third tier. I didn't know it had a name.

Yael was very quiet.

— It all comes from the same place — she said at last, softly. — That's the thing nobody up here will say plainly, because it's too big and it frightens them. Your grandmother's bread and my grandmother's *dhikr* and the ten lamps you lit downstairs by *breathing.* The same place. We've just been calling it different names from different hills. — She looked at Selene with her three-coloured light burning steady in the dawn. — I think you might be the one who gets to stop calling it names and just — see it. All of it. At once. — A breath. — That terrifies me a little, if I'm honest. In a good way. Mostly in a good way.

The bell rang.

It was not a gentle bell. It was a single deep stroke that came up through the floor and the bedframe and Selene's breastbone, and Yael's three colours went sharp and small in an instant, all the warmth dropping out of her face.

— That's wrong — Yael said. She was already on her feet. — That's the Foundation Trial bell. That's not — Selene, that bell doesn't ring for three more weeks. The cohort isn't *ready,* nobody's prepared, that bell does not ring early, it has *never* rung early —

A knock at the door. The door read the knock and did not open; the voice came through the stone, level and clear, Proctor Vethal's voice, with the not-quite-steadiness of a woman delivering an order she does not fully understand.

— Selene Velara of Avilara. — A pause. — The Foundation Trial Chamber summons you at dawn. The cohort is assembling. Master Iolen has asked that you come now.

Selene stood. Outside the window the central spire of Crystalpeak breathed against a sky going gold, and somewhere far beneath her feet, in the deep body of the mountain, something nine thousand years old had spoken her name into a bell, and was waiting.

— Well — Yael said, into the silence, with a bravado that did not reach her eyes. — *Welcome to the Academy.*
