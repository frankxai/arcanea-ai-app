# Prologue — La Subida del Cerro

*The Climb Up the Hill*

---

On the morning of her nineteenth birthday Selene climbed the Ávila alone in the dark, because her grandmother had told her — fifteen years before, with cornmeal on her hands — that if she went up to a certain flat stone and said her own name three times into the wind, something would answer.

She had carried the instruction the way you carry a stone in a pocket: forgetting it for months, then closing your hand around it and finding it still there. She had never gone. There was always a reason. And then she had turned nineteen at midnight, alone, in a city that did not know it, and she had lain awake until four and understood that the reason she had never gone was that she was afraid the mountain would stay silent — and that a silent mountain would be one more thing, the last thing, agreeing with the doctor.

So she dressed in the dark to settle it.

She told no one. Her mother was on the night shift at the Vargas, two hours still from the bus she would sleep on the whole way home, waking with the seat printed into her cheek. Her grandmother was behind the closed door at the end of the hall — closed, which meant sleeping, because Mercedes left it open when she was awake. Selene laced her boots by feel. She left a note on the counter under the chipped Quintana Roo mug her grandmother had drunk her *café con leche* from for forty years: *Fui a caminar. Vuelvo a las nueve. Te quiero, mamá.* To her grandmother she wrote nothing. Her grandmother would know.

The elevator was broken again. She took the stairs, and the street, and the long empty avenue that climbed east toward the trailhead, and Altamira at a quarter to five was the only honest version of the city — no traffic to lie over the quiet, the *panaderías* still dark, the great mass of the Ávila already blacker than the sky behind it, which meant the sky had begun, somewhere, to prepare for light.

A man passed her near the trailhead with a thermos and a dog. The dog had a soft contented orange around it, the orange of a creature that has never once doubted it is loved. The man had a thin worried blue. Selene looked at their hands, the way she had taught herself, and not at the colours, and said *buenos días*, and climbed.

---

She had seen the light around the living for as long as she had been alive, and had been told, for almost as long, that she had not.

The first one she could remember belonged to a woman with a bag of plantains in the Plaza Francia, when Selene was eight and her grandmother had taken her out at dusk and taught her to watch people without letting them feel watched. *Mira despacio*, her grandmother had said. *Look slowly.* And Selene had looked slowly, and there it was, the way it was around everyone — a soft amber-rose breathing at the woman's shoulders, brightening when the woman smiled at a child. *Abuela, los veo*, Selene had whispered, terrified and certain at once, and her grandmother had squeezed her hand, hard, one time, and said *bien, mi amor*, and changed the subject forever.

The next year she made the mistake of telling her mother.

Her mother had sat her on the edge of the bed and held Selene's face in both warm tired hands and explained, gently, the way you explain a thing you need to be true, that Selene was a girl with a great deal of imagination, and that imagination was a gift, but that not everyone would understand the things she thought she saw, and did Selene understand? Selene had said yes. There had been a doctor, after that, kind, with a cool office, who used the word *synesthesia* and then the word *imaginative* and then, to her mother, when he believed Selene had stopped listening, the word *attention* — the kind a child invents things to get.

She had been nine. She believed him for eleven years. She did not stop seeing the light; she stopped saying she saw it. By twelve she could pass a whole day looking only at people's hands.

When her father left she was twelve, and the light around him on the last night was a sour grey-green she had no word for and would not have used if she had. He kissed the top of her head and said *te llamo el sábado*, and the grey-green moved over his face like weather coming in, and she had known, with the certainty the light always gave her and never explained, that he was not going to call. He called, for a while. Then less. Six months ago he had not been listening to her on the phone and she had stopped saying *papá* and he had stopped saying *mi hija* and she had not cried, because she had finished, years before, being a person who cried about that. She had become instead a person who walked.

Her name was Selene Solamar Velara. Solamar was her father's; she had stopped writing it at fifteen. She carried it in her body the way you carry a name you have not yet decided whether to forgive.

She walked now, up the mountain, in the blueing dark, and the higher she climbed the quieter the city's pressure in her chest became — that low constant hum of ten thousand near strangers and their ten thousand lights, which she had felt her whole life as a kind of noise and had never once been able to switch off. It thinned as she rose. By the high meadow it was almost gone, and in its place was something she had no practice at hearing: a single low note that seemed to come up through the soles of her boots, out of the mountain itself.

She did not name it. She had stopped naming things at nine. But her chest knew it, the way it knew the colours, in the unteachable place behind language.

---

The dawn came up grey, then pearl, then with the first long fingers of pink testing the eastern ridge, and at the summit she was alone, and the wind had a different shape — wide and flat and steady, as though some agreement had been reached overnight between the mountain and the sky and the wind had been sent down to deliver it.

She found the stone her grandmother had described. Not the highest, not the one the tourists climbed for the photograph: a flat-topped grey rock the size of a small altar, weathered smooth, exactly as it had been described in a kitchen fifteen years before by a woman with cornmeal on her fingers who had stopped, mid-sentence, and reached across the table to touch four-year-old Selene's chest with two fingers — not a poke, a *placing* — and said, *cuando llegue el día, tú vas a saber.* When the day comes, you will know.

The day had come. Selene did not feel that she knew anything. She felt nineteen and cold and faintly ridiculous and entirely alone on a mountain at dawn.

She put her hands flat on the stone. It was cold. She closed her eyes and said her name.

— Selene Velara.

The wind crossed the rock and her face and did not change.

She said it again.

— Selene Velara.

A bird called once, far down the ridge. The pink climbed the sky. Nothing.

She said it the third time, quietly, almost ashamed, because she was nineteen and alone and had climbed a mountain in the dark on the word of an old woman, and because some part of her had needed, her whole life, to find out.

— Selene Velara.

The mountain said her name back.

It was not the wind. It was not the rock under her hands. It was not a thought. It came from the rock and the wind and a third thing underneath both of them, and it said *Selene* — slowly, fully, the way her grandmother said *mi vida*, the way a person says the name of someone they have been waiting a long time to see — and it was the first time in her entire life that anything had said her name as though her name were true.

She did not move. She did not open her eyes. She did not breathe, for what might have been a moment and might have been much longer.

When she opened her eyes there was a cat on the stone across from her.

She had never seen one like it and she knew, in the instant, what it was, the way you know the word for a thing you have been waiting all your life to name. A *gato plateado* — silver-grey, the fur faintly luminous in the new light, eyes the colour of polished pewter. It sat with its tail curled around its paws and looked at her the way one person looks at another who has just, at long last, remembered her own name.

Selene did not reach for it. She had learned, with the colours, not to reach.

The cat held her eyes for a long moment. Then it stood, stretched once in the unhurried way of cats, and was not there — not faded, not gone behind anything, simply not there, the pink dawn falling now on the empty stone where it had been.

She sat down on her own rock. She did not understand what had happened. She understood exactly what had happened. The two understandings sat beside each other and looked at the sunrise and did not argue, and after a while she stood and went down the mountain to find her grandmother, because there was only one person on earth who could tell her what to do with a morning like this, and Selene had finally, at nineteen, run entirely out of reasons to pretend she could not.

---

Her grandmother was at the kitchen table when she let herself in, drinking from the Quintana Roo mug, the radio low on the traffic report, the note Selene had left already gone.

Mercedes looked at her over the rim of the mug, and did not ask where she had been, and did not ask why her eyes were the way they were. She set the mug down. She was eighty-one. Her hands had been kneading dough since before this country had the name it had now, and they did not shake.

— ¿Subiste? — her grandmother said. *You went up?*

Selene nodded.

— ¿Y?

Selene opened her mouth, and closed it, and found there was only one word.

— Habló — she said. *It spoke.*

Her grandmother put both hands flat on the table, and closed her eyes, and for a moment she was not an old woman in a kitchen but a girl on a different mountain a lifetime ago, hearing the same impossible thing. When she opened her eyes they were wet and entirely steady.

— Ven — she said. *Come.*

In the bedroom she took from the bedside drawer a box of sandalwood Selene had known her whole life and been forbidden, at four, ever to open. Mercedes opened it now. Inside, on a square of old cloth, lay a stone the size of a small guava — pale, translucent, and warm; Selene felt the warmth of it from across the bed, a warmth with no business in a stone in a box in a drawer. Her grandmother lifted it to the window and the morning light went into it and came out the far side as a small soft prism thrown across the wall, every colour at once.

— La piedra de los velos — Mercedes said. The stone of the veils. She said it the way she had once said *bien, mi amor* on the bench in the Plaza Francia: as though she were handing Selene, at last, a word she had been keeping for her.

And then she sat on the edge of the bed and told her.

She told her about Avilara, and the *Cerro Luminoso*, the luminous hill, and *la subida del cerro* — the climb, the oldest thing her people did, the rite in which every Avilaran goes up the mountain alone, once, and says their name, and is named in return. She told her about a corridor that had opened in the deep rock under the Ávila in 1965 and carried her — twenty-one, terrified, sure she was damned or dreaming — out of one world and into Caracas, where she had married a kind agronomist named Camilo Velara and never gone back, because three months after she crossed the corridor closed behind her like a mouth, and had not opened again. Not for sixty years.

She told it in a Spanish that was suddenly not Selene's mother's Spanish at all, but something older underneath it, a current beneath the current, surfacing for the first time in six decades.

— And last night — Mercedes said, and touched her own chest, over the heart, with two fingers, the gesture Selene had watched her make a thousand times and never understood — last night, for the first time since I was a girl, I felt the *zumbido* again. Here. It means the corridor is open. It means someone is meant to climb.

Selene closed her hand around nothing. — Abuela. The colours I see. The ones Mamá says I invent. — She had never, in nineteen years, said this sentence aloud to this woman. — You see them too. Don't you.

For a long moment her grandmother did not answer. Then: — Sí, mi vida. I see them too. I never told you, because to tell you was to tell you that one day you would have to leave. And I was not ready for the day. — She pressed the warm stone into Selene's palm and closed both her old hands over it. — There is one more thing. Your father.

The room went very still. They did not speak of her father.

— Solamar — her grandmother said. — Tu segundo apellido. The one you stopped writing at fifteen. Whatever they tell you over there — keep it. Acuérdate.

— Abuela. — The word came hard. — Is he alive?

Her grandmother looked at her with the particular love of a person who has carried a thing too many years to weigh.

— Sí, mi vida — Mercedes said. — Está vivo. They will explain it to you over there, better than I can. Tonight the corridor opens again, and I am too old now; I would die in the threshold. But you — — she folded Selene's fingers tighter around the stone — you can go.

---

That night Selene climbed the Ávila a second time, in the dark, the city below her glittering like an inverted heaven — the lights of Caracas the stars seen from the wrong side.

She had left her mother a second note. *Voy a estar fuera unos días. No te preocupes. Cuida a la abuela. Te quiero.* Her grandmother had blessed her at the door with a hand on her forehead and said *anda, mi vida,* and had not wept where Selene could see, which Selene understood was its own kind of climbing.

At the summit she went to the stone. She did not need to put her hands on it. The *piedra de los velos* was warm against her chest beneath her shirt. She closed her eyes and said her name once — *Selene Velara* — and the mountain answered, and she opened her eyes.

The stone in front of her had become a door.

Not a trick of the dawn, because there was no dawn; not a trick of the light, because the light was wrong — a tall narrow door of weathered grey rock, standing slightly open, and through the gap came a light that did not belong to her world, pink-gold and slow, the light of some other mountain's evening.

Selene Solamar Velara — nineteen years old, born in Altamira, daughter of Camila, granddaughter of Mercedes, who had crossed this same threshold from the other side sixty years before — took one breath of the woodsmoke-and-night air of the only world she had ever known.

And she stepped through.
