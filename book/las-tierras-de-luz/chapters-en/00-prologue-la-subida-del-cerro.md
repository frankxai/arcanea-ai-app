# Prologue — *La Subida del Cerro*

## Caracas, the morning of her nineteenth birthday

On the morning of her nineteenth birthday Selene did not tell anyone, and at four-thirty she rose in the dark and dressed for the climb.

The apartment in Altamira held its night-shape around her. Her mother was at the Vargas, two more hours on shift, then the bus that her mother would sleep on the entire way home and wake with her cheek printed by the seat. Her grandmother was in the room with the closed door at the end of the hall, the door her grandmother kept closed when she was sleeping and open when she was awake. The door was closed.

Selene tied her boots in the dark. She had packed the night before — a small canvas bag, water, a piece of bread, the gold cross around her neck under her shirt where it had always lived. She left the note on the kitchen counter, weighted with the chipped Quintana Roo mug that her grandmother had been drinking *café con leche* out of for forty years.

*Fui a caminar. Vuelvo a las nueve. Feliz cumpleaños a mí. Te quiero, mamá.*

She did not write to her grandmother. Her grandmother would know.

She slipped out into the corridor and pulled the door shut without sound. The elevator was broken again. She took the stairs.

Altamira at four-forty-five was the city she liked best — the streets empty enough to be honest, the *panaderías* a few minutes from their lights, the *Ávila* in the gap between buildings already darker than the sky behind it, which meant the sky was already preparing for what came next.

She walked east. The avenue rose. Cars passed and did not see her. A street dog crossed her path and did not stop. By the time she reached the trailhead at Sabas Nieves the city was still asleep but the sky above her was the deep blue that meant dawn was on its way.

She turned and looked back, once, at the apartment building. The third-floor window of the kitchen was dark, which meant either her grandmother was still in bed or her grandmother had risen and was sitting in the dark which was the same thing.

She turned and began to climb.

---

For the first half-hour the trail was only the trail. The packed-dirt path under her boots. The smell of damp leaves and dry leaves at once that the Ávila had at any season. The breathing of her own body, which was the breathing she had been waiting to hear — the breathing that came when she was alone enough that the *zumbido* of the city stopped pressing against her chest and she could hear, instead, the *zumbido* of the mountain.

She did not call it that. She had stopped calling it anything when she was nine.

When she was eight her grandmother had taken her down to the Plaza Francia at dusk and shown her, without showing her, how to watch people. *Mira, mi vida. Mira a esa señora con la bolsa de plátanos. Mira a ese señor con el periódico. Mira a esa niña que se acaba de caer. Mira despacio.* Selene had looked. She had seen, around each of them, the soft color she had been seeing since she could remember — the woman with the plantains was an old amber-rose, the man with the newspaper a cool deep blue, the child who had fallen a bright laughing coral that did not flicker even when the child began to cry.

She had said, *Abuela, los veo.*

Her grandmother had taken her hand and squeezed it once, hard, and said, *Bien, mi amor*, and had not said anything else about it. The next day her grandmother had begun to cook a different kind of bread. Round, flat, made from the cornmeal in the back of the pantry that Selene had thought was for emergencies. The bread tasted like remembering you were warm.

When Selene was nine she told her mother. Her mother sat down on the bed and took Selene's face in both hands and said, *Mi cielo, eres una niña con mucha imaginación. Eso es bueno. Pero no a todos les vas a poder explicar lo que ves. ¿Me entiendes?* Selene had said yes. Her mother had kissed her forehead. From the hallway her grandmother had watched and said nothing.

After that Selene stopped naming the colors. They did not stop being there. She stopped naming them.

When her father left for Madrid she was twelve and the colors around him on the last night had been a sour gray-green she did not have a word for and would not have used if she did. He had kissed the top of her head and said, *Te llamo el sábado*, and the gray-green had moved over his face like weather, and she had known he was not going to call. He had called for a while. Then less. The visits dwindled. Six months ago, on the phone, she had asked him a question about her thesis and he had not been listening, and she had not said *papá*, and he had not said *mi hija*, and the phone call had ended and she had not cried because she had stopped being a person who cried about that. She had become a person who walked.

Her name was Selene Solamar Velara. The Solamar was her father's name; she had stopped using it at fifteen. She wrote *Selene Velara* on her university papers. The Solamar she carried around in her body the way one carries a name one has not yet decided to forgive.

She walked now. The Ávila under her boots was the steadying body she had been climbing in her sleep for years.

---

The dawn came slowly. The trail rose. By the time she was through the high meadow the sky had gone from blue to gray to a kind of pearl, and the first long fingers of pink were testing the eastern ridge.

She stopped, once, to drink water. The view fell away beneath her — Caracas in its bowl, the highways already humming, the buildings she had walked through and slept in and grown inside, the schools she had attended and the cafés where she had broken up with the boy from her etymology seminar last March. From here the city was a small bright thing, hatched into the floor of the valley like an embroidery.

She climbed.

At the summit she was alone. The wind had a different shape here — flat, wide, steady, as if the *zumbido* of the upper sky and the *zumbido* of the mountain had agreed on something and the wind was carrying the agreement down to the rocks.

She walked to the place she had been climbing toward. A flat-topped rock the size of a small altar, weathered, gray. The Ávila was crowded with rocks and this one was not the highest, was not the most dramatic, was not the one tourists climbed for the photograph. It was the one her grandmother had described, fifteen years ago, in the kitchen, with cornmeal on her fingers.

*Cuando seas más grande*, her grandmother had said, and then had stopped. Selene, four years old, had waited. Her grandmother had said: *Cuando seas más grande, mi vida, vas a subir el Ávila tú sola. En la madrugada. Antes que salga el sol pero no mucho antes. Y vas a ir a una piedra. Una piedra plana, así, como un altar pequeño. Y vas a decir tu nombre. Tres veces. Y vas a esperar.*

*¿Esperar qué, abuela?*

Her grandmother had not answered. Her grandmother had reached across the table and touched her chest with two fingers — not a poke, a *placement* — and had said, *Tú vas a saber, mi amor. Cuando llegue el día, tú vas a saber.*

Selene was nineteen. She had remembered the instruction every year of her life.

She placed her hands flat on the stone. It was cold. She closed her eyes.

She said her name.

*Selene Velara.*

The wind moved across the rock and across her face and did not change.

She said it again.

*Selene Velara.*

The wind moved. The dawn rose. A bird she did not know the name of called, once, from somewhere lower on the ridge.

She said it the third time, softly, because she was nineteen and alone and on a mountain at dawn and her grandmother had told her to.

*Selene Velara.*

The mountain spoke her name back.

It was not metaphor. It was not the wind. It was not the rock vibrating. It was — and she knew this in the way she had always known the colors, in the unteachable place behind language — it was the *Ávila*, the mountain itself, the rock and the wind and a third thing under both, saying her name in a voice that had been waiting for her to ask. *Selene.* Slow. Patient. The way her grandmother said *mi vida*.

She did not move. She did not open her eyes. She did not breathe for what might have been a long time and might have been a moment.

When she opened her eyes the *gato plateado* was sitting on the stone opposite her.

She had never seen one. She knew what it was. The way one knows the word for a thing one has been waiting to name. Medium-sized, silver-gray, with the kind of fur that looked faintly luminous in the dawn light. Eyes the color of polished pewter. The cat sat with its tail curled around its front paws and looked at her the way a person looks at another person who has just remembered her own name.

Selene did not reach for it. She had learned, with the colors, not to reach.

The cat looked at her for what might have been a long time. Then it stood, stretched once in the deliberate way of cats, and was not there. Not faded — *not there*. The dawn light fell on the empty rock where the cat had been.

Selene sat down on her own rock. She did not know what had happened. She knew exactly what had happened. The two knowings were not at war. They were two of her, sitting beside each other on the summit, looking at the dawn.

After a while she stood and began to descend.

---

The city was awake when she reached Altamira. The metro was running. Her mother was already on the bus, her grandmother was — when Selene let herself into the apartment, drinking *café con leche* at the kitchen table with the radio low on the morning news. The chipped Quintana Roo mug was in her grandmother's hands. The note Selene had left was gone.

*Buenos días, mi vida*, her grandmother said. *Felicidades.*

*Gracias, abuela.*

Her grandmother looked at her over the rim of the mug. The kitchen window framed the Ávila, indifferent in the late-morning light. Selene poured herself coffee. She sat down opposite her grandmother. The radio was murmuring about traffic on the Cota Mil. Her grandmother set the mug down on the table.

*¿Subiste?* her grandmother said.

Selene nodded.

*¿Y?*

Selene opened her mouth and closed it. After a moment she said, *Habló.*

Her grandmother put both hands flat on the table. She was eighty-one. The hands were the hands of a woman who had been kneading dough since 1958. The hands did not shake. They had been waiting for this moment for sixty years and they did not shake.

*Bien*, her grandmother said. *Bien, mi amor. Ven conmigo.*

They went down the hall to her grandmother's room. Her grandmother closed the door. From the bedside drawer her grandmother took the sandalwood box. Selene had known the box her whole life — she had been told, when she was four, that it held a stone her grandmother's grandmother had given her, and that she was never to open it. She had never opened it.

Her grandmother opened it now.

The stone inside was the size of a small *guayaba*. Translucent. Faintly warm — Selene felt the warmth from across the bed. Her grandmother lifted it out and held it in her palm. The light from the window passed through the stone and came out the other side as a small soft prism on the wall.

*La piedra de los velos*, her grandmother said.

She said it the way her grandmother had said *Mira, los veo* — as if she were giving Selene a word she had been waiting to give her.

Then her grandmother sat down on the edge of the bed and began to tell her.

She told her about Avilara. About the *Cerro Luminoso*. About *la subida del cerro*. About the corridor that had opened under the Ávila in 1965 and had carried her, twenty-one years old, into Caracas, where she had married Camilo Velara and never gone back. About the corridor that had closed three months after her crossing and had not opened again, not for sixty years, until the night before — the night before — when her grandmother had felt, in her chest, the *zumbido* she had not felt since she was twenty.

She also told her — softly, in the Avilaran Spanish that was suddenly its own language and not Selene's mother's Spanish at all — that there were things about Selene's father that Selene did not know, and that Mercedes had not been allowed to say, and that Selene would learn in the place she was going. *Solamar*, Mercedes said. *Tu segundo apellido. Acuérdate.*

Selene closed her eyes. The room was quiet. The *piedra de los velos* was warm in her grandmother's palm.

— Abuela, ¿papá está vivo?

Her grandmother looked at her with the kind of love that had been holding a secret for too many years to count.

— Sí, mi vida — Mercedes said. — Está vivo. Te explican allá.

*Esta noche, mi vida*, her grandmother said. *Esta noche se abre otra vez. Y yo soy muy vieja. Yo me moriría en el umbral. Pero tú —* her grandmother held the *piedra de los velos* out to her on her palm — *tú puedes ir.*

Selene took the stone. It was warm against her hand. The warmth was the warmth of a body, not of a sunlit window. Her chest filled with something she would later understand was grief and recognition braided so tightly together she could not name either alone.

*Abuela —*

*Mira hacia el Ávila*, her grandmother said. *Haz lo que hiciste esta mañana. Pero esta vez, sigue.*

*¿Sigo a dónde?*

Her grandmother smiled. The smile was the smile of a woman who had been holding a secret for sixty years and was at last allowed to set it down on the kitchen table with the chipped Quintana Roo mug.

*Sigue, mi vida. Tú vas a saber.*

---

That night Selene climbed the Ávila a second time.

She did not tell her mother. She left a second note — *Voy a estar fuera unos días. No te preocupes. Mamá, te quiero. Cuida a la abuela.* — and her grandmother, in the kitchen, smiled at her from the chair and said *Anda, mi vida*, and Selene closed the door of the apartment behind her for what might have been the last time and might not.

The trail in the dark was a different trail. The city below her glittered like an inverse heaven — the lights of Caracas were the stars seen from the wrong side. The Ávila under her boots was the patient body that had been carrying her.

At the summit she went to the rock. She did not place her hands. She did not need to. The *piedra de los velos* was warm against her chest under her shirt where she had put it for safekeeping. She closed her eyes.

She said her name.

*Selene Velara.*

The mountain answered.

She did not say it a second time. She did not need to. She opened her eyes.

The rock in front of her had become a door.

It was not a metaphor. It was not a trick of the light. The rock had become a door — a tall narrow door of weathered gray stone, slightly ajar, beyond which a different light was coming through.

Selene Velara, nineteen, born in Altamira, daughter of Camila, granddaughter of Mercedes, stepped through.
