# Chapter Two

## El Pan de Don Emilio

Three days.

She had not told anyone.

In those three days Mira had walked to school, and walked home from school, and eaten the soup her mother made, and done the homework her teacher had given her, and brushed her hair the way her mother liked it brushed, and she had watched. That was what she was doing now. Watching. She had become, in three days, a quiet girl who watched, and her mother had noticed only the quiet.

— ¿Estás bien, mi vida?

— Sí, mamá.

— ¿Segura?

— Estoy pensando.

And Remedios, who had a shop to run and a small brother to feed and the long narrow thing called being a woman in charge of everything to do, had kissed the top of Mira's head and said *pues piensa, mi reina* and gone back to the ledger.

Mira had meant what she said. She was thinking.

She was thinking that in three days she had seen more than a hundred destellos. She did not know yet that the word for what she was seeing was *destello.* The word had not arrived. The things had arrived first, and the things were so many and so various that she could not hold them all in her head, and so she had done what she always did with too-many things: she had begun, quietly, to make a list.

The list lived in the blue notebook that her father had sent her for her tenth birthday, with the gold-stamped letters that spelled MI CUADERNO on the cover. She had not used it for two years because she had not known what to write in a notebook her father had chosen for her. Now she had something to write.

She wrote it at night, by candlelight, while Chispa hovered above the page and turned the ink colors Mira could not otherwise see.

*La viuda Herreros — violeta pálido, del tamaño de una almendra.*

*El perro del carnicero — dorado, como un pan pequeño.*

*La sombraluz del patio — ámbar, grande, despacio.*

*Mi madre — rosa, cuando canta. Oro cuando amasa.*

*Mi hermano — azul. Muy brillante. No tengo palabra.*

*Marisol — no puedo verla. Ella me ve a mí.*

*Señor Vidal — oscuro, como el Río Claro donde es más profundo. No tengo miedo. Tampoco sé qué es.*

She had not written her own entry. She did not know what color Chispa was. She had been asking for three days and had not arrived at an answer. Sometimes it looked green. Sometimes gold. Sometimes, when she had been very still for a long time and the candle had begun to gutter, it looked like every color at once — like the edge of a rainbow seen in a soap bubble, always shifting, never settling — and then she blinked and it was green again, and she did not know whether she had imagined the rest.

She had written in the notebook, at the top of the third page:

*Nadie más los ve.*

That was the line she kept coming back to. That was the line that turned the notebook from a list into something else. It sat on the page by itself with nothing to do. It sat there and waited.

On the fourth page she had begun writing a list of things she had noticed that were not destellos but were also new.

*La hora de los destellos — justo antes de anochecer. Las luces son más visibles. Los adultos llaman a los niños para que entren. No es casualidad.*

*Cuando canto para mí misma, los zorzales cantan antes por la mañana.*

*En la plaza, cerca del banco del olivo viejo, sentí algo. Como una brasa invisible. Me acerqué. Había algo allí, del tamaño de mi mano, girando. Un poco naranja, un poco dorado. No dijo nada. Era cálido. Nadie más lo vio, pero la señora que tejía en el banco dijo "ay, qué agradable hace hoy," y sonrió al aire. Cuando me fui, la cosa ya no estaba. O estaba todavía, solo que no pude seguirla con los ojos.*

She did not have a word for what she had seen in the plaza. She had reached toward it and her fingers had passed through a warmth the size of a small cupped palm, and the warmth had spun around her hand slowly, as if recognizing her, before drifting back to its place at the edge of the bench. She had watched it for perhaps a minute. It had not been afraid of her. It had not been anything, exactly — it was closer to an *echo* of a thing, the way the taste of a meal remains on the tongue for a moment after the meal is done. And then an old man had walked past carrying a basket of luzfruta, and Mira had stepped aside to let him pass, and when she had turned back the warmth was gone.

Later, in the notebook, by candlelight, she had written under the entry:

*Creo que vienen cuando la luz se mueve. Creo que son la luz recordándose a sí misma.*

And then, because writing it that way felt too big for an eleven-year-old, she had crossed it out. And then, because she also felt it was true, she had rewritten it underneath, unreasoned, unargued, exactly as it had arrived.

---

On the morning of the fourth day she left the house early.

Remedios was already in the shop. Tomás was still asleep. Mira put on her school uniform and her canvas shoes and her wool cardigan — the dawn was cool, though the day would be warm — and she let herself out the courtyard door and closed it softly behind her.

Chispa hovered at her shoulder, patient as always.

The pueblo at six o'clock in the morning had a particular silence. Not empty. Not asleep. The silence of a place getting itself ready. Shutters being unlatched but not yet opened. Dogs stretching in warm doorways. The first shy sound of a broom somewhere at the end of a street. The campanile bells had not yet rung. The sky above the terracotta roofs was the color of the inside of a shell, and the piedra viva of the walls seemed, in that half-light, to be almost breathing — holding the coolness of night in their dry joints, the warmth of yesterday's sun in their cores, translucent at the edges where the first light was finding them.

Mira had not planned where she was going. She told herself this as she walked. She told herself this as she turned left at the fountain instead of right toward the school. She told herself this as she crossed the little bridge over the Río Claro, where the floración azul grew along the bank — low, five-petaled, blue shifting toward violet in the shade, this morning mostly closed because the morning was only half-awake and the flowers had not yet decided to open. She told herself this as she made her way along the narrow street where the bakery stood — Panadería Almería, the sign said, in letters painted by someone's grandmother in the year the bread shop opened and never repainted since — and as she slowed, and as she stopped, and as she stood in the small pool of warm yellow light that came through the open bakery door.

She had not planned it. But there she was.

And the floración azul along the river at the bakery's side wall — the ones closest to the door, the ones nearest the kitchen window — were completely open. Fully. As they never were this early. As they should not have been at all, before the full morning light. As they always were, Mira noticed now, whenever she happened to walk past the bakery, on any day of her life.

She had never noticed that either.

Inside, Don Emilio was alone.

He was at the long wooden table with the flour on it, and the dough in front of him was the size of a small dog, and he was working it with both hands in the slow unhurried way that was the way he had been working dough for fifty-one years. He did not look up. He was humming. He always hummed. He had hummed for as long as Mira could remember, the same three notes, in the same order, with the small breath between the second note and the third that she had always thought was a mistake and had recently begun to suspect was not.

Mira stood in the doorway.

She looked at Don Emilio.

She looked at the place in his chest — beneath his flour-dusted apron, beneath the worn blue shirt, beneath the ribs that had been kneading bread since before her own father was born — and she saw it.

His destello.

It was amber, warm as late afternoon, and it was threaded through with a slow deep rose. The rose moved inside the amber like a swirl of milk in coffee that never quite mixed. It was the most patient thing she had ever seen. It was sleeping, peacefully, unbothered, complete.

For three days she had watched destellos. For three days she had thought: *I see them. That is enough. That is already so much.*

Now, standing in the bakery doorway at dawn, something in Mira bent down toward Don Emilio's amber-rose light and wanted, with a wanting she did not know she had room for, to say hello to it.

That was the word. *Hello.*

Not to wake it. She would not have used that word. She did not know yet that it was sleeping. She only knew that she wanted to speak to it, the way she had spoken to Chispa on the first morning, the way you speak to a dog you have seen from across a plaza and never been close enough to touch.

And because she did not know what else to do, she hummed.

She hummed the three notes Don Emilio was humming.

They were not his notes, she realized as she sang them. They were not even, quite, her voice. They were older than Don Emilio. They were older than Mira's father — and Mira's father, who had hummed these same three notes every morning in the kitchen when she was very small, humming them into the dough as he worked, had not known where he had learned them either. He had told her once, when she was six and had asked. *Me las enseñaron sin enseñármelas,* he had said. *Es una canción que ya sabía.* I learned them without being taught. They were a song I already knew.

Mira had not thought of that sentence in three years.

She hummed the three notes, softly, at the rhythm Don Emilio was keeping.

And Don Emilio's destello lifted out of his chest.

It came slowly. Not like a startled thing. Like a gato plateado stretching awake in a patch of sun. It rose through the blue shirt and through the flour-dusted apron and into the air above the wooden table, and it stretched itself, and it was larger than Mira had expected — the size of a small loaf of bread — and it shaped itself, while she watched, into exactly that: a loaf-shape, amber threaded with rose, glowing with the warm glow of late afternoon even here at six o'clock in the morning.

It floated, slow, above Don Emilio's hands.

Mira stopped humming.

The destello did not return to him. It stayed. It hovered, calm, above the dough he was kneading, and Mira saw that it was doing nothing at all — only being. Only being there. Only being the amber-rose thing it had been inside him for fifty-one years, finally outside of him, finally visible, finally standing up in the air as itself.

Don Emilio stopped kneading.

He looked at his hands.

He looked at the dough.

He looked up — Mira held her breath — but he looked past her, into the middle distance, at no one in particular, and he smiled. A smile Mira had never seen on him. A smile that belonged to a different man — not a younger man, an older one. An older man who had remembered something.

— Qué mañana más buena — he said, to no one.

His amber-rose destello hovered three fingers above his left shoulder, doing its slow quiet nothing, radiating the warmth of a long afternoon Don Emilio could not name.

He did not see it.

He bent back to the dough.

The destello stayed for the length of time it takes to say a short prayer. Then it settled, slowly, back into his chest, and the light in the bakery returned to the ordinary light of a bakery at dawn.

Don Emilio was already humming again.

And across the river — Mira saw now, turning her head — the floración azul that had been fully open for forty years had opened just a little further. Pulsing slightly, at the rhythm of Don Emilio's three notes, the way the flowers of the valley had been doing every morning for four decades while no one thought to ask why.

She stepped back from the doorway.

From the opposite side of the narrow street, Marisol was watching.

The old perro veldoriano sat on her haunches in a patch of gold light, eyes steady, ears forward. She had not followed Mira to the bakery — Marisol had never followed Mira anywhere except the morning walk to school. She had been *here,* ahead of Mira, as if she had known. She had seen what Mira had done. Mira understood, in the small strange way one understands things one has never been told: Marisol had been watching this particular bakery through this particular door for most of her long life, waiting for this particular morning. She had known which morning it would be the way certain dogs know weather.

Their eyes met across the street.

Marisol's tail swept the dust, once. Slowly.

Mira made herself breathe.

— Ya lo sé — she whispered, and did not know what she knew, only that there was something now to know.

Marisol lay down in her patch of light and put her head between her paws. She did not stop watching.

---

Mira walked down the narrow street. She walked past the fountain. She walked past the little church. She did not remember, later, walking any of it.

What she remembered was this: her hand had flown up to her mouth halfway down the street, and she had stood under the acacia tree at the corner of the plaza for what might have been a minute and might have been longer, breathing, not crying, her heart knocking inside her chest the way a bird knocks at a closed window — not in panic, in insistence — and Chispa had pulsed at her shoulder in the same rhythm, as if Chispa could hear the heart, as if Chispa had always been able to hear the heart, as if Chispa was in some way *made* of the same thing the heart was made of.

And Mira had understood.

*I can wake them.*

Not *I saw it wake.* Not *I watched it happen.* *I can wake them.*

It was the three notes. It was her voice. It was something in her voice meeting something in his destello, and the meeting was the waking.

The word *wake* had arrived in her mind unbidden. She had not chosen it. It was the correct word. The destellos were sleeping. And she, with her three notes she had not invented, could meet them in their sleep and say hello, and they would rise.

She stood under the acacia.

And then she felt it: the *zumbido* dimmed.

It was brief. Two seconds. Three. The hum she had been carrying beneath her ribs since the first morning — the one she had not known was anything at all before Chispa and now found she could feel as a constant low tone at the edge of hearing — dropped. Not to silence. To *less.* As if a candle had been briefly cupped.

She looked up.

Across the plaza, moving against the early light, was a shadow.

It was moving in a direction the sun did not cast. There was nothing to cast it. It was a patch of darker air — not quite darkness, a kind of *dimming* — the size of a large sheep, moving slowly, deliberately, diagonal across the sunlit flagstones. It crossed the patch of warmth where only a moment before Mira had stood watching the chispero. It crossed in front of a sleepy old woman at a shop-door, who paused mid-yawn and pulled her shawl closer, unbothered, moving on. It passed through the base of the fountain as if the fountain were not there. It reached the far corner of the plaza and, as it did, seemed to thin — to become less opaque, less itself — and then it was gone.

The *zumbido* returned to its usual level.

Mira let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

She did not know what she had just seen. She knew, with the same certainty she had known about the destellos, that it was not dangerous — not to her, not to anyone. It was a passing thing. A dimming. A *less* that moved through the valley the way a cloud's shadow moved through a field on a windy day. Her chest felt, briefly, as if it had contained slightly less room than before, and then the feeling faded.

*La sombra que anda,* she thought, and she did not know where the words had come from. *The walking shadow.*

She pressed her hand flat against the warm piedra viva of the acacia-corner wall. The stone was warm. The warmth came back into her chest. Chispa pulsed, slow and steady, at her shoulder, as if reassuring her, as if saying: *I am still here, we are still here, the light is still here.*

She walked on.

She walked with the slow careful care of a child carrying something fragile.

She walked with the weight of a power she had not asked for and the quiet of a witness who had just understood that the world had more than one kind of weather.

Because — she realized, halfway to the schoolyard — she had no one to tell. She could not tell her mother. Her mother had not seen Chispa. Her mother would not see Don Emilio's loaf of light, or the chispero at the plaza bench, or the dimming that had crossed the fountain. Her mother would kiss her forehead and leave a print of flour and say *qué imaginación tienes, mi amor,* and mean it kindly, and not see.

She could not tell her teacher. She could not tell the girls in her class, who were sweet and silly and who would ask her to repeat it and repeat it until she could not bear the sound of the story in her own mouth.

She could not tell her father, who had not called in two weeks.

There was no one.

---

The schoolyard gate was ahead. La Abuela the sombraluz had, on her lowest branch, the first small indigo bud of the year — not yet a flower, but no longer merely a bud. Mira saw it and put her hand out and brushed it with two fingers, lightly, as if greeting a small animal.

— Hola — she whispered.

La Abuela was lit. The slow green destello deep in her bark pulsed once, softly, at the rhythm of Mira's heart.

The tree had been saying hello her whole life.

She went inside.

She sat at her desk.

She was eleven years old, and she could wake the destellos of every being she passed, and she had no one to tell.

She opened her blue notebook. Under the line that said *Nadie más los ve*, she wrote, in her careful blue-ink handwriting, the line that would live at the center of the notebook for the rest of the year:

*Pero yo puedo despertarlos.*

And then, because the truth of the sentence seemed to need another sentence beneath it to keep it company, she wrote, smaller:

*No sé qué significa. Pero es cierto.*

I don't know what it means. But it is true.

She closed the notebook.

She put her hands on the desk.

The first teacher came in and began the morning blessing — the same words the school had used since before any child in the room was born, words that the teacher said without thinking and that the children said along with her without thinking, a rote opening to the day as automatic as breathing. Mira stood with the rest of the class and said the words she had said every school morning of her life.

And today — for the first time — she heard, beneath the old words, the three notes.

The same three notes. The same three notes Don Emilio had been humming. The same three notes her father had hummed over the dough. The same three notes her mother hummed while kneading, every morning, so softly you could only hear them if you had just been standing in a bakery doorway at dawn.

They were in the blessing. They were the shape of the blessing. The cadence of the words rose and fell on exactly those three pitches — the voice of a whole school of children and their teacher, rising and falling on the same three notes, together, every morning of every year for as long as the school had existed.

She had not heard them before.

She had been singing them her whole life.

Mira sat down when the blessing ended. Chispa hovered, patient, at her shoulder. The morning class began. No one else in the room saw anything at all.

Under her desk, out of sight of her teacher, Mira opened the blue notebook to a blank page and wrote one more sentence, very small, very steady, in the way a girl writes something she needs to remember longer than the day:

*Las tres notas no eran mías. Nunca fueron mías. Yo solo las oí primero.*

The three notes were never mine. I only heard them first.

She closed the notebook again. Her teacher was writing on the slate. Chispa pulsed, slow, at her shoulder. And somewhere in the valley — though Mira could not have said how she knew — a woman she had known her whole life was sitting on a stoop across a street and shelling green beans into a wooden bowl, humming a song under her breath that no one in the pueblo had ever asked her to explain.

The morning continued. The bells at midday rang the pattern of ten that Mira had begun, now, to hear as ten.

She watched and she learned and she waited.

She did not know she was waiting.

*End of Chapter Two.*
