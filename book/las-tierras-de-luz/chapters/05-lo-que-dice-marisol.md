# Chapter Five: Lo Que Dice Marisol

In the morning the blue door was still closed.

Mira saw this before she saw anything else — even before her mother's voice in the kitchen, even before Tomás's breathing in the next room. She saw it through the lace curtain from her pillow, the way she had seen it last night before sleep and the way she had seen it the night before that. The shutter was closed. The lamp behind it was unlit. The chimney was bare of owl.

She had not slept well.

She had not slept badly. She had slept the kind of sleep one sleeps when the body had decided, against all advice, that two weeks ago the world had changed, and that there was no longer such a thing as a night that did not have a particular shape.

She got up.

She washed her face. She put on her uniform. She braided her own hair the way her mother had taught her — three strands, even tension, a small flat ribbon at the end — and went down to breakfast.

Remedios had set out a piece of bread and a small glass of warm milk at her place. Tomás was already eating. Neither of them mentioned the way Mira had been quiet last night, because Mira had been quiet a great many nights this month and the quiet had begun to be considered ordinary. Mira was grateful for this in a way she was not ready to be grateful for. She ate her bread. She drank her milk. She kissed her mother's flour-dusted cheek, and her brother's wet curl, and went out into the morning street.

Marisol was already at the corner of the Calle del Almendro.

Always. For four years. Always.

What was different today was the distance.

Marisol fell in beside her at Mira's right — but at four feet, not six. Mira noticed without choosing to. The dog walked the half-block to the school gate at this nearer distance, neither of them looking at each other, the way two people walk together who have not yet found a reason to say good morning. At the school gate, where every other day Marisol had stopped — for four years — and sat down to wait, today she did not stop.

She walked through the gate.

Mira slowed. She glanced back. Marisol was already past her, walking calmly across the schoolyard toward La Abuela. She did not seem to be choosing anything. She seemed to be doing what she had decided, before either of them woke, was what she would do today.

She lay down at the base of La Abuela's trunk. She put her chin on her paws. Her honey eyes were open. She was looking, generally, at nothing.

The first bell rang.

Mira walked to the classroom door.

---

She did not pay attention in the morning lessons.

She wrote what she was supposed to write. She read what she was supposed to read. When Señorita Inés asked the class a question about the names of the rivers of the Romance Realms — a list Mira had memorized when she was seven — Mira answered correctly without looking up from her notebook. The class moved on.

From the side window, if Mira leaned slightly to the left in her chair, she could see La Abuela's lower trunk. She could see the patch of bare earth at the base. She could see the curl of grey-brown that was Marisol, lying still, watching nothing.

Marisol did not move during the entire morning.

When the bell rang for first recess Mira waited. She let the others go first. She gathered her notebook and her water flask and walked, slowly, into the yard.

She crossed to La Abuela.

She sat down against the bark.

Marisol did not lift her head. She rolled her honey eyes upward to find Mira's face, held the look for a breath, and let her gaze settle back into its general not-looking.

Mira sat.

The other children had begun their recess noises in the far corner of the yard. The boys with their stone. The girls with their elastic. Soledad was at her wall. Her coral destello, Mira saw without looking, was a little brighter than yesterday. The size of a pigeon's egg now, no longer a sparrow's. It was glowing on its own, without urgency.

Mira looked at Marisol.

Marisol looked at the wall.

Mira waited a count of ten breaths. Then — because the chapter of her chest that had been kept closed for two days was beginning to push at the door of itself — she did the thing she had not yet done in two weeks of seeing.

She tried to ask.

She did not speak. She did not hum. She turned her face toward Marisol's grey-brown side and she put the question into her own mind as carefully as she would have put a small bowl down on a slick table, and she sent it forward — not loud, not pleading, the way you send a thought toward someone you trust to receive it.

*¿Dónde está? ¿Está bien?*

Where is she. Is she well.

She did not name Bela. The question carried the name without saying it.

For one breath Marisol did nothing. She remained as she had been. Honey eyes on the wall, chin on paws, the slow ribcage rising and falling at the unhurried rate of a very old dog at rest.

Then — without hurry, without drama, without anything that could have been mistaken for a rebuke — Marisol turned her head.

Away from Mira.

To the right. Two degrees. Three. The smallest reorientation of the muzzle, the smallest readjustment of the line of vision. She did not get up. She did not stand. She did not move from the spot she had been lying in. She only turned her face away, toward the far corner of the yard, toward nothing in particular, in the way an old animal turns its face away from a hand that has reached too far.

Mira's question stopped in the air between them.

It did not return to her. It did not echo. It simply stopped, and then it was gone.

She understood, the way she had understood things on the morning Chispa had appeared, the way she had understood that Soledad's destello had decided: this was not a refusal. Marisol was not telling her *no.* Marisol was not telling her anything in words. Marisol was simply demonstrating, in the only language she had, that this was not the kind of thing that could be asked.

The asking had been the error.

Not the wanting to know. The wanting to know was permitted. Mira was permitted to want to know. But the trying to extract the knowing — through Marisol, of all things, who had been a witness for four years and had never once been treated as an instrument — that was the small wrong-shaped thing she had just done.

She closed her eyes.

She put her hand flat on the warm earth beside Marisol's hip — not touching, six inches away — and she let it rest there.

After a long time Marisol turned her head back.

She did not look at Mira's face. She looked at Mira's hand on the earth. She looked at the hand for the length of time it took Mira's breath to become regular. Then she put her chin back on her paws.

Mira opened her eyes.

The bell rang.

She did not stand up at the bell. She let the other children file back inside without her. Marisol did not move. They sat — the girl and the dog, six inches apart, separated by a hand on the earth — until the second bell, and only then did Mira get up, brush her uniform, bend, and rest her palm for one breath above the dog's grey-brown shoulder. Not on it. Above it.

Marisol's eyes were closed.

Mira walked into the classroom.

---

Sitting at her desk with the column of arithmetic untouched in front of her, the memory came up.

It came up not in pictures but in the body, the way certain old memories return — the smell of flour first, then the cold of the courtyard tiles, then the particular grain of one specific cotton handkerchief, then the rest.

She had been nine.

It was the year her father's visits had begun to grow shorter. He had been home for two days, then gone again. He had left in the morning, before light. Mira had been sleeping. By the time she had come down to the kitchen the only sign of him had been the empty cup at his place at the table and his small leather travel-bag missing from the hook by the door.

Remedios had been preparing the bread.

Mira had eaten her breakfast. She had gone to school. She had come home. She had played a game with Tomás that involved a small wooden horse and a row of wooden pins. She had eaten supper. She had been about to go upstairs when she had seen, on the kitchen counter, the place where her mother's small piedra viva pendant always sat — the one her mother wore by day and removed at night and set on the counter while she did the evening dishes.

The pendant had not been there.

Mira had asked.

Remedios had checked her own neck, the way one checks a thing one has worn for so long one no longer feels its presence — and the pendant had not been at her neck either. Remedios had searched. The kitchen, the basin, the apron pocket, the small clay dish where she sometimes set her rings while kneading. The pendant had not been anywhere.

Remedios had paused in the middle of the search. She had said, in the bright deliberate voice of an adult who has decided, in the moment, not to be upset in front of a child, *no era tan importante, mi vida.*

But Mira had known. The pendant had been from before. It had been from the year she was born. It had been one of the small piedra viva pendants the women of the valley wore for twenty-six years without noticing they wore them, and Mira's father had given it to Remedios in the early days, when he had been home, when the house had not yet learned to be warm without him.

Mira had not said any of this.

She had sat down on the kitchen floor against the wall by the door, and she had cried — quietly, without sound, in the way certain children cry when they have already learned that crying loud will make a parent feel worse and then a parent will be cross with them for making the parent feel worse — and Remedios had not noticed because Remedios had gone back to the bread.

That was when Marisol had come.

Mira had not seen her arrive. She had become aware of her gradually: a presence in the open courtyard door, where the door had been propped open against the warm afternoon. The grey-brown shape of an old village dog, lying down in the doorway, chin on paws. Eyes open. Not coming in. Not asking anything. Just there.

Marisol had never come this close before. Mira had been seven when Marisol had begun walking her to school. Marisol had never crossed onto the family's stoop. Marisol had never lain down in their doorway. Marisol had never, that Mira had noticed, even slowed in passing the courtyard.

That afternoon Marisol had lain in the doorway for the length of time it took Mira to stop crying.

Mira had not stopped crying because Marisol had come. She had not stopped because she had been comforted. She had not stopped because anything had changed. She had stopped because — eventually — children stop crying, and she had been a child who learned early that crying alone for too long was a thing that wore her out without serving anything.

When Mira had stopped, Marisol had got up. Stretched once. And gone away.

Three days later Remedios had found the pendant in the basket of flour — where, the previous week, before Mira's father's visit, she had set it down for one absent moment while taking off her apron, and where it had sat under the white powder for a week without being missed because it had been against her chest in her mind, and she had kept reaching for it and finding the place at her throat where it should have been, and had each time, in some small distracted corner of herself, thought *I will look for it later.*

The pendant had been recovered. The piedra viva had still been warm. Remedios had washed it in the basin and put it back around her neck, and had said nothing about it for three weeks, and then one evening at supper she had said, casually, *encontré la piedrita,* and that was all.

Mira at nine had not understood why Marisol had come.

She had not, at nine, had any way to understand. She had assumed — the way children assume things — that Marisol had simply chosen, that day, to lie down in the doorway. Children took such things at the level of weather.

At eleven, sitting at her desk with the column of arithmetic unwritten, she understood.

Marisol had not come for the pendant.

Marisol had not come because she could fix anything. Marisol could not have found the pendant any more than the lechuza could have found it. Marisol had no use that was a use.

Marisol had come because Mira had been crying, and because some kinds of crying needed witness, and because witness — Mira understood now with a clarity that had no language — was a thing certain old animals knew how to give without doing.

Marisol had been demonstrating, at six feet, since Mira was seven.

Mira put her face down into her hands at her desk. Señorita Inés did not notice. The children around her did not notice. After a moment Mira sat up and began the column of arithmetic.

Forty-seven plus twenty-eight. Seventy-five.

She underlined the answer twice with her usual careful hand.

---

After school Marisol did not follow her.

This was not unusual. Marisol had never followed her home. The four years of accompaniment had been morning-only, the school walk only, never the return. When the second-bell of the school day rang and Mira came out into the yard, Marisol was no longer at La Abuela's base. The patch of earth where she had lain was indented, slightly warmed, and empty. Mira did not know which way she had gone.

Mira walked alone.

She walked the long way, but not toward the bakery. She did not pass the Calle del Almendro. She turned, instead, at the bridge, onto the small footpath she had not taken in three years — the one that branched off the road and went down through the willow scrub to the bend in the Río Claro where the floración azul grew thickest.

She had played here when she was small. She had stopped, the year her father's visits had begun to shorten, without quite deciding to stop. The path had become one of those places that belonged to a younger version of herself.

The path was overgrown. The willows had let down their long leaves over it. Mira pushed through. Her uniform sleeve caught on a twig and tore — not badly, a small thing — and she did not slow.

The bend opened.

The Río Claro at this hour was celadon. The water moved slowly because the bend had built up, over geological time, a shelf of rounded piedra viva that held the channel at a wider, shallower flow. The floración azul along the bank were wide open, all of them, in their five-petaled blue-shifting-toward-violet, stretching from where she stood to as far down the bank as she could see. There was no one. There were no voices. Even the zorzales had stopped — they were always quieter at this hour, which was the hour before *la hora de los destellos* — and the only sound was the soft burble of the river over the stones.

Mira sat down on the bank.

The earth was cool. The grass smelled of grass. Chispa hovered at her shoulder, and then — slowly, without being asked — drifted forward. Out over the river. To a meter in front of her, in the patch of light that came through a gap in the willow leaves, where the gold of the late afternoon was catching the surface of the water.

Chispa hung there.

It pulsed, slow, at a rhythm that was not Mira's heartbeat.

Mira closed her eyes.

The zumbido deepened.

It deepened in a way that startled her — not louder, but thicker. As if the air at the river had more of it than the air everywhere else. As if the channel itself were a vein the valley used to carry something it carried always but only here in concentration. The hum was in her chest, in the cold stones of the bank, in the soft earth, in the leaves of the willow above her, in the floración azul at the water's edge. It was everything at once.

She sat in it.

She did not hum. She did not need to. She did not have a question for the river. She did not have a question for Chispa. The morning's wrongness with Marisol had used up the part of her that asked. What was left was the part of her that simply was.

Above her, the willow leaves moved.

Below her, the water moved.

In her chest, the hum that had been thinner-than-usual for two days began, gently, to come back to its proper depth.

She thought — not in words exactly, more in the shape that thoughts have before they are language — that the river had been here before she had been born and would be here after she was gone, and that the hum in the river was the same hum that had been in her chest on the first morning, and that there was a kind of company that did not require the company to know that you were there.

She sat for what might have been an hour.

When she stood, her legs were stiff. The light had moved. Chispa drifted back to its place at her shoulder, slow, settled. The zumbido in her chest was at a depth she had never felt outside of *la hora de los destellos.*

She walked back up the path.

---

The afternoon was at the gold edge of dusk when she reached her own street.

She did not look at the blue door.

She told herself this. She told herself this as she turned onto the Calle del Almendro. She told herself this as her feet moved past the row of houses on the right. She told herself this until — three doors before her own — she looked.

The wooden bowl was not on the stoop. The chimney was bare of owl. Bela was not on the step.

But the small high window beside the blue door — the one that had been dark for two evenings — had, at the very edge of the closed shutter, a thin line of yellow.

A lamp had been lit inside.

Not bright. Not announcing. Just on. The kind of lamp lit by an old woman doing some quiet thing in a back room and not yet done with it.

The small space inside Mira's chest that had been closed for two days opened.

She did not stop walking.

She did not turn her head.

She passed the door and reached her own and lifted the latch and went inside.

In the kitchen, her mother was setting plates. Tomás was washing his hands at the basin. Remedios looked up.

— ¿Dónde estabas, mi vida? Te tardaste.

— En el río.

— ¿En el río? ¿Sola?

— Sí. Estaba bien.

Remedios looked at her for a moment longer than she usually did. The half-beat with the pen capped, the half-beat with the ladle over the pot — the small pause the BIBLE had named *radial.* The look that almost reached the layer below words. Then she capped the look the way she had capped the pen, and went back to the plates, and said only:

— No vayas sola otra vez sin decirme.

— Sí, mamá.

— ¿Comiste algo?

— No.

— Pan y queso. Tomás, lava bien.

Tomás laughed — *¡estoy lavando!* — and Mira sat down at the table, and the household resumed its evening rhythm, and the bread was passed and the cheese was cut and the small absurd argument about whose turn it was to feed the chickens in the morning was had again, the way it was had every evening, and Mira ate.

---

That night she wrote in the notebook on the eighth page, in the small steady hand:

*Marisol no me dejó preguntar.*

*No me regañó. No me alejó. Solo miró a otro lado.*

*Aprendí algo. Todavía no sé qué.*

She underlined nothing. She did not gloss the lines. She did not write what she had thought at the river, because what she had thought at the river had not been in words and she did not have the words yet.

Below it, very small, she wrote:

*La luz de la ventana azul está prendida.*

She closed the notebook.

She lay in bed with her face toward the window and looked through the lace curtain at the small thin yellow line at the edge of the closed shutter across the street. After a while she heard — felt — the silent passage of the lechuza Silencio across the rooftops. Going home.

The yellow line stayed lit.

Chispa hovered above her chest, the prismatic edge softly visible in the moon-light, pulsing once at the rhythm of her heart and then settling.

She slept.

*End of Chapter Five.*
