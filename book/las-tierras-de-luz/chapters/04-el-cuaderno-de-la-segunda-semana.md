# Chapter Four: El Cuaderno de la Segunda Semana

The new rule announced itself during arithmetic.

Mira was at her desk at the back of the classroom, copying numbers into a column that ran down the right-hand margin of her exercise book. Adding. Carrying. Señorita Inés was at the board, drawing with a piece of chalk that squeaked at the same place on every downstroke. The room was full of the small even sounds of children who were not paying complete attention but who had learned that not paying complete attention had to look like paying it. Pencils. Throat-clearing. The thin scrape of a chair leg. Outside, a zorzal had begun the second part of its mid-morning song.

Mira was not thinking about Chispa.

That was the new thing. For two weeks she had been thinking about Chispa with every breath — checking the air at her right shoulder, watching the light pulse, watching it not pulse, naming colors, mapping the pueblo. Now, at her desk, she had let the mapping lapse for one full lesson because Señorita Inés had asked her to add forty-seven and twenty-eight and the column had asked to be finished. She had been adding, and carrying, and not thinking about anything in particular, when she happened to look up — just a glance, the kind you make to rest your eyes.

Señorita Inés's destello rose halfway out of her chest.

It was the color of weak tea — pale brown, golden at the edges, soft. Mira had seen it before. It was small. It was not anyone's idea of a remarkable destello. Today it lifted free of Señorita Inés's blouse, drifted upward by perhaps a finger's width, and hovered there for the time it took Señorita Inés to write *equals seventy-five* on the board and underline the answer twice with her usual careful hand.

Then it sank back.

Mira did not breathe.

She had not been humming. She had not been thinking of Señorita Inés. Chispa was at her shoulder, doing the unremarkable thing Chispa did when Mira was not paying attention to it — drifting, not pulsing, almost asleep itself.

She wrote the next answer in the column. Forty-three plus nineteen. Sixty-two. Her hand was steady. Her face, she thought, was steady. Inside, the place near her heart sat down and refused to make sense.

When the bell for first recess rang, she did not move. She let the other children file out. She read the column of answers in her exercise book three times without reading it. Then she opened the blue notebook in her lap, under the cover of her exercise book, and wrote, in handwriting smaller and more careful than usual:

*Hoy: Chispa hace una cosa cuando no estoy mirando.*

She did not finish the sentence. The period sat at the end of the line like a placeholder, waiting for the rest to arrive.

She closed the notebook.

In the corridor, on her way out, she walked past a knot of older girls who stood in their habitual cluster by the chalk-bin, and she saw, without meaning to look, that all five of their destellos had grown half a shade brighter since the morning. Gold and rose and lavender and pale gold and the small patient amber of a girl who, Mira knew from her mother, had been keeping the family's accounts since she was eleven. Each one a small candle warmer than it had been at first bell. Not because Mira had hummed at them. Not because Mira had wanted them to. Because she had been seeing them, every morning for two weeks, and they had, in the way of certain things, grown into the looking.

She kept her face down and walked into the yard.

---

La Abuela's slow green was breathing in the trunk above her head. Mira sat against the warm bark and watched the other children. The boys playing some game with a stone. The girls with their elastic. Every body had its color now. Coral, lavender, gold, verde, rose. The small fizzing brightnesses of children who were in no danger of being unattended for long.

And Soledad, sitting alone against the opposite wall.

Soledad was nine. Two years younger than Mira. She had come to school with her grandmother every morning until three weeks ago, and now she came alone, and she sat now where she had sat every recess since: against the wall, in the shade, knees drawn up, reading nothing, watching nothing. The grandmother had not been spoken of. There were rules in the pueblo about how children spoke of recently-empty houses. Mira did not know them, exactly. She knew the shape of them.

For a week, when Mira had thought to look, she had not been able to find Soledad's destello.

It had not been a thing she had named. She had simply looked, the way she had learned to look, and where there should have been color there had been a kind of stillness. A low place in the air over Soledad's chest. Not absence. Quieted. As if a candle had been put inside a glass, with the wick still warm but the air around it given no work to do.

Mira had thought, at first, that this was what grief looked like. Now, sitting under La Abuela, she was beginning to suspect that she did not know what grief looked like, only what some kinds of grief did to the seeing of people who saw destellos.

She did not approach Soledad.

She had stopped approaching after the Tomás afternoon. She had written *no se puede empujar* in the notebook and meant it. She did not want to push. She did not want to try. She had not even meant to look — but the looking had become, in two weeks, the way she breathed, and she had looked across the yard at Soledad without choosing to, and the looking had held longer than she had intended because of the quietness over Soledad's ribs.

She turned her face toward La Abuela's bark and closed her eyes.

She hummed.

Not loud. Not for anyone. Three notes — the three notes — soft, almost under her breath, the way her mother hummed them while kneading. She had been humming them to herself this way for a week, in private moments, when no one could hear. It had begun to feel like something she did to keep her own zumbido company. Like saying *here I am* to whatever in her chest still did not have a name.

She hummed the three notes once. Twice. Three times.

She heard Soledad gasp.

It was a small sound. A child's sharp inhale. The kind of breath you take when you have been pinched, except softer, except more startled — the kind of breath you take when something good has happened to you that you cannot account for.

Mira opened her eyes.

Soledad's face was wet.

She was sitting against the wall in the same posture as before — knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them — but she was crying. Not sobbing. Tears running, simple and unguarded, the way water runs over a thing that has been dry for a long time. Her face was open with a kind of confusion. Her mouth was slightly parted.

And above Soledad's heart — visible, plain — was a small destello. Coral. The pale coral of the inside of a shell. The size of a sparrow's egg. It was not blazing. It was barely brighter than ambient air. But it was there, where for a week there had been nothing.

Soledad put her hand to her chest.

She looked surprised.

She wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist and looked at the wetness on it. She looked across the yard at the other children. She looked up at the sky. She looked back at her hand.

Mira had not moved.

Soledad sat for the length of time it took Mira's heart to beat thirty times. Then her face softened, in the way a face softens when an unexplained thing has settled and become bearable. She rubbed both eyes, calmly, with both knuckles. She picked up the small flat stone she had been turning in her hand. She turned it again.

The coral destello settled.

It did not disappear. It returned to the place above her heart and dimmed, but it did not extinguish. It glowed faintly there, the way a banked coal glows in a hearth at dawn.

Mira watched.

She had not been singing for Soledad. She had not been thinking about Soledad. She had been singing for the company of her own zumbido. The three notes had reached across the schoolyard without being aimed. The destello in Soledad — which had been quieted by something Mira did not have a word for — had risen on its own breath, met Mira's three notes in the air, and decided to come back.

Decided was the word that came to Mira. Soledad's destello had decided.

It had not been Mira's choice. It had not been Soledad's choice. The destello itself had chosen.

Mira's hand went up to her chest and pressed there. The place near her heart was sitting down again, the way it had sat down on the first morning. She pressed harder, as if she could hold it there.

The bell rang for the end of recess.

Soledad stood up. She brushed her skirt. She walked toward the line forming at the classroom door.

As she passed Mira she paused — half a second, perhaps less — and her eyes flicked to Mira's face. Not at her chest. Not at her shoulder. At her face. Her expression was not a question. It was a gentleness that had been newly issued. Her mouth did not smile. The corners of her eyes did, briefly.

She walked on.

Mira sat under La Abuela for another moment.

Then she got up and walked into the line.

---

She walked home the long way.

She did this without choosing to, the way she had walked the long way to the bakery in the second week. Her feet went through the small movements of choice — turning left at the fountain instead of right — and the rest of her went along behind, watching.

The afternoon was gold in the low slow way of late-week afternoons. The whitewashed walls were turning warm. The piedra viva at the base of the fountain had begun, in the gold light, to glow at its translucent edges, the way it always did at this hour, and that no one in the pueblo had ever bothered to name.

She passed the bakery. The floración azul along the side wall were still wide open in the unreasonable way they had been wide open for forty years. Don Emilio inside was wiping down the long table with a damp cloth. He did not look up. He was humming.

She turned into the Calle del Almendro.

The blue door was closed.

She had known, at the corner, that this might be the case. She slowed her step before she could see it, because her chest had told her before her eyes had. By the time she came level with the door she had already absorbed the absence into her walking.

The wooden bowl was not in the doorway.

The stoop was bare.

Above the chimney, where Silencio sat most afternoons, the roof tiles were unremarkable in the gold light. The window beside the door — the small high window that had held a single warm lamp the night before — was dark. Not just unlit. Dark in the way a window is dark when the shutters are closed and the inside of the room is not being used.

Mira did not stop walking.

She walked past the blue door at the same pace she had been walking before. She did not look at it directly. She glanced at it the way one glances at a friend's face across a plaza — to confirm — and then walked on, because confirmation was all she had needed.

Twenty meters later, at her own courtyard door, she stopped.

She put her hand on the latch.

She did not lift it.

She stood there for what might have been ten breaths. Chispa hovered, patient, at her shoulder. The zumbido in her chest was thinner than usual. Not gone. Just less. As if the valley itself was paying attention to something elsewhere.

She lifted the latch.

She went in.

In the kitchen her mother was cutting onions and humming the three notes. Tomás was at the table making a careful drawing of a fish with all of its scales individually marked. Remedios looked up.

— Hola, mi vida. ¿Buen día?

— Sí, mamá.

— ¿Sí?

— Sí.

Mira put her schoolbag in its place by the door. She washed her hands in the basin. She sat down at the table next to Tomás and looked at his fish. He pointed to one particular scale near the gill plate.

— Esa es la favorita.

— Es muy bonita.

She sat with him for a while, watching him draw. She did not look across the courtyard toward the wall that faced the Calle del Almendro and the blue door behind it. She did not need to.

---

After dinner Tomás wanted the courtyard.

It was the soft hour just before dusk — *la hora de los destellos,* though nobody in their household used the phrase anymore, the way certain phrases in long-rooted families lapse and survive only in the grandmothers — and Tomás had been indoors all day at school and again at the kitchen table, and the courtyard fountain was full of new water from the morning, and he wanted to put his hands in the cold. Remedios said yes. She said it with the small distracted *yes* of a mother who has already moved past the question and is thinking about the morning's bread, and Tomás clattered out into the courtyard with a small painted clay cup he had decided was for fish, and Mira, who had been about to read, took her book and followed him out and sat on the low stone bench against the wall.

The courtyard was small. The fountain in its center was small. Three steps to cross it, four to walk around the basin. The water was cold even in the warmth of the afternoon, because the well that fed it ran through the deep stone of the Shoulders and never warmed. Tomás dipped both hands in up to his wrists and laughed at the cold.

— ¡Mira, está helada!

— Lo sé. Siempre.

— ¡Pero hoy más!

— Hoy más.

He dropped to his knees on the wet stone surround and reached deeper, both arms now, looking for the narrow black darts of the new fish that the spring rain had carried in from somewhere underground. He had been after them for days without catching one. He did not really expect to catch one. The catching, Mira thought without thinking, was not the point.

She opened her book.

She did not read.

She watched him from the corner of her eye, the way she watched everyone now — half-aware of the water and his laugh and the gold light coming over the eastern wall, half-aware of his small azul claro destello flickering beneath his ribs in its usual restless way.

She had decided not to sing near him.

She had been keeping that promise for a week.

She had even, unconsciously, stopped humming the three notes when she was within ten meters of him — the way a person who has spilled hot water in a kitchen begins, without choosing to, to hold cups differently when a small child is in the room.

The promise held now. She did not hum. She did not even shape the notes silently in the chamber of her mouth, the way she sometimes did. She watched her brother fail to catch a fish. She read a sentence of her book three times without reading it.

And Tomás's azul claro erupted.

It happened without warning. It had nothing to do with her. He had laughed at the cold. He had reached deeper. The light underneath his ribs had been doing its small fluttering thing, flaring and settling, and then — between one of his laughs and the next — it lifted, all at once, in the way that water sometimes lifts off a hot stone all at once instead of evaporating slowly: the whole of it at once, vertically, without warning.

It rose three feet above his small wet head — the blue of the inside of a flame, white at the heart, with a halo at the edge that was almost violet, the size of a melon. It expanded — Mira watched — to the size of a small dog, and contracted, and expanded again, in a rhythm that was not Tomás's heartbeat, that was not anyone's heartbeat, that was the rhythm of a thing that was itself.

Tomás laughed and reached deeper into the water. He had not noticed.

Mira looked down at her book.

She looked at the page. She read the sentence. *La luna salía detrás de los olivos.* She did not look up. She did not look up. She kept her face very still and read the sentence and turned the page even though she had not finished the page.

She heard a splash — Tomás had moved, water had gone — and his bright laugh.

She turned the page back.

She did not read it.

She held the book in her lap for the length of time it took the destello to settle. She knew, without looking, when it had: the air in the courtyard returned to ordinary. The brightness she could not see but could feel — the way you feel a candle burning behind your shoulder without turning to look — went out. Tomás laughed again, less brilliantly this time. He had failed to catch the fish.

She lifted her eyes.

Tomás was kneeling at the fountain rim, both arms wet to the elbow, his hair stuck to his forehead in damp curls. His destello, beneath his ribs, was once again the small fluttering azul claro it had been all evening. He looked up at her.

— No me deja —

He meant the fish.

— Otra vez mañana — Mira said, and her voice came out level.

He sighed in the way a six-year-old sighs about an almost-fish, and stood up, and shook his hands so the water went everywhere, and ran inside to dry off.

Mira sat.

Chispa was at her shoulder. Its color, she noticed, had stilled. It was not green now, or gold, or prismatic at the edges. It was the small steady pearl-tone it sometimes settled into when something in Mira had grown very quiet inside her. As if Chispa, too, had looked away.

She closed her book.

---

That night the moon was full enough to throw shadow across her bed through the lace curtain. Chispa hovered above her chest, doing the soft pearl-toned thing. The house was asleep. Tomás had gone down without complaint. Remedios had kissed her forehead and gone to her own room — the door closing with the small soft click that had been the boundary of Mira's nights for as long as Mira could remember.

Mira opened the blue notebook on her knees.

She wrote on the seventh page, in a hand she had been growing into:

*Sin querer.*

She wrote it once. She did not gloss it. She did not explain it. Two words for a thing that had, today, happened twice — once to Soledad, once nearly to her own brother — and that she did not yet know what to do with.

She stared at the page.

She wrote, in smaller letters, underneath:

*Brillan así sin saber. Sin que nadie cante. Sin que nadie pida.*

Then, smaller still:

*¿Qué hago yo entonces?*

She did not write more. She did not know how to write the question that was sitting underneath the question. She closed the notebook.

She blew out the candle.

She lay on her back with the moon-light moving in the lace curtain above her bed and Chispa hovering quiet above her chest and the zumbido under her ribs at its thinner-than-usual rate. She turned her head sideways on the pillow and looked through the curtain at the row of houses across the narrow street.

All the windows were dark.

All of them.

The blue-door house was dark in the way it had been dark in the late afternoon — not asleep, not lit, not anything. The lamp was unlit. The chimney was bare of owl. The street was the kind of empty street that an old pueblo becomes after dinner has been eaten and the dishes have been washed and the children have been put down — the kind of empty that belonged to the pueblo and not to any one house.

But this particular emptiness had a shape Mira could feel.

She watched the dark window for a long time.

Once — she did not see it, she felt it — the lechuza Silencio passed silently across the rooftops. Going somewhere. Coming from somewhere. She heard nothing. She felt the small displacement of air against the moon-shadow on the lace curtain. Then nothing.

She did not know whether the old neighbor was inside that dark room or whether she was elsewhere, and Mira understood that this not-knowing was now a thing she would carry, the way she had begun, in the last two weeks, to carry several other things she had not signed up to carry.

Her mouth, in private, in the dark, formed a word she had never said aloud. Not the polite *Señora.* Not the careful *vecina vieja.* Just the name itself, the four-letter shape of it, the shape of someone you were beginning to need.

*Bela.*

She did not say it aloud. Some names you do not say aloud yet.

She closed her eyes.

She slept.

*End of Chapter Four.*
