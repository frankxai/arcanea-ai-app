# Chapter Three: La Mujer del Otro Lado

By the second week, Mira had woken eight destellos.

She kept count in the blue notebook. She wrote them down in a small column on the third page, under the two lines that had been there from the beginning, and she added, at the top of the fourth page, a new line in smaller handwriting:

*Nadie se da cuenta.*

She had tested it carefully. She was a careful child. On the morning she woke the stray gato plateado's destello — marigold-gold, shaped like a spinning sun, wildly excited to be outside at last — she had watched the cat's owner, who was sitting on the bench across the street drinking bitter morning tea, and the owner had not looked up. She had tested the vegetable-seller, Señora Marquina, whose destello was verde and the size of a plum; Señora Marquina had smiled at nothing, said *hoy sí hay buen día,* and gone on sorting luzfrutas. She had tested the postman. She had tested the blind old man who sold lottery tickets on the corner and who, she had been certain, would hear even if he could not see. He had turned his head slightly — she had held her breath — and then had said, to the empty air, *hoy huele a pan nuevo,* and smiled, and gone on waiting for customers.

No one noticed.

She had stopped trying to tell the grown-ups.

This was the new thing. Not the seeing — the seeing she had made her peace with. The waking she was learning to use like a new hand she had not realized was hers. What was hard was the telling. The telling was the place the aloneness lived.

She had tried her mother twice more. Once she had said — carefully, not looking up from her plate — *Mamá, ¿tú a veces ves luces en las cosas?* And Remedios, serving soup, had paused for half a heartbeat, her ladle over the pot, and had said without turning, *A veces, mi vida. Cuando estoy cansada.* And had gone on with the soup.

*A veces, mi vida. Cuando estoy cansada.*

Mira had written that sentence in the notebook too. She had underlined it. She did not know why she had underlined it. It did not seem like the kind of sentence a person underlined. But she had.

The second time she had tried, she had been washing dishes beside her mother, and she had said — she had tried to say — *Mamá, ¿y si yo viera más que cansancio?* But her voice had come out small and half-Veldarín and half-something-else, and Remedios had laughed and kissed her temple and said *mi niña filósofa,* and that was all.

She had not tried again after that.

But she had tried Tomás. Her brother. Six years old. Her azul claro brother whose destello was so bright Mira had decided not to sing near him too often.

It had been Tuesday. She had been sitting with him in the courtyard after school, helping him fold a paper boat, and his destello had been restless under his ribs — azul claro flaring and settling, flaring and settling, in the rhythm of a child who had been indoors too long and wanted to be outside. Mira had looked at him for a long time. She had thought: *if I could show anyone, I could show him. He is not yet full of the reasons why grown-ups do not see. He is only six.*

She had put down the paper.

— Tomás — she had said carefully. — Mira. Mira allí, sobre tu pecho. ¿Ves algo?

Tomás had looked down at his chest. He had looked up at her. He had laughed — the bright laugh of a six-year-old who loves his sister and trusts that anything she says is funny — and said, *¿Qué juegas, Mira?*

She had swallowed. She had tried again.

— Hay una luz, Tomás. ¿La sientes? ¿Como un calorcito?

And Tomás had looked at her seriously — he could be serious, he was small but he was serious — and he had placed his hand flat against his own chest, and concentrated hard, and said, *Sí, Mira. Tengo calor. Es mi corazón.*

Then he had laughed again and gone back to the paper boat.

She had sat with the paper boat in her lap for a long time. It was not his fault. It was not anyone's fault. But something had shifted in her — something small and definite. She had *tried* to make him see. She had *tried,* with her voice, to pull his seeing toward the thing. And Tomás had not seen, and he had not been harmed, but Mira had felt, briefly, as if she had nudged at a door that was supposed to be opened from the inside. As if the *trying* itself had been a small intrusion — polite, affectionate, and wrong.

She had put the paper boat in her pocket and not finished it.

That night she had written in the notebook, very small, on the fifth page:

*No se puede empujar. Creo que se tiene que venir por sí solo. No sé cómo sé esto. Lo sé.*

You can't push. I think it has to come on its own. I don't know how I know this. I know it.

She had stopped trying to tell, then. Not just because no one listened. Because the trying itself had begun to feel like an error.

---

It was a Thursday in the second week.

The light was the gold light of late afternoon, the hour the grandmothers called *la hora de los destellos* — the hour when Veldoria's whitewashed walls turned the color of warm bread, and children were called in from the plazas, and the destellos in old objects briefly became visible if you happened to be looking in exactly the right way. Mira was carrying a basket back from the market. Two lemons. A small string of dried peppers. A jar of olives. A handful of dried hierba del recuerdo that her mother had asked for because the plateau beekeepers had brought it down and it was cheap this week. The basket was light. She had taken the long way, as always, which meant coming up the narrow street that passed the church on the left and — on the right — the row of old houses where Señora Marquina's mother had lived once, and where the widow Herreros still lived, and where — at the far end of the row, in the oldest house on the street — stood the door that everyone in the pueblo simply called the blue door.

It was the only blue door in el pueblo.

The rest of the pueblo's doors were wood. Some were painted green. A few were painted the same cream color as the piedra viva walls. But this one — the one at number seventeen of the Calle del Almendro — was blue. A deep blue, a blue with a little black in it, the blue of the Mar Arcano at the hour the sea began to think about becoming night. It had been that blue for as long as Mira could remember, which meant it had been that blue since before Mira had been born, which meant the woman who lived behind it had painted it that blue once, a long time ago, and had not painted it anything else since.

The woman who lived behind the blue door was Señora Bela.

Mira had known this her whole life without it meaning anything. Señora Bela was one of the old women of the pueblo — *la vecina vieja del otro lado,* her mother sometimes called her, the old neighbor on the other side of the street — and she was the kind of old woman children said good afternoon to and then forgot about. She lived alone. She came out sometimes to buy bread. She went to the early blessing on Sundays and sat at the back, on the left. She sometimes sat on her stoop in the late afternoons with a wooden bowl in her lap, shelling beans, singing to herself a song that no one in the pueblo recognized.

On her roof, for as long as anyone had cared to notice, a large pale lechuza del velo perched every night and most dusks, silver-grey and near-invisible against the sky, and the pueblo had long ago stopped remarking on it. The plateau owls sometimes came down. This one just happened to have chosen Señora Bela's chimney.

Mira had said good afternoon to Señora Bela perhaps four hundred times in her life.

Today Señora Bela was on the stoop.

Today, as Mira had always done, Mira looked up as she walked past — and saw her — and prepared, because she was a polite child, to nod and say the three small words she had said four hundred times before.

She did not get to the words.

Because today, walking up the narrow street in the gold afternoon with a basket on her arm and Chispa hovering three fingers above her right shoulder, Mira had been looking — as she had learned to look — at the destellos of everyone she passed. The butcher's amber. The widow Herreros's pale violet, almond-small. The carpenter's boy, a coral orange that fizzed. And now, as Señora Bela came into view on the stoop, wooden bowl in her lap, her small brown hands moving slowly over the green beans, Mira looked for — expected — and did not find —

Nothing.

She did not see Señora Bela's destello.

She walked three more steps, slowing. She looked again.

She saw Señora Bela clearly. The worn grey dress. The white hair braided and pinned in a low coil. The small piedra viva pendant at her throat — so like Remedios's, and yet not exactly: Bela's stone was redder at the center, a color Mira had not seen in any of the pendants of the valley women. The hands — which were not Veldorian hands, quite, which had a different carriage Mira had never thought to notice — moving over the beans. But beneath Señora Bela's ribs, where Mira had learned to look, she saw no color. She saw no shimmer. She saw nothing at all.

No. Not nothing.

Mira slowed to a near-stop.

It was not that Señora Bela had no destello. It was that Señora Bela's destello was not *sleeping.*

It was awake.

It was awake and it was held, deep inside her chest, with a deliberateness Mira had never seen in any of the other destellos of the pueblo. A color that was not a single color. Not shimmer. Not green. Not gold. Something more like the inside of a pearl — opalescent — and small, and perfectly still, and hidden the way a candle flame is hidden when a hand cups around it to keep it from blowing out.

Mira stopped walking.

From inside Señora Bela's open doorway came a smell — not strong, not insistent, just present — of something baking that was not bread. Something grainier, sweeter, rounder. *Como maíz tostado, pero más lento. Con algo de sol adentro.* Mira had never smelled it in any kitchen in her life.

At the same moment, Señora Bela looked up.

Her eyes were dark. They were the dark brown of old aceituna wood. They found Mira's face first — the polite recognition old neighbors offer each other — and then, without hurry, without drama, without anything more than the small interior movement of a gaze relocating itself, they shifted.

They shifted to Mira's right shoulder.

To the space three fingers above Mira's right shoulder.

Where Chispa was hovering.

For one breath — one single unhurried breath in the gold late afternoon of a Thursday in the second week — Señora Bela looked at Chispa.

And something in her face — nothing dramatic, nothing a stranger would have noticed, only the smallest interior widening, as if a door far back inside her had opened slightly — moved. Her hands did not stop. But for half a heartbeat her hands slowed. And — without choosing it, without knowing she had done it — Bela's right hand left the bowl and touched her own chest. Palm flat above her heart. The briefest pressure. The way one touches a thing to be sure it is still there. The hand returned to the beans before the touch was finished.

And the opalescent light cupped inside her chest, which Mira had just learned to see, brightened — briefly, by a small fraction — as if something inside Bela had lifted its head to look.

Then it dimmed back. Her hands continued. Her face returned.

And Chispa — who had never moved differently in two weeks, who had always hovered at exactly that three-fingers distance at Mira's shoulder — pulsed.

Once.

Slow.

The way Chispa pulsed when Mira's heart knew a thing before Mira's head had caught up.

Señora Bela's gaze returned to Mira's face.

Mira could not breathe.

— Buenas tardes, niña — said Señora Bela.

Her voice was not a valley voice. Mira had said good afternoon to Señora Bela four hundred times and had never, once, noticed that her voice was not a valley voice. The vowels were softer. The consonants leaned differently — as if each syllable were being set down gently rather than sounded. There was a warmth in the *niña* that came from somewhere further away than this pueblo. Somewhere with a different sky. Somewhere the sun sat at a different angle in the afternoon.

Mira could not find her voice.

She managed — barely — a nod.

Señora Bela smiled. It was a small smile. Not a smile that explained anything. Not a smile that asked anything. A smile that said only: *I see you.*

And then she said, still shelling beans, her hands not stopping, looking down again at her bowl as if the moment had never happened —

— Hace buena tarde para las luces, ¿no?

*A good afternoon for the lights.*

That was what she said.

*Hace buena tarde para las luces, ¿no?*

It could have been a weather remark. It could have been the kind of thing an old woman says to a passing child about the quality of the afternoon. It could have meant the gold light on the walls. It could have meant the first stars that were not yet out but would be out soon. It could have meant the candles she was about to go inside and light because it was a Thursday and certain Thursdays, in the country her voice had come from, had always meant certain candles at dusk.

It could have meant any of these.

She did not answer. She could not. Her throat had closed. Her eyes had begun — without her permission — to fill.

Señora Bela did not look up again.

Her hands went on with the beans. She had begun, very softly, to hum.

The song was not a song Mira had ever heard. It was not a valley song. It was a song from somewhere else — somewhere with a different sun and a wide sea and a rhythm that came from a place Mira could not name — and it was slow, and gentle, and almost private, as if Señora Bela had been humming it in this doorway every afternoon of her long life and no one in the pueblo had ever thought to ask what it was.

But.

At the very beginning of the phrase the song returned to again and again, at the top of each repetition, there were three notes.

The same three notes.

The same three notes Don Emilio hummed every morning over the dough. The same three notes Mira's mother hummed under her breath while kneading. The same three notes her father had hummed in a kitchen three years ago and had said, laughing, *Me las enseñaron sin enseñármelas. Es una canción que ya sabía.*

The same three notes.

Mira's hand moved to her mouth of its own accord.

Señora Bela hummed on.

Mira stood for another breath. Then — because she did not know what else to do, because her throat was closed and her eyes had begun to overfill — she bowed her head a little, and turned, and walked.

She walked the last twenty meters to her own door without feeling her feet.

Silencio, the old lechuza on Señora Bela's roof, turned its head a half-rotation and watched her go. Its eyes were pale gold. It did not blink. It was still watching when Mira closed her courtyard door behind her.

---

She opened the courtyard door. She set down the basket. She stood for a moment in the courtyard, holding the handle of the door, looking at nothing. Chispa pulsed at her shoulder, slow and certain, the way a heart beats in a chest that has just understood something.

From inside the house her mother called — *¿Eres tú, Mira?* — and Mira said *sí, mamá,* in a voice that sounded almost like her own, and went in.

She went in and she ate the soup her mother had made, and she helped Tomás with his letters, and she washed the plates, and she set the basket by the door for the morning, and she kissed her mother good night, and she went upstairs. She did all of this as if she were watching herself from two rooms away.

In her room, with the shutters closed and the candle lit, she opened the blue notebook.

She did not write a list.

She wrote one sentence, in her very best handwriting, across the middle of the sixth page:

*Ella también ve.*

She also sees.

She did not underline it. She did not explain it. She did not qualify it. She sat with the sentence for a long time, watching the candle burn, watching Chispa hover above the page, watching the ink dry.

Then she closed the notebook, blew out the candle, and got into bed.

---

Her window looked out onto the narrow street. From her pillow she could see, through the thin lace curtain, the row of houses on the other side, the pale fronts of them silver in the moonlight, the dark rectangles of their shuttered windows.

All the windows were dark.

All except one.

At the far end of the row, in the house with the blue door, a single warm lamp still burned. It was not a bright lamp. It was the soft yellow of an old oil lamp, or of a candle in a glass. It threw one small square of gold light into the otherwise dark street.

Mira watched it for a long time.

She did not know what the light meant. She did not know whether the old woman was awake beside it, or whether it burned by itself in an empty room, or whether a small hand was moving beside it in some slow evening ritual a younger girl had no way of guessing. She did not know whether the look she had seen today had been a look — really — or whether she had invented it, whether her hunger to be seen had begun, finally, to invent eyes for her where there were none.

But.

For the first time in two weeks, she did not feel entirely alone with Chispa.

She lay in the dark and watched the single lamp across the street, and Chispa hovered above her chest at exactly three fingers' distance, pulsing with her heart, and somewhere in the house the clock in the kitchen chimed ten, and somewhere in the pueblo an old dog barked once, and settled.

Somewhere on the roof across the street, Silencio the lechuza lifted into flight, silent, and crossed from the blue-door house to Mira's own roof, and settled there. The lechuza had never, in eleven years, done this before.

Mira closed her eyes.

She did not sleep for a long time.

When she did, she dreamed — for the first time in three years — of her father's three notes. But in the dream the notes kept going. Past the third note there was a fourth, and a fifth, and a whole song that Mira had never heard but somehow knew, a song from a country she had never seen, whose walls were rust-red piedra roja that held the heat of a different sun, whose markets were louder and whose sky was deeper gold and whose people touched their own chest before reaching toward each other, a small gesture that meant *I bring only what I have.*

In the dream the song traveled. It traveled with a young woman across a corridor between Realms — a corridor that was open the year she crossed it and would be closed by the time her granddaughter's generation came of age. The young woman carried the song in her mouth because she could not carry her neighbors, or her kitchen, or the color of the stone walls, or the angle of her native sun. She could only carry the song.

She carried it into a valley that was not her valley.

In the dream, the young woman was still humming it on a stoop, sixty years later. Her hands moved slowly over green beans in a wooden bowl. Her small opalescent destello, cupped inside her chest the way a hand cups a candle flame, brightened slightly as she hummed.

And above the girl who was dreaming, in the room across the narrow street, a small prismatic light hovered.

In the dream, the two lights touched.

What began first — she could not have said. It was not sound. It was the way the valley's hum had sounded for one full breath, that first morning, when everything was at the same frequency. Like that, but longer. Like that, but answered.

---

*End of Chapter Three.*
