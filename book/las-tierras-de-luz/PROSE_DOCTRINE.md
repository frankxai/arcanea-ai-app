# PROSE DOCTRINE — Las Tierras de Luz

> The standard is not "good fantasy prose." The standard is the opening of *One Hundred Years of Solitude*, the moral plainness of Le Guin, the warmth and city-texture of Zafón and Allende, the forward pull of Bardugo and Rothfuss. Every page must earn a reader who has those books on the same shelf.

This document is the foundation the v1 draft lacked. It is binding on every chapter, both editions.

---

## THE DIAGNOSIS (what the v0/v1 draft did wrong)

1. **It told the gift instead of staging it.** "She had always seen colors around people" is a fact delivered to the reader. The masters would drop us inside *one* seeing — one person, one specific light, one concrete object the light is compared to — and let us infer the rest. The reader should discover Selene is different by watching other people *fail to react* to what she sees.

2. **Backstory stacked in past-perfect.** Long runs of "she had… had… had…" hold the reader at arm's length. Backstory must arrive as *scene* or as a single concrete image, never as a paragraph of summary the narrator recites.

3. **The narrator explained the emotion.** "the oldest fact of her life, older than language" is the writer telling us it's profound. Cut. Let the image be profound and say nothing.

4. **Monotone rhythm.** Too many sentences of the same medium length. Master prose has dynamic range: a clause that runs forty words and then a two-word sentence that lands like a door.

5. **Abstractions floated free.** Every abstraction must be tied to an object: the patched water-stain, the grandmother's stone warm in a palm, the loose thread on a sleeve. No feeling without a thing.

6. **The camera opened too wide.** Starting on "the city" before we are anchored in a body. Anchor in the body first. Earn the wide shot.

---

## THE SEVEN LAWS OF THIS BOOK

1. **Open every chapter inside a body, in a concrete present moment.** No establishing summary. The reader arrives already standing somewhere, already seeing one specific thing.

2. **Stage the miraculous as ordinary; stage the ordinary as miraculous.** García Márquez's law. A man with no light is reported in the same calm voice as the price of bread. The smell of bread is given the same wonder as a man with no light. The flatness of the delivery is what makes the magic real.

3. **Perception is event, not attribute.** Selene's sight is never described in the abstract. It happens, once, to one person, in front of us, and we feel its cost.

4. **Cut the connective tissue. Trust the cut.** Juxtapose. Two images side by side do more than a sentence explaining their relation. White space is a tool.

5. **Dynamic rhythm, always.** Read aloud. If three sentences in a row share a shape, break one. End hard beats on short sentences. Let one sentence run when wonder is accumulating.

6. **Every emotion is earned through a thing.** Grief is a mismatched patch of ceiling paint. Love is a lullaby sung over a crib and called a children's song. Abandonment is a colour going gray at the edges. Name the thing; let the feeling arrive on its own.

7. **Respect the reader's intelligence.** Le Guin's law. Never explain what the reader can feel. Never moralize what the story already means. Say less. Mean more.

## VOICE — THE FUSION

- **From García Márquez:** the matter-of-fact sublime; the sentence that accumulates; the family treated as cosmology; the first line that contains the book's whole destiny.
- **From Le Guin:** restraint, moral weight in plain words, the dignity of the unsaid, magic with rules and costs.
- **From Zafón & Allende:** the specific living texture of a city (Caracas: the *panadería*, the blackout, the Ávila, Altamira); family as the engine of fate; warmth that earns the darkness.
- **From Bardugo & Rothfuss:** momentum; a narrator you trust on sentence one; sensory immediacy; the chapter that ends on a hook you cannot refuse.

The two editions are not translations of each other. The **Spanish is the true edition** — Venezuelan-inflected, unitalicised, idiomatic, the language Selene thinks in. The English is its equal twin, not its shadow. Each must read as though written first in its own tongue.

---

## THE FIRST-SENTENCE TEST

Every chapter's first sentence must do at least two of these: drop us in a body; deliver a concrete image; contain a hidden ache; promise a destiny. If it only sets a scene, rewrite it.

The book's first sentence is the most important sentence in the book. It must contain the whole novel in miniature: a girl, a gift, a loneliness, a threshold.

---

*Applied first to: Prologue, Chapters 1–3, both editions. Then the standard holds for all eighteen chapters.*
