## Book One of the Arcanea Saga

# Chapter Two: Healer's Touch

The water in the wooden basin was cold, but Mera’s hands were warm.

She pressed a damp cloth against the scrape on Arion’s shoulder, her fingers moving with the precise, rhythmic light-touch of someone who had spent her youth sorting herbs by feel in the dark. The hearth-fire in her cottage threw long, amber shadows across the clean pine floor, smelling of dried chamomile and spruce needles.

"You're lucky the bone isn't cracked," Mera said, not looking up. She rinsed the cloth, the water turning a faint pink. "A charge from a wild boar is one thing. Whatever left these bruises... it had the weight of iron."

Arion sat on the low three-legged stool, his shoulder stiff. "It wasn't a boar, Mera."

"I know." She stopped her hands, looking at him now. Her eyes, the deep green of pine-woods in the rain, were serious. "I saw the ash on your boots. The grey stuff that doesn't wash out. That's not mud. That's the rot from the south."

She reached for a small clay jar of marigold ointment. As she worked the cool salve into his skin, Arion felt the tension leave his shoulder, replaced by a subtle, flowing warmth that tasted faintly of salt-air—Mera's natural resonance, the fluid current she had inherited from her mother’s coastal lineage. It was a gentle power, one that didn't force the skin to close but invited it to remember how to be whole.

But as her hand brushed his temple, she paused.

"Your eyes," she whispered. Her fingers lingered at his brow. "Arion, look at me."

He met her gaze.

"The brown is gone," she said. Her voice held a quiet, trembling weight. "There is... there is gold in the corners. And blue. Like the deep pools in the creek when the sun hits the stone. What happened in the woods?"

"I saw the lines, Mera," he said quietly, his voice flat. "Everything. The trees, the stones, the ground. They weren't solid. They were... configurations. Threads of light. I pulled one to stop the beast."

Mera looked down, her thumb tracing the line of his jaw before she caught herself and pulled her hand back, curling her fingers into her apron. She had looked at him this way since they were ten—with a focus that saw every hidden scrape, every quiet silence he tried to carry alone. For years, she had been the one who stayed when the others left, the one who cooked the broth when his father was too weak to stand, holding her own feelings in the quiet corners of her heart, waiting for him to look at her with the same attention.

But Arion’s gaze was already drifting toward the corner of the room.

In the shadow of the wood-box, Mamoru lay curled on a wool blanket.

The pup had been the size of a hare when Arion carried him in at midnight. Now, in the morning light, he was the size of a grown hound-pup, his joints thick, his ears standing tall and alert. His fur held the pale, metallic sheen of starlight, and as he breathed, the gold-white light wreathed his ribs in slow, warm pulses.

*Warm,* a thought drifted into Arion's mind—soft, like clean fleece. *Safe. The water-girl has a kind song.*

Mera walked toward the wood-box, her boots silent. She knelt beside the blanket, her hand hesitating above the blue-white fur.

"He doesn't have the shadow-chill," she said, her voice soft with wonder. She lowered her palm, touching Mamoru’s head.

The wolf pup leaned into her touch, letting out a soft, whistling sigh. As he did, a wave of liquid warmth rolled out from his skin, so bright and clean that Mera’s eyes widened. She did not pull her hand back.

"He feels like... like the first sun on the hills after a spring gale," she whispered. "There is no rot in him. But he isn't a wolf of the woods, Arion. He feels like the things they sing about in the old songs. The things before the war."

"He calls himself Mamoru," Arion said.

Mera looked at him over her shoulder. "He speaks to you?"

"In thoughts. Not words. Impressions of things."

Before she could answer, the heavy oak door of the cottage rattled. Three sharp knocks, hard and urgent.

Mera stood up, smoothing her apron, and pulled the latch.

It was Ismael, his coat unbuttoned, his face red from running up the hill. In his hand, he held a copper cog—an old piece of a water-wheel mechanism he had been trying to tune for days. It was vibrating, the copper teeth clicking against one another with a rapid, irregular rhythm that sounded like dry leaves in a storm.

"The southern boundary," Ismael said, his breath catching in his throat. He looked at Arion, then at the wolf. "The ward-stone. It's cracked. The old copper bands we laid last season... they're melting. The elders are calling the watch. Something is coming through the pass."
