## Book One of the Arcanea Saga

# Chapter One: Eye of the Wolf

The forest did not use words, but it kept a count.

Arion walked three paces behind his father, his boots finding the damp soil where the spruce needles had already packed down into a silent floor. The air smelled of wet bark and the cold, sharp scent of incoming winter. Dusk was settling over the Greenvale valleys in long, purple shadows, the light draining from the canopy like water sinking into sand.

Orion stopped. His hand rose, palm flat—the old hunter’s sign for stillness.

Arion went still. He did not breathe. He held his short ash-bow low, the wood cool against his palm.

The silence in the woods was not the ordinary quiet of evening. The birds had not simply stopped singing; they had vanished from the branches. The wind, which had been rattling the birch leaves a minute ago, died entirely. The forest felt heavy, like a room where the air had been compressed until it was difficult to draw into the lungs.

"Arion," Orion whispered, his voice barely carrying past his collar. "The ground."

Arion looked down.

The moss at his feet was not green. It was turning the color of wet ash, the small star-shaped heads of the moss-plants curling inward and dissolving into a dark, oily liquid that smelled of stagnant ponds and scorched iron. The decay was moving in a slow, circular wave, spreading outward from the deeper brush.

A sound came from the thicket. A wet, rattling breath.

The brush parted.

The stag that stepped into the clearing was large, but it was no longer a beast of the wood. Its left flank was stripped of fur, the flesh beneath grey and patterned with black veins that pulsed in time with its breathing. Its antlers, which should have been clean bone, were wreathed in a thick, tar-like shadow that dripped onto the forest floor, leaving small, smoking pits in the loam.

The animal's eyes were gone. In their place were two holes leaking a cold, white steam that drifted down its snout.

Orion lowered his spear, the iron head steady despite the dark. "Get behind the stone, Arion. Now."

"Father—"

"Go."

The stag did not bellow. It lunged.

It moved with a speed that was physically wrong for its size, its hooves striking the earth with the heavy, dead thuds of stone blocks falling into mud. Orion stepped forward, his weight shifted over his back leg, aiming the spear-point at the creature's neck.

But the stag did not veer. It struck the spear-shaft with its shoulder. The ash-wood shattered with a sharp report that echoed through the trees. Orion was thrown back, his body crashing into the roots of a fallen pine, his breath escaping him in a ragged gasp.

The stag turned, its head lowering, the tar-dripping antlers lining up with Orion's chest.

Arion did not think. He did not choose.

He reached out.

The world did not shift; it tore. The green and brown of the forest dissolved, the trees and soil turning into a complex, luminous grid of silver and gold threads that ran through everything, connecting the stones to the roots and the roots to the sky. The corrupted stag was a dark, jagged tear in the grid, a mass of black static that hummed with a high, painful screech that hurt Arion's teeth.

He saw the lines. He saw the tension in the earth beneath the stag's hooves.

He grabbed a cluster of the gold threads—the ones that ran deep into the limestone bedrock of the valley, humming with a low, heavy vibration that felt like gravity itself. He pulled.

The ground beneath the stag did not merely break; it recalculated. A slab of limestone rose three inches, the angle shifting in a fraction of a second.

The stag’s hooves found no grip. Its forward momentum carried it sideways, its heavy body slipping on the angled stone and crashing headlong into the trunk of a great stone-oak. The oak did not yield. The impact was a wet, heavy crack that shook the remaining leaves from the branches.

The black static wreathed around the stag flickered, hissed like water on hot coals, and went out. The beast lay still, its grey form rapidly dissolving into ash that the wind scattered across the grass.

The silver-gold grid vanished.

Arion fell to his knees, his hands pressed into the dirt. The skin of his palms was cold, his chest aching as if he had run five leagues without stopping. His ears rang with a high, empty hum, and the light of the forest seemed dull, flat, and distant.

"Arion," Orion groaned, struggling to sit up against the pine roots. His hand was pressed to his side, but his eyes were fixed on his son. "Your eyes..."

Arion did not answer. He was looking at his hands. They were shaking.

From the roots of the stone-oak, a sound came. A small, dry whimper.

Arion crawled forward, his knees scraping on the stones. In the hollow where the oak's roots met the earth, half-buried in the grey ash left by the stag, lay a pup.

It was no larger than a hare, its fur the pale, shimmering blue of starlight. But its chest was black with the same shadow rot that had consumed the stag, the dark veins creeping up toward its throat. It was wreathed in a faint, gold-white light that pulsed weakly, fighting the decay.

The pup looked at Arion. Its eyes were not empty; they were two clean circles of gold, wide with a silent, desperate terror.

*Help,* a voice said. It was not a sound in the ear; it was a cold, sharp needle of thought in the back of Arion's mind, smelling of pine-sap and early snow. *Please.*

Arion reached into the hollow.

"Arion, don't," Orion called, his voice strained. "It's touched by the Fen."

"It's dying," Arion said.

His fingers touched the pup’s side.

The cold hit him first—a freezing current that traveled up his arm like ice-water, turning his muscles to stone. The black veins on the pup's chest flared, reaching toward Arion's hand. He felt the shadow rot enter his own blood, a heavy, dissonant weight that wanted to silence his heart.

He did not let go.

He closed his eyes and looked for the gold threads again. He did not call them; he simply remembered them. He remembered the deep, low hum of the valley's bedrock, the slow, safe weight of the earth that had held his home since before he was born. He let that hum rise through his boots, through his spine, and into his hand.

The gold-white light around the pup flared. The black veins on its chest stopped their advance, then began to recede, dissolving into grey mist that drifted away in the wind.

The pup let out a soft, warm breath against Arion’s thumb.

The cold in Arion’s arm receded, leaving his skin warm and tingling with a strange, clean energy that felt like starlight made liquid.

He lifted the pup, tucking it inside his wool coat against his chest. The little beast was light, almost weightless, but its heart was beating fast and strong against his ribs.

Orion reached them, limping, his hand still holding his bruised side. He looked at the pup inside Arion’s coat, then looked at his son’s face.

"The village elders," Orion said quietly, "are going to have questions we cannot answer, Arion."

"Let them ask," Arion said, his hand resting over the small, warm weight of the wolf.

They turned back toward Greenvale, the lights of the village visible through the trees like small, orange sparks in the dark, entirely unaware of the storm that had just begun to gather.
