The light around the woman beside Selene’s desk turned the colour of a struck match, and Selene deleted the last sentence of her proposal.

“Too much?” she asked.

Her colleague looked up from her phone. “What?”

“The new layout.”

“No. My sister’s in labour.”

Selene put the sentence back.

On the screen, a clean black line divided two columns of text. She had spent forty minutes getting it right. Beyond the office windows the Ávila stood beneath a low shelf of cloud, and the light at her colleague’s wrists fluttered again. Joy, perhaps. Fear. Both. Selene could see it as plainly as the coffee stain on the desk and understand it considerably less well.

At twenty-nine, she was very good at making this distinction in retrospect.

She finished the proposal. She answered a message she wanted to answer and three she did not. When the others left for drinks, she went with them, laughed at a story about a spectacularly unsuccessful date, and stayed until the restaurant filled with the overlapping colours of strangers. Then she excused herself with a headache. It was easier than describing the effort of listening to one voice while someone else’s sorrow flared behind it.

At home she made coffee too late in the day. Guacamayas crossed the gap between the buildings, blue wings briefly catching the sun. Her phone showed the photograph she had taken of the mountain that morning: ordinary cloud, ordinary stone. None of the fine silver lines she had seen moving through it.

She opened the front camera.

A tired woman looked back. One eyebrow sat slightly higher than the other. The little gold hoops her grandmother had given her needed cleaning.

There was no light around her.

She moved the phone closer, as if a thing that had refused her for twenty-nine years might yield to a better angle.

A voice message arrived from Mercedes.

“Before you invent an excuse, I have made enough food for two.”

Selene smiled despite herself.

Her grandmother lived six floors below, in a flat whose windows were crowded with plants. Dinner was arepas, avocado, white cheese, and an argument about whether Selene was sleeping enough. Mercedes lost the argument without conceding it. When Selene took the plates to the sink, the old woman set a small cloth bundle on the table.

Inside lay a translucent stone.

Its light touched the ceiling in five narrow bands. Selene put a hand beneath them. The bands slid over her skin; nothing answered.

“Where did you get this?”

“From home.”

“You’re home.”

Mercedes turned the stone with one finger. “From before.”

Selene sat down.

The story took most of the evening. A place called Avilara. A mountain with a voice. A passage that had once opened beneath the Ávila and closed behind a frightened young woman carrying too little money and the wrong kind of shoes.

“You could have told me.”

“Yes.”

The quick agreement made Selene angrier.

“All those years I thought—”

“I know some of what you thought.” Mercedes’s hand remained on the table, an inch from hers. “I don’t know all of it.”

For once, Selene did not help someone finish the difficult part.

The old woman waited. Then she said, “I was afraid you would go.”

“So you let me be afraid to stay.”

Mercedes lowered her eyes. Her destello loosened around her shoulders, pale violet threaded with amber. Selene could have spent the night trying to decide which part was remorse and which part was love.

Instead she said, “I need you to answer my questions.”

They spoke until the coffee went cold.

The stone was a lens. It could clarify a living light for two breaths, then needed a day’s warmth against a living chest. It could neither create a gift nor tell its bearer what to do with one. No one, Mercedes said, could see their own destello.

“Then how do you know you have one?”

Her grandmother opened her mouth, stopped, and tried again.

“I have been told. By people I trust.”

Selene slept little. In the morning she told her mother where she was going, gave her the route Mercedes had drawn, and promised to return to the threshold the following evening if it remained open. Her mother disliked every part of it. They arranged a second check-in anyway.

At the mountain, mist caught in Selene’s hair. A silver cat sat beside a cleft she did not remember from any trail.

She lifted the stone.

For two breaths, the rock disclosed a seam of pink light. Beyond it stood a valley too deep for the mountain to contain.

The stone went cold.

Selene checked the knot on her bag. Then she stepped through.
