At two in the morning, Selene found a gardener cutting the dead centres from perfect flowers.

He dropped the brown pieces into a covered basket.

“Why not take the whole bloom?”

“People complain.”

“About dead flowers?”

“About empty branches.”

The tree outside her room had split along three limbs. Its blossoms still shone. Beyond the garden, light covered the city’s streets so thoroughly that Selene could see neither stars nor the shadows of the people walking beneath the lamps.

She borrowed a lantern and went to the stables.

Brío lifted his head when she entered. She let him smell the shutters before putting them in her bag. His nostrils moved over her fingers. No sudden terror. No transformation. Just the warm, useful fact of him.

They rode out before sunrise.

At the reserve’s edge, a patrol stopped them. Vyne’s temporary pass admitted Selene to the viewing terrace. It did not admit her to the lower stations.

“The order is for my safety?”

“And the system’s.”

She handed the pass back. “Then tell him I’m outside the system.”

It was a fine line. She regretted it before the guard had finished writing it down.

She went to the public landing instead.

The ferrykeeper was unloading baskets of fish so small that three fitted across her palm. She looked at Selene’s clean shirt, then at her face.

“You saw the hospital.”

“Yes.”

“People always return from the hospital as though we haven’t got sick people here.”

Selene took the next basket without answering.

Renata arrived that afternoon with Iolen and a cart full of tools. She also brought Tomás, his wrist bound but healing, and three station keepers whose wages had continued arriving after their stations closed.

They spread the maps on an upturned boat.

Each person had a different piece. Iolen knew the stone bypasses. The ferrykeeper knew the old flood marks. The station keepers knew which wheels were seized. Tomás knew the maintenance tunnels because he had spent two winters carrying replacement lenses through them.

Selene could see the living current.

It was useful. It was not enough.

Together they found a route through the plans: open the western relief beds, drain the tower circuit in sections, preserve the hospital’s isolated reservoir, and return the night draw to the marsh. The work required the reserve-house crew. It required Vyne. The relief beds would also flood the city's lower garden and threaten two storehouses built where Iolen's old map showed open ground. They marked the buildings for evacuation.

Renata volunteered to take the proposal.

“He’ll say no,” Selene said.

“Then he can say it to the people whose water he’s using.”

They waited.

At dusk, the marsh flowers began to glow. Huge white bowls opened above the black water, their edges bruised purple. Brío stood beside Selene on a low island while the last ibis crossed westward.

Under the nearest flower, a fish lay belly-up.

Selene removed the temporary shutters.

The Anima current ran bright and wrong beneath the surface. Four short pulses. One long. Then the long pulse did not end.

The roots tightened.

A sound travelled across the marsh like cloth tearing from one horizon to the other. The flower above her folded sharply inward. Brío jerked his head up.

From the city came the first alarm.

Tomás ran from the landing with a message strip in his good hand.

Vyne had agreed to an inspection at dawn.

The reserve had begun its new permanent cycle at dusk. Vyne had authorised the automatic change three days earlier; the inspection had not suspended it. Once under load, the master valve could only be closed after the relief beds opened. His message included the valve sequence and his seal.

Selene read the strip twice. Then she handed it to the ferrykeeper.

“Can we open the relief beds before the city releases its emergency water?”

The woman looked at the rising line on the stake.

“If we start now.”

Selene turned toward Brío. He was watching the slope that led away from the water.

This time she followed his gaze.
