Brío stopped at the mouth of the tunnel, and Selene got down.

The alarm bell rang somewhere beyond the dark. Water dropped from the lintel onto the horse’s ears.

Behind them, the landing crew were hauling the last boat above the flood mark. The western relief wheel lay through this service passage, beneath the old escarpment. Without it, opening the city gates would crush the settlement against its own embankment.

Renata had gone to the reserve house with the valve sequence. Iolen and two keepers were already hauling an iron brace along the upper road. The tunnel saved twenty minutes, but its ledge could not carry their cart. Selene and Tomás would reach the wheel first and establish the old bell relay; the third keeper remained with the ferry crew.

Selene loosened the girth and took off the saddlebags.

“I can walk from here.”

Tomás looked into the tunnel. “You won’t reach the far station before the release.”

He could run the first section, but his wrist would not let him turn the heavy signalling crank. Brío could carry the wedge and tools and bring Selene to the wheel. He could also refuse.

The horse lifted his head, listening.

Selene took the bell rope where it ran beside the entrance. She followed it to a brass housing and wrapped her spare shirt around the clapper. The alarm became a dull knock.

Brío breathed out.

She walked him to the entrance and waited. She felt the wet ground through her boots. She let her own breath arrive without arranging it into something impressive.

One ear came forward.

They entered at a walk.

The tunnel widened into a cavern behind the waterfall. Through the falling water Selene glimpsed a sky streaked with lightning. Pale roots descended into the rock, their Anima pulled taut toward the city. The sound was enormous, yet Brío’s breathing remained close enough to count without trying.

At a fork, the official route dipped into rising water.

Brío turned left.

Tomás checked the marks on the wall. “Old inspection ledge. It might still connect.”

“Might?”

“I haven’t used it.”

Selene examined the floor with the lantern. Dry grit. Recent hoofprints from the station mule. A repaired rail farther ahead.

“We try it slowly.”

The ledge brought them above the submerged corridor. At its far end, Tomás stayed with the signalling crank, above the ravine and in sight of the relief station. Its repeater line connected to the city bells. Selene remounted beyond the last low beam and asked Brío forward on the open service road.

He went.

They emerged beneath a sky torn with rain. The relief station stood on the opposite bank of a shallow stone spillway. Between it and Selene, the giant jaguar carried her cub by the scruff, crossing the last dry shelf toward high ground.

Selene waited.

The cat passed without looking at her. Its tracks showed where the water lay shallowest. She checked the footing, then let Brío approach.

Together they crossed.

At the station, the wheel would not turn.

Selene measured the rising water with the ferrykeeper’s red cord. One knot vanished. Then another.

She found the fault under the wheel housing: a root had forced a stone shoe sideways, trapping the spindle. A long crack ran through the supporting block.

The living current shimmered along the root, splitting where it pressed against glass.

She took out the stone of the veils.

One breath.

The lens separated the current from its reflection. The real strain ran beneath the spindle; the bright line above it was an echo in the wet glass.

Second breath.

She marked the lower seam with charcoal.

The stone went cold.

She pushed it back against her chest. No third breath would improve the mark now. She placed Iolen’s wedge against the seam and struck it with a maintenance hammer.

The wedge moved.

So did the crack.

Selene stopped hitting.

On the far side of the ravine, Tomás sounded the signal: the reserve-house crew were ready.

She answered once. Wait.

Then she put her palm on the stone and tried to remember exactly how little she could hold.
