Brío took the cuff of Selene’s shirt between his teeth and tested whether it was edible.

“It isn’t,” she told him.

“He likes to establish these things himself.”

The woman at the stable door had silver in her dark hair and a knife worn short from sharpening. She introduced herself as Renata Solar, examined the small map Mercedes had drawn, and sat down very abruptly.

For a moment Selene thought she might be ill.

“Mercedes,” Renata said.

“My grandmother.”

“That stubborn woman.”

“She’ll be delighted you remember.”

Renata laughed. The sound shook something loose in her face.

She had found Selene on the threshold path the evening before, wet to the knees and furious that her phone could photograph a flower taller than a bus but could not send the photograph anywhere. Renata had given her a bed, fed her, and sent a runner to check the passage. The runner crossed before dawn with Selene’s letter and returned with her mother’s reply. After breakfast Selene checked the cleft herself, well before the evening she had promised. It had closed. Renata gave her the watch service’s schedule for the next likely opening. Selene sent word by the corridor-watch service whenever a passage allowed it; going home would require more patience than going out.

Now there was Brío.

He was a dark bay gelding with a small star on his forehead and an expression suggesting that most human plans required supervision. Selene had ridden on family trips to the plains, then taken lessons when she could afford them. She knew enough to recognise the difference between a horse standing quietly and one holding himself still.

Brío watched a brass bell hanging outside the stable. One ear never left it.

“He was a river courier,” Renata said. “A flood took a bridge under him. His rider lived. Neither of them works that route now.”

“And you’re lending him to a stranger?”

“I’m deciding whether to.”

They began in the yard. Brío turned readily, stopped promptly, and refused to approach the bell. Selene tried a firmer cue.

His back tightened.

Renata lifted a hand. “Get off.”

Selene dismounted, cheeks hot.

“Watch his feet. Then his ears. Then decide what you know.”

The yard smelled of leather and damp wood. Outside, orchids leaned from the trees, their violet throats large enough to shelter a person from rain. Their roots held the stable roof in place. Selene looked at the flowers because it was easier than looking at Renata.

“I know he’s afraid.”

“You suspect. What did you see?”

“A fixed ear. Tension in his back. He shifted his weight away from the bell.”

“Good. Keep the observation. You can ask him about the rest.”

They moved the bell indoors. Brío walked across the yard.

Renata offered no congratulation. She handed Selene a brush.

By midday they had negotiated an arrangement. Renata would escort them as far as the first river station. Selene would check the saddle at every rest, walk the steepest descents, and bring Brío back if he became lame. Their destination was Solavila, where visiting Luminors received travellers seeking instruction. From there, if she still wished it, the road continued to Crystalpeak and the Academy.

“A school,” Selene said, trying to hide her disappointment.

“A good place to discover how much you don’t know.”

“I have quite a lot of experience with that.”

“Then you may be an excellent student.”

The path rose beneath red hanging bracts. Beyond them stretched a country assembled from recognitions that had outgrown their names: moriche palms beside black water, distant table mountains, an araguaney shedding yellow blossoms into a stream. A fallen bract held enough rain for Brío to drink.

Selene took out her phone. The camera made the mountains small.

She put it away.

Renata rode ahead on a grey mare. Brío followed, his stride warm and sure beneath Selene. There were lights everywhere—at the centres of birds, under the bark of trees, threading the enormous flowers—but distance gave them room. For the first time in months, she could look without bracing.

“Destellos,” Renata said when Selene asked.

“I know the word.”

“Then begin with the things you don’t know.”

Near evening, they passed a closed pavilion of white glass. No vine touched its walls. Selene noticed a thin blue current travelling from the surrounding roots toward the building.

A sign on the door read: RIVER STATION. SERVICE SUSPENDED.

Renata’s mouth tightened.

From somewhere inside came the sound of a bell.

Brío stopped so sharply that Selene caught the saddle.

Far ahead, between two orchid stems, something enormous moved against the light.
