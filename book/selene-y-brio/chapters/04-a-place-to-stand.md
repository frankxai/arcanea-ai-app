The bowl hit the ground for the fourth time, and Master Iolen Stoneward looked at Selene’s boots.

“You keep trying to balance it with your face.”

“It would help if it were flat.”

“It’s a bowl.”

He picked it up and placed it on the wall between them.

Iolen repaired the old river terraces in exchange for meals, tools, and the privilege of complaining about other people’s mortar. Renata had brought Selene to him after the flood. He listened to her account, asked three questions about the bridge supports, and offered her an apprenticeship until the next supply cart reached Solavila.

“For my gift?”

“For the wall.”

For six mornings she carried stone.

Her palms blistered. Her shoulders learned to object before her pride did. Iolen showed her how to set the heaviest load close to her body and how to stop before her grip failed. When she tried to hurry, he took half the stones out of her basket and made her carry them twice.

In the afternoons they sat beside the unfinished wall.

He did not tell her to empty her mind.

“Feel the place your heel meets the ground.”

“I can still see everything.”

“I didn’t ask you to stop seeing.”

A beetle crossed the back of her hand. Green light moved through the hedge. Below them, a family of chigüires settled in a warm pool with the solemnity of people who had booked it in advance.

Selene pressed her heel down. Stone. Damp leather. The weight of her own leg.

For a moment the lights became part of a landscape she occupied.

Then she thought, I’m doing it, and lost the feeling.

Iolen handed her the bowl.

By the eighth day she could carry it full of water from one end of the terrace to the other. On the ninth, he asked her a question halfway.

“Why did you tell the horse to go?”

Water splashed over her knuckles.

“Someone needed help.”

“Try the other reason.”

She stared at the little pool darkening the dust.

“Because I wanted to be certain.”

He waited.

“Because I’m tired of explaining what I see. I wanted one thing to happen that would prove it was useful.”

Iolen took the bowl. “The wall still needs finishing.”

She almost laughed. Then she did.

That afternoon he showed her a simple Foundation working. A grounded palm, a narrow intention, enough Anima to steady one loose stone while the mortar settled. He demonstrated it with his own hand. Selene felt the fine vibration through the wall.

When she tried, the stone shivered.

Then every muscle in her forearm locked.

Iolen lifted her hand immediately. “Enough.”

“I nearly—”

“Your hand is shaking. That is the answer for today.”

She hated how little she had accomplished. An hour later she could not button her cuff.

Seeing had never spent this part of her. Acting did.

They waited two days before she tried again. This time she held a pebble steady for the space of an ordinary exhalation. Then she stopped while she still could.

Iolen made no announcement about destiny.

He gave her a better-fitting pair of work gloves.

Renata visited at the end of the second week. She found Selene on the wall, laughing because Brío had carefully stolen a strip of sweet peel from Iolen’s pocket while the master was explaining discipline.

“He looks well,” Renata said.

“He’s an extortionist.”

“He was that before.”

The letter Renata carried was from Mercedes. Her mother had added a line at the bottom: Call me the moment anything in that place learns to use a telephone.

Selene read it twice.

Renata also brought news from the river. Three more stations had closed. The orchids below them had stayed open for four consecutive nights. Their petals were beginning to split.

Iolen folded the notice.

“The flowers need darkness to rest,” he said. “Whoever is drawing through those roots has forgotten what a pause is for.”

Selene looked down at the wall they had finished that morning. Every stone rested on something.

“What is the city resting on?” she asked.

Iolen went to fetch his old survey maps.
