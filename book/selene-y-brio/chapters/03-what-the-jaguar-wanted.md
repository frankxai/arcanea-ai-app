Selene’s hand closed on the reins before she understood how large the jaguar was.

Its shoulder stood higher than Brío’s head. Rain darkened the gold between its rosettes. One ear was torn, and the light running beneath its fur pulsed with a dense, restless green.

The cat stepped onto the path.

Renata’s mare backed once. Brío turned his head toward an uphill track.

“Turn uphill with him,” Renata said.

But Selene was staring at the jaguar’s light. It was brightest along the riverward side of its body. The cat looked downstream, then at them, then downstream again.

“It wants us to follow.”

“Selene—”

A bell rang below.

Through the trees a young courier stood beside a narrow service bridge, pulling a warning cord. Water foamed against the first support. A woman on the far bank held a child on her hip.

Selene felt the brief, intoxicating alignment of fear and purpose. Here was someone she could help. Here was a light that seemed to mean something simple.

She asked Brío down the slope.

He refused.

This time she pressed him.

He took three stiff steps. Mud slid under his hind foot. Selene hauled the rein, and the horse twisted to find his balance as the courier ran toward them. His boot struck a root. He fell badly, one arm trapped beneath him.

The bridge gave a dry crack.

Renata was off her mare and past them before Selene moved. She threw the woman a rope. The courier tried to rise and cried out. Water struck the bridge broadside and took half of it away.

What followed came back to Selene later in separate pieces: Brío scrambling onto firm ground; her own hands dragging the courier by his coat; Renata planting her boots around a tree root; the child refusing to let go of a red shoe.

They got everyone uphill.

For a while, all Selene could hear was the river.

The courier’s wrist swelled beneath Renata’s fingers. Renata asked his name. Tomás. He looked younger now that he was trying not to cry.

“I’m sorry,” Selene said.

He nodded without looking at her.

She wanted him to say it was all right. She knew better than to ask.

Across the water the jaguar climbed a shelf of rock. Something small emerged from the ferns beneath its belly: a cub, mud to its ears. The mother touched it with her nose and faced the broken bridge.

Selene looked from the cub to the cat’s green light.

“She was guarding the bank.”

“Perhaps,” Renata said, tying a splint. “Now you have another piece of information.”

“You could have stopped me.”

“I told you to turn.”

It was spoken without cruelty. Selene found that worse.

They spent the night in a maintenance shelter above the flood line. At the shelter they found a station attendant. Renata sent him ahead on the grey mare for help; the woman and child slept near the stove. Brío stood under the awning, favouring no leg, but he moved away when Selene reached for him.

She left her hand where he could see it.

Eventually she put it down.

At dawn Renata showed her the river schedule nailed inside the shelter. The emergency release was not due for another six days. Beside the date someone had stamped an amendment in blue ink.

CONTINUITY RESERVE: INCREASED DRAW.

“Solavila,” Renata said.

“The city did this?”

“The city signed a paper. We need to find out what the paper did.”

Selene offered Tomás her remaining cash before remembering it belonged to another world. She put the useless notes away. Renata found her work instead: clean the shelter, boil water, carry his bag to the returning transport.

When they lifted him into the cart, Selene said, “I made your route more dangerous.”

“Yes,” he answered.

“I’ll write what happened. Including what I did.”

He studied her, then gave her the name of the office that handled injuries.

After the cart left, she stood beside Brío for a long time. She brushed dried mud from his shoulder. When he shifted away, she followed only far enough to remain beside him.

Upstream, the bell rang again.

This time she listened to the horse.
