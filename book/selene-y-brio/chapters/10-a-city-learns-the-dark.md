The stone pressed back against Selene’s hand.

She set her heels. She kept the intention small: this seam, this moment, long enough for the wedge to move.

Anima passed through her arm with a force that made her teeth ache.

The crack steadied.

She drove the wedge once.

The spindle shifted. Water struck the far side of the gate, and the crack began to open again.

Selene took her hand away.

For a second she could not feel her fingers.

She had wanted to stay until the work was finished. Her hand was shaking too hard to close. Iolen’s voice, irritatingly ordinary, returned to her: That is the answer for today.

She sounded the alarm for assistance.

It was the signal she had least wanted to use.

Across the ravine, Tomás repeated it. The repeater bells carried it to Renata at the reserve house and to Iolen, already coming down the upper road.

Selene sat on the dry edge of the platform. Brío stood in the station's small stone-walled mule pen beside her, his bridle removed and the gate latched. She leaned her forehead against his shoulder.

“I can’t hold it.”

His skin twitched under a fly.

She began to laugh, then cried instead.

The wheel turned a fraction under the water’s pressure. Somewhere inside the stone, something clicked.

She wiped her face with the clean part of her sleeve and took up the red cord. She could still measure. She could still send a signal. She did both.

The level rose to the next knot.

From the city, a new signal answered: HOSPITAL RESERVOIR ISOLATED.

Then: TOWER CIRCUIT HELD.

Vyne had chosen.

The towers dimmed one by one. Their reflections shortened across the marsh. For the first time since Selene had entered Solavila, a patch of sky appeared between them.

The wheel eased.

Iolen arrived with two station keepers by the upper road, breathing hard and furious about the condition of the steps. He examined the charcoal mark, the wedge, and Selene’s hand.

“You stopped.”

“Yes.”

“Good. Give me the cord.”

He knelt against the stone while the keepers fitted an iron brace. Selene called the measurements. When the brace took the load, Iolen removed his palm. His whole body sagged. One keeper gave him an arm; the other braced her boots against the wheel.

Together they turned it.

The relief gate opened a handspan.

Water entered the old beds in a heavy, controlled sheet. The first channel filled. The second took the overflow. Selene watched the Anima current loosen along the roots.

“Hold there,” she said.

The keeper looked at Iolen.

He nodded toward Selene. “She can see that part.”

Four short pulses.

One long.

Then a pause.

Across the marsh, the flowers began to close.

The city crew opened the next gate. The ferrykeeper’s lantern moved along the landing as people checked the moorings. Tomás sent the all-clear from the tunnel and then, because he had only one good hand and was tired of cranking, added a much less formal signal.

Selene laughed so hard it hurt.

They spent the rest of the night making sure the first success did not become a second flood.

By dawn, two storehouses were damaged. The lower garden was gone. A station keeper had broken an ankle, and a week’s supply of fish spoiled before it could reach the market. The hospital lamps had stayed on.

Vyne arrived carrying a ledger that had got wet at one corner.

He asked for everyone’s names.

“For the inquiry,” he said before Selene could speak. “Including mine.”

She gave him the written account of the bridge accident, folded inside Iolen’s original letter. He read the first page, then placed it with his own report.

The sun rose on a city less brilliant than the one she had entered.

Below the relief station, the jaguar and her cub lay on a dry shelf above the river. The mother’s eyes followed a moving shadow in the reeds.

Selene left them their distance.

Her own hands remained bare of light.

She used them to pass Iolen a cup of water.
