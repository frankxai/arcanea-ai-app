Selene woke with Brío’s breath against her cheek.

He was looking beyond the camp.

The jaguar stood between two trees, its torn ear black against the moon. For one wild second Selene thought she had dreamed it there. Then the cat lowered its head and began to drink from the pool.

Brío trembled.

She moved slowly to his shoulder. The line of the escape path lay clear behind them. She took up the lead, keeping herself between him and the open slope only long enough to turn him toward it.

The cat did not follow.

At dawn she found its tracks beside the water. Smaller prints crossed them, then vanished into the ferns. The mother and cub had travelled upstream as the low banks flooded.

Selene ate cold bread and waited until the forest sounds returned.

The high road took them through wet stone and cloud. She walked beside Brío on the steepest sections. Pitcher plants grew in cracks beside the trail, their cups filled with clear water and the small remains of insects. Far below, the wetland had become a silver distance.

By late afternoon she found the cause of the cat’s silence.

A drainage grate had trapped the cub in a shallow stone basin. The mother paced above it. Each time Selene approached, the great head turned.

The green light beneath the rosettes sharpened.

Selene stopped.

“I can see you,” she said softly. “That doesn’t mean I know you.”

The sentence was for herself.

She took Brío far enough away to wait behind a broad rock. The cat’s attention returned to the cub. Selene studied the grate. One hinge had rusted through; the other held fast. If she could lift it from the side, the opening might be wide enough.

She remembered the stone trembling under her hand.

No.

She fetched a fallen branch instead.

The first attempt broke the branch. The noise brought the jaguar forward. Selene retreated until the mother stopped, and waited, sweat cooling beneath her shirt. On the second attempt she stayed behind the grate’s stone housing. She fitted a stronger pole under the damaged hinge and put her weight on it.

The metal rose.

The cub scrambled out, all paws and indignation, and disappeared under its mother.

Selene stepped back.

Nothing happened that would have made a useful legend. The jaguar did not bow. It did not offer a name. It licked the cub’s wet head until the cub protested.

Selene laughed once, breathless, then sat because her legs would no longer hold her.

They camped on opposite sides of the valley.

That night the mother limped down to the pool. It worried one forepaw, then rested it on a flat stone. Selene could see the thorn caught in the loose skin above the pad. She stayed beside the open path, watching until the cat stopped licking.

Selene held out her empty hand.

She waited through the ache in her arm. Brío stood behind her on a loose line, alert but no longer trying to leave.

At last Selene approached from the side. The jaguar watched her hand and kept its paw on the stone. When its lip tightened she stopped. Only when its breathing settled again did she reach the last small distance.

The fur was warm and coarser than she expected. She removed the thorn with two fingers. The cat’s breath moved the loose hair at her temple.

For an instant her sight went very deep.

She felt another attention arrive against her own: immense, particular, wholly uninterested in the person she had been trying to appear. Her palm prickled. She could have followed that presence farther.

She let it go.

The jaguar withdrew. Between the trees the cub made an impatient sound.

Before she slept, Selene wrapped the thorn she had removed in paper, labelled it with the date, and put it in her bag.

Then she wrote a second line beneath the first.

I helped. I was also afraid.

Both lines remained.

On the next ridge, Solavila appeared beyond the cloud. Its towers shone with a light so even that, for a moment, she could not see where the sun touched them.
