The ferrykeeper put Selene’s bag back on the bank.

“Horse or luggage.”

“There’s room.”

“There is. There’s also a current.”

Selene looked at the broad wooden ferry, the black water, and Brío, who had planted all four feet on dry ground. Iolen’s maps were wrapped inside her bag. So was the letter requesting an inspection of the city’s draw stations.

“The papers have to come.”

“Then you make two trips.”

It took until afternoon to get Brío aboard. They loosened the ramp, moved a clattering chain, let him examine the boat, and waited. Selene led him on and straight off again twice. On the third attempt he stayed.

The ferrykeeper pushed away without comment.

She was a lean woman with a white scar across one knuckle and a laugh that arrived rarely but occupied considerable space. People at the landing called her Maestra. When Selene asked what she taught, she pointed to the boat.

They crossed a country of palms and flooded grass. Giant otters surfaced near the reeds, calling to one another. Scarlet ibis lifted from a low island in a long red ribbon. No tepuis rose here; the land had opened over three days of travel until the sky seemed impossibly large.

At the far bank, the ferrykeeper handed Selene a pole.

“Tomorrow you can earn the second fare.”

“I thought I’d paid.”

“For passage. You asked about Flow.”

Selene stayed four days.

She learned where to place a foot on a moving deck. She learned that correcting too late required more force, and that a correction could itself tip the load. The ferrykeeper gave her a floating leaf to follow with her attention while ropes creaked, passengers asked questions, and Brío objected to a basket of chickens.

“Come back to the leaf when you notice you’ve lost it.”

“How often?”

“As often as you lose it.”

Selene tried to do this well enough to be praised. The ferrykeeper kept loading the boat.

On the third night they crossed beneath water flowers opening pale as porcelain. Their wide leaves lifted against the hull. Selene saw blue-white Anima running along the veins, thinning toward the roots where a metal collar had been driven into the riverbed.

The current pulled east.

The river flowed west.

She asked the ferrykeeper to stop.

They tied up on an island and followed the buried pipe to a service chamber. Inside, the same mark from the closed station glowed on a glass gauge. CONTINUITY RESERVE.

The gauge read NORMAL.

Outside, a fallen flower smelled faintly sour.

Selene wanted to tear the collar free. Instead she unfolded Iolen’s maps and checked the line. It connected to the city’s floodgates. The ferrykeeper showed her a maintenance wheel beneath the gauge.

“Turn this without opening a relief channel and you send the whole head of water into the landing.”

“Who taught you that?”

“My mother. After somebody with a better education nearly washed us away.”

They measured the water against a stake. They wrote the time. On the return crossing, Selene kept the leaf in sight while the ferrykeeper counted the beats between the gauge’s pulses. Four short. One long.

At breakfast they compared the count with Iolen’s notes. The reserve was drawing during its scheduled rest.

“Can you make it stop?” the ferrykeeper asked.

“Not by myself.”

“Good. Now you know how many people to bring.”

When Selene left, the woman gave her a short length of red measuring cord. One knot marked each handspan.

“No mystical significance,” she said when Selene examined it. “Don’t lose it.”

That evening, Brío stepped onto a shallow ford without being pressed.

Selene let him choose where to put his feet.

Above the far bank, an orchid opened. It was beautiful enough to make her stop.

Then she noticed the split running down its throat.
