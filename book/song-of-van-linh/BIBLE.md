## Voice register

- **Contemporary literary fantasy with mythic threading.** Modern Hanoi — neon, motorbikes, the specific humid evening light of the Old Quarter — carries the prose's foreground. The mythological layer breathes underneath.
- **Vietnamese cultural texture honored, not orientalized.** Food, ritual, family relationship, ancestor practice rendered with specificity and humility. No "exotic East" framing. No mystical-Asia tropes. The book trusts the reader to live inside the texture without the prose explaining it.
- **Romance threaded through, not centered.** The book is romance, ecological reckoning, and awakening — *in roughly that order, depending on the chapter.* The romance is Quan and An (per character profiles); their tension drives certain chapters but does not eclipse the larger frame.
- **Ghibli atmosphere.** Per the AUTHORS_NOTE and book.yaml. Hayao Miyazaki's *Spirited Away* and *Princess Mononoke* are the closest atmospheric references. Strange wonder. The natural world as alive and observing. The protagonist as small inside something vast and patient.
- **Multi-POV close third (probably; outline confirms).** An is the centering POV. Quan, Linh-Chi, supporting cast may carry chapters.
- **Sensory primacy in the dual-world dynamic.** The prose foregrounds **what crosses between the worlds** — sounds heard in Hanoi that come from Van Linh, smells in Van Linh that echo Hanoi, the simultaneity of two worlds breathing in the same air.
- **Arcanean voice (universal):** elevated but accessible, mythic but practical. The grief here is specific (ecological, post-colonial, intergenerational), and the prose carries it with weight without foreclosing hope.
- **Avoid:** "delve," "tapestry," "nestled," corporate language, exclamation marks for effect, italicized hype words. The chosen-one frame. The "magical Asia" trope. The ecological-disaster-porn trope (the book grieves but does not luxuriate in destruction).

## Dialogue style — LOCKED 2026-04-26 (revisable by FrankX after Vietnamese sensitivity reader pass)

**Standard quotation marks for spoken English. Vietnamese phrases that appear in dialogue render in Vietnamese without italics or translation; context reveals meaning.**

```
"What did you hear?" Quan asked.

An listened to the lake. The motorbikes behind her. The vendor's morning
call — *banh mi nong, banh mi nong nay!* The lake itself, holding its
breath under thirty meters of water and sediment.

"Something old," she said. "Something that's been waiting."

"Dau?" Quan's voice softer. "Where?"

She did not point. The lake answered for her — a single low pulse felt
in the chest, the way the river-hum had been felt before it stopped.

"There," she said.
```

**Spirit communication and Tu Linh speech rendered in italics, no quotes**, distinguishing it from human-mouth speech. The Tu Linh do not speak Vietnamese or English; they speak in the language that is older than language. The italics are the prose's gesture toward that distinction.

**Vietnamese diacritics:** the prose carries them. *banh mi* without diacritics is a Western take on a Vietnamese word; *bánh mì* is the word in its own register. The book uses the diacritic-bearing form consistently. (Frank confirms with Vietnamese sensitivity readers whether full Vietnamese diacritics or the simplified versions; this BIBLE locks "diacritics carried" as the principle.)

## The five spiritual beats — Song of Van Linh (Book One: The Girl Who Heard the River)

The four-book series carries a larger arc, but Book One specifically runs the Author Team five-beat structure:

1. **The Cracking** — An's ordinary world is contemporary Hanoi, with the cracks beginning to surface. Spirit foxes she has been seeing without naming what she is seeing. The lake breathing she has been hearing without naming what she is hearing. The world is the same; her access is changing.
2. **The Knowing** — She names what she has been perceiving. Quan's role: not to teach her, but to confirm she is not alone in seeing it. The romantic tension begins here — recognition before desire.
3. **The Wrong Move** — She tries to act on what she perceives without yet understanding the listening-discipline. She speaks (level 3 of Tieng Goi) before she has fully Listened (level 2). The cost is real — to her, to a creature she did not mean to harm, to a Gate she did not know she was destabilizing.
4. **First Contact** — She encounters a Tu Linh directly. Most likely Rua (the cracked Sword Gate's guardian) — the most patient, the most tired. The contact is not transactional. The Tu Linh does not give her power. The Tu Linh shows her how to listen properly. The romance with Quan deepens here as he witnesses her change without trying to redirect it.
5. **The Carrying** — Book One ends with An as a Listening-tier practitioner who knows the next three books are coming. She does not yet bridge. She does not yet save anything. She has changed her ear. The arc continues; the book closes on changed-listening, not on resolution.

(Books Two, Three, Four extend through Speaking-tier, Bridging-tier, and the Ky Lin's emergence at the Crown.)

## Canon guardrails (author compliance notes)

1. **Vietnamese mythology is not metaphor.** The Tu Linh are real entities in the canonical Arcanea cosmos. Rua is a being. Long is a being. They are not symbolic. Treating them as symbolic is a violation of both Arcanean canon and Vietnamese cultural respect.
2. **The ecological grief is real and specific.** The Mekong's salt intrusion, the Central Highlands' deforestation, Hoan Kiem's last turtle dying in 2016 — these are real-world facts. The book does not invent ecological crises; it sees the existing ones through the Mirror Realm's lens. Do not soften.
3. **The Po Yang shamans are real traditions.** The Ede, Jarai, Bahnar, Mnong indigenous communities exist. Their animist practices are theirs. The book engages with them with the reverence due any living religious practice. Sensitivity readers extend to indigenous-Vietnamese readers if the book draws substantially on Highland material.
4. **The romance is in the literary register.** "Romantasy" in the tags refers to the romance subplot's importance, not to the prose's commercial-romance conventions. No inner monologue about Quan's jawline. No fated-mates framing. The romance is recognition between two people who are independently real and whose meeting changes them both.
5. **The Tu Linh do not speak human language.** They communicate in older registers (felt resonance, image, dream). When they "speak" on the page, it is the prose's interpretation of an experience that did not happen in words. Italics, present tense, no quotation marks — locked above.
6. **No translation patronization.** Vietnamese phrases stand. Diacritics carried. The reader who knows enough to read them reads them. The reader who does not feels the music of the unfamiliar without being lectured.
7. **The Lag is 20-30 years.** This is canon-load-bearing. Van Linh is the lagged-mirror, not the simultaneous-mirror. The destruction now in Van Linh is the destruction Earth did a generation ago. This shapes everything about the book's temporal structure.
8. **The Ky Lin is born new in Book 3.** Do not introduce earlier. The Ky Lin's emergence is a Book 3 event, the four-book arc's pivot.
9. **An's awakening is a long arc, not a Book 1 climax.** Book 1 ends with changed listening, not with completed power. The four-book series is the awakening; Book 1 is the cracking that begins it.
10. **The book ends inside Vietnam (or its Mirror).** No translation to a Kingdom of Light Realm. No Academy. No canonical-Arcanean adept arrives to explain. The frame remains inside the dual-world structure.
