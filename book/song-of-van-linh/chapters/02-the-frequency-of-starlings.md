# Chapter Two: The Frequency of Starlings

## Part One: The Morning After

The cub was breathing.

An stood in the doorway of the enclosure at 5:47 a.m., clipboard in hand, and verified this with her eyes and nothing else. She did not go in. She wrote down the time, wrote *respiration normal, posture relaxed, coat dry*, and closed the door.

The drive home on the motorbike was supposed to be recovery time. Twenty minutes of Hanoi waking up around her, the city in its brief early-morning truce with itself: vegetable sellers pushing carts, a woman in a conical hat carrying two bamboo baskets balanced on a pole across her shoulders, the particular golden quality of light before the smog caught up with the day. An had taken this route five hundred times. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting.

She made it four blocks before the parrot stopped her.

Not physically. She was moving when it happened. A pet shop on Hang Bong, cages stacked three high outside the door, the birds inside mostly asleep or muttering. The parrot was green, missing a tail feather, and as An's motorbike idled at the light, something cracked open behind her sternum.

Not sadness. She was clear about this, would remain clear about this, was already formulating the explanation: *proximity, sleep deprivation, elevated cortisol from last night's incident.* What she registered was specific. The parrot did not miss its flock in some vague sentimental way. It missed a particular sound — the sound of rain on large leaves, the kind of sound you only heard outside Hanoi, in places where trees were still big enough to make a roof. The parrot had been taken from a place where it rained on leaves. The parrot had been in this cage for so long that the memory of that sound had become the central geography of its interior life.

An knew this.

She did not know how she knew this.

The light changed and she drove.

She catalogued the rest as symptoms. The stray dogs near the construction site on Doi Can, their anxiety a specific frequency she could map without trying: the machinery was wrong, the vibrations were wrong, there was something buried under the new excavation that smelled wrong to them and they had no language for wrongness except their bodies. The koi in the temple pond on Hang Trong, three of them motionless near the surface — not sick, she checked, she could not stop herself from checking — but something between patience and grief, the particular consciousness of creatures who had outlived every human who had ever fed them. There was an old woman who had come every Thursday for eleven years and had not come in four Thursdays and the koi were waiting with the absolute faith of things that had learned to wait across decades.

An pulled into her alley, killed the engine, sat on the motorbike in the cool shadow between buildings.

She was tired. She had been working a night shift on minimal sleep on top of an emotional incident that had required a level of focused presence she was not trained to sustain. The brain, under those conditions, generated connections. Pattern recognition was the fundamental tool of the scientific mind, and sleep deprivation pushed that tool past its calibration point. She was seeing patterns. She was assigning meaning. This was the neurological literature on temporal lobe sensitivity, on the relationship between sleep states and anomalous perception, on the known and measurable ways grief could open holes in the perceptual apparatus.

She had touched a traumatized animal. She had experienced an empathic response. That was all.

She climbed three flights of stairs to the apartment and did not wake Linh, who was a tangle of dark hair and blankets in the second bedroom. She showered. She slept for four hours. She was back at the facility by noon, in clean clothes, hair properly secured, carrying two iced ca phe sua da because the intern on day shift was twenty-two and running on ambition.

"You look terrible," said Minh, accepting the coffee.

"I look exactly as I have always looked. Report."

The cub had eaten. Not much — a few bites of chicken, hand-fed by Minh, but the important thing was the swallowing. Something in the cub had decided to participate in its own survival. The whole team was pleased in the careful way of people who had learned not to celebrate too early, but still: pleased.

An wrote this down. She asked three precise questions about the feeding protocol. She established that she would not be the cub's primary handler going forward — the attachment dynamic needed management, and she cited three peer-reviewed articles to support this position, all of which were accurate, none of which were why she was making the request.

She was very good at her job for the next six hours.

At 4:15, the starlings came.

A murmuration. Not large — a few hundred birds at most, tight and liquid, shifting across the rectangle of sky visible from the office window. An was at her desk writing up the incident report from the previous night, using precise language and the standard forms, and she heard the birds before she saw them: not their sounds, which were unremarkable, but something underneath the sounds, a kind of — she reached for the word and her hand went still — *mathematics.* Each bird holding its position relative to seven others. Not seven specifically, but the literature said seven, and she could feel the truth of it, the web of relative distances vibrating in her chest like a plucked string. The flock was not thinking. The flock was the thought. Each individual bird was a word and the murmuration was the sentence and the sentence was not in any language she knew but she understood it completely.

She became aware that she had stopped typing.

She became aware that Minh was standing beside her desk.

"You okay?" he said.

"Yes," she said. The word came out exactly as words were supposed to come out. "Tired. Night shift residue."

Minh looked at her for two seconds longer than the question required. He was twenty-two but he was not stupid. "Okay," he said. "The Nguyen report needs to go out by six if you want me to —"

"I'll handle it."

She put on her headphones. She turned on the playlist she used for focused work: high-tempo instrumental, no lyrics, frequencies chosen to stimulate the prefrontal cortex and suppress the wandering mind. She had used it for four years. It worked.

The starlings came through underneath it.

Not the starlings specifically. But whatever it was that the starlings had opened up — it sat below the music like a second track, below comprehensible frequency, and it was not just birds anymore. It was everything. The city breathing. The enormous, layered, exhausted, alive murmur of ten million creatures sharing a delta.

An finished the incident report. She sent it at 5:58. She did not make any factual errors.

She drove home with the music playing through her headphones, which she knew was illegal and did not do ordinarily.

It didn't help.

## Part Two: The Doubling Down

Three days passed. An was functional.

She documented this to herself with the same precision she applied to field notes. *Functional.* She arrived at work. She completed her tasks. She ate. She slept, after a fashion, with headphones on and the volume high enough that there was no room for anything else, which was new behavior that she labeled *temporary adaptive response* and did not examine.

The city was louder now. Not in decibels. In layers. She had always been able to filter it — the neurological default that lets humans move through a sensory world without drowning in it. That filter was no longer fully operational. She caught things at the edge of range: the specific fear of a dog tied to a post outside a pharmacy, not generalized anxiety but the precise dread of a creature that had learned that tied meant abandoned. A rat in the wall of the facility with three young, her attention a bright urgent dot in the general murk of the city's underlife. A fish in someone's tank three floors above her, tracing its circuit with the patient monomania of something that had reduced life to its essential geometry.

She made a list in her phone under the label *anomalous perceptual data* and documented each instance with timestamp, context, and a working hypothesis. The list was fourteen items long by the end of day two. She stopped adding to it on day three because the list was not helping.

She avoided the lake. Went three blocks out of her way on the commute, every trip. The lake was where the old thing breathed. She did not need the data point.

The transfer on the cub had gone through cleanly. Minh was now the primary handler; he had good instincts with traumatized animals, patient and low-affect in exactly the way the situation required. An reviewed the daily reports with appropriate thoroughness and did not go to the enclosure.

On the third day, her mother called.

"Con." Her mother's voice: the specific combination of love and pre-emptive worry that An could identify in the first syllable, the way she'd been able to since she was old enough to understand tone. "How are you? Are you eating?"

"I'm eating. I'm fine. How are you?"

"Your father's knee is bothering him again. He won't see the doctor. You know how he is." A pause during which her mother let the implied comparison — *you know how stubborn the men in this family are, this is also about you* — breathe in the silence. "Ba Noi is having another episode."

An went still. Not the tired kind of still — the other kind, the tell she'd never quite eliminated from her body's vocabulary. "What kind of episode?"

"Talking to the birds again. The staff called me this morning. She's agitated, keeps asking for you. They said she barely ate yesterday." Her mother's voice shifted into the register An recognized as *I am about to suggest something practical as a substitute for something I can't name*. "I'm wondering if we should speak to Dr. Phuong about adjusting the —"

"I'll go see her."

A brief silence. "It's Tuesday."

"I know it's Tuesday."

"You said this weekend —"

"I'll go tomorrow morning."

Her mother made the small sound that meant she was processing a variation from the expected pattern. "She's not always lucid, An. You know how it goes lately. You might drive all the way there and she won't know —"

"She'll know," An said, and did not know why she was certain.

Her mother's voice went careful in the way it went careful when she was deciding whether to say a thing or let it alone. She had grown up in a village outside Hung Yen with a grandmother who spoke to rivers, and she had moved to Hanoi at eighteen and she had built a life on the solidity of what could be seen and measured and taught, and she loved her daughter with a fierceness that expressed itself as protection from precisely the things that had made her mother seem, sometimes, to be slipping away. She chose not to say whatever she had decided not to say.

"Bring her some biscuits," she said instead. "The kind without too much sugar."

"I will."

After she hung up, An sat on the edge of her bed and was quiet for as long as she could tolerate, which was approximately ninety seconds, and then she put the headphones back on.

Linh was home by eight. An heard her in the kitchen, heard the specific sounds of someone else being considerate in a shared space: the soft closing of cabinets, the kettle not allowed to whistle to full volume. Then a knock at An's open door.

An pulled out one earbud.

Linh was leaning in the doorway with the expression she used when she had decided not to say the first three things that had occurred to her. She was a graphic designer and she noticed things the way designers noticed things: composition, weight, the way a person's body held or didn't hold the space it was standing in.

"Banh mi on your desk," she said. "Thit nuong."

"Thank you."

Linh looked at her for a moment. The headphones around An's neck. The untouched tea from this morning, gone cold. An's posture, which was functional and therefore revealing.

"You look like you're fighting something," Linh said. "If it's a person I'll help. If it's something else I'll still help but I'll complain more."

An almost smiled. It reached her eyes for a second before she closed it down. "It's nothing. I'm tired."

"Okay," Linh said. She had the gift of accepting deflection without endorsing it — she would leave, but she would leave in a way that kept the door open. "The tea is the good kind. Actual tea."

She left. An ate half the banh mi and fell asleep sitting upright, headphones on, and didn't dream.

## Part Three: The Dream

The second night, she did.

It was not a normal dream. She understood this in the dream itself, which was unusual — she generally didn't acquire self-awareness until the last few seconds before waking, that brief corridor of almost-consciousness. This dream she walked into wide awake. There was a moment of transition, of descent, and then she was simply there.

Underwater, but not drowning. Light came from somewhere diffuse, green-brown and warm, the color of river water in the late afternoon. She was not at the bottom of anything — there was no bottom visible, and no surface above, just this diffused column of lit water, and in it: a temple. Or what had been a temple. Stone pillars, massive, covered in moss and the fine intricate writing of something that had been inscribed with great care a very long time ago. The script moved. Not blown by current — the water was still — but alive, rearranging itself, sentences folding into new sentences.

She did not try to read it. She looked at it the way she had learned to look at animal behavior before interpretation: pure reception, no conclusion.

The floor between the pillars was not stone. It was water that looked like stone — she stepped onto it and it was solid, but she could see through it, down through layers of water and silt and time, and somewhere far below was something that pulsed. Slow. Patient. Old.

There was a map. She could not find its edges. It was made of currents, not lines — paths of slightly different temperature through the water, routes that glowed faintly blue-white where they intersected. She understood, looking at it, that it was not a map of physical places. It was a map of connections. Lines of relation between things that lived. The whole web of it impossibly intricate, impossibly alive, and she was somewhere in it, a single bright point, and there were other bright points, and the lines between them —

A voice. Not old. Strong and young and unhurried, a voice that had never yet learned what it would lose:

*You were always the one.*

An opened her eyes.

The ceiling of her apartment. 3:12 a.m. by the phone screen.

The room smelled like rain on limestone, and something green, not a leaf-green but a stone-green, the smell of old moss in moving water. She lay in the dark and catalogued this carefully: no rain outside, windows closed, no plants in the bedroom. The smell was specific and real and present. She would not add it to the list in her phone. She was done adding things to the list.

She lay there until the smell faded, which took eleven minutes, and then she went to the kitchen and made tea and did not sleep again.

In the morning, she texted her mother: *Going to see Ba Noi. Be back by noon.*

Her mother replied with three characters: a noodle bowl, a heart, a worry face. This was her mother's entire emotional vocabulary and An found it, in this moment, precise enough.

## Part Four: Ba Noi

The care facility was in a quiet street in Tay Ho, close enough to the lake that you could smell the water some mornings. An had visited every few weeks for the past two years, since Ba Noi had made it clear — with the precision that senility, apparently, could not touch — that she would rather live near water than near her daughter-in-law in Hung Yen, nothing personal, but the river there was dying.

An signed in at the desk and a young nurse named Phuong, who An liked because she spoke to the residents in complete sentences and at normal speed, told her that Ba Noi was in the garden.

"She's having a good morning," Phuong said, which An had learned meant: lucid, present, herself. "She was up before five again. The birds come to her, you know. I've been here two years and I've never seen anything like it. I thought maybe she was hiding seeds." Phuong smiled, equal parts charmed and uncertain. "She isn't."

The garden was small: a square of concrete tiles, raised beds of herbs, a few ornamental trees whose names An had never asked. Two residents in the far corner were playing a slow game of chess. Ba Noi was on a bench near the herbs, her back a gentle, permanent curve, her hands folded in her lap.

The birds.

Three sparrows on the bench back, one on her shoulder. A pair of mynahs in the grass at her feet, attentive, not foraging. A dove on the arm of the bench. An counted seven birds in contact range before she stopped counting.

She had visited this garden twelve times. She had never seen this.

She had seen it without seeing it.

Ba Noi turned before An was close enough that sound should have carried. Her face was small, scored deep, the brown of old wood, and her eyes — this had always been true but An was reading it differently now — her eyes were not confused. Her eyes had never been confused. They were the eyes of someone watching a long, slow thing move toward a moment she had been expecting.

"Con gai," Ba Noi said. *Granddaughter.*

"Ba Noi." An sat on the bench beside her. The sparrows adjusted, shuffled, did not leave. An did not reach out to touch them. "How are you feeling?"

"The same." Ba Noi turned back to the garden. Not dismissive — simply looking at what there was to look at. "You're late."

"I was here three weeks ago."

"I don't mean this visit." Ba Noi's voice was spare the way some landscapes were spare: nothing wasted, nothing absent, everything exactly placed. "I've been waiting for you since Thursday."

An did not say *that's when it happened.* She did not say anything. There was something happening in her chest — not the animal-voices, not the murmuring undercurrent of the city, but something that was specifically this moment, this bench, this old woman with birds on her shoulders, and An had spent fifteen years learning not to cry in relevant situations so she was not going to cry now.

"You should have come sooner," Ba Noi said. "You were making yourself tired for no reason."

"I'm fine."

Ba Noi turned to look at her with the calm patience of someone who has lived long enough that lies are simply data points. She did not say anything. The silence between them held the shape of the disagreement without requiring it to become words.

An was quiet. That was the thing: she was actually quiet. Not performing quiet. Not building the next sentence behind her teeth. She sat in the garden with her grandmother and she listened — not to sound but to Ba Noi herself, the way you'd tune to a frequency — and something in her opened that she had kept shut since she was nine years old.

Ba Noi said: "You touched the cat."

An's voice came out careful. "How did you know that?"

Ba Noi was quiet for a moment. The dove rearranged its wings. "I felt it from here. Something closed for a long time opened up. I've been feeling for it for years. It was always — distant. Like a fire behind glass." She looked down at her hands. "Thursday it was not behind glass."

"That's not possible," An said. She did not say it combatively. She said it the way you said something that you already knew was not quite true, because the saying of it was its own kind of grief.

Ba Noi was silent.

The silence was the answer.

An looked at the sparrow on Ba Noi's shoulder. It was tilting its head with the preoccupied attention of birds at rest, blinking its bead-bright eye, profoundly unimpressed by the enormity of the conversation it was sitting in the middle of. An thought: *what does it hear?* She let herself think it without immediately constructing the refutation.

"You know what you are," Ba Noi said. Not a question.

"I don't," An said honestly.

"Nguoi nghe." Ba Noi said it the way you said something that had a name, a real name, not a label invented for convenience. *A listening person.* "There is no good translation into the formal language. In the dialect of my village they called it —" she used a phrase An didn't recognize, three syllables, a cadence that felt like river water. "It means roughly: the one who the land can speak to."

An kept her voice steady through some work. "You're one too."

"I was." Ba Noi said this without sadness, the way you would say *I used to run* — a fact, not a loss. "It fades with age. The ears get specific. You can still hear the deep things. But the fine things, the individual voices, that goes." She touched the sparrow on her shoulder with one finger, a single slow stroke. The bird closed its eyes. "I haven't been able to hear the birds clearly for ten years. But I remember what they sound like."

"How long have you known? About me?"

Ba Noi considered this. "Since you were seven. You used to tell me what the chickens were thinking. You thought you were making it up." A very small smile. "You weren't."

An's throat was doing something she was monitoring with clinical attention. "I stopped when Mit died. I closed it up. I thought I was —"

"I know." Ba Noi said it quietly. "Your mother thought it would be better. She loves you very much. She was afraid of what happened to me." She paused. "She watched me speaking to things no one else could hear and she thought I was —" she searched for the word with the unhurried patience of someone who did not worry about being exactly right — "falling away. She was trying to keep you with her." Ba Noi turned to look at An directly. "She was right to be afraid. It is not a small thing, being a listening person. It costs."

An said nothing.

Ba Noi said: "You've been calling a river a problem for fifteen years. The river doesn't care what you call it."

Something cracked open in An's chest. Not painfully — or not only painfully. The way something cracks open when what's inside has outgrown the container.

"What is it?" she asked, and she meant several things at once: the gift, the voices, the thing beneath the lake, the map in her dream, all of it, the whole inexplicable shape of what had been happening to her for three days and what had been sleeping in her since she was nine. "What is it that I'm hearing?"

Ba Noi reached into the cloth bag at her feet. She took out a journal.

It was small, hand-bound in dark cloth, the stitching careful and old. The pages had the yellowed density of paper that had absorbed decades of air. She held it in her lap for a moment, looking at it with an expression An could not name — not nostalgia, not pride, something more fundamental. The expression of someone about to let go of what they've been carrying.

She held it out.

An took it. It was heavier than it looked, or not heavier — *denser.* More present than an object that size should be.

"My grandmother gave me this," Ba Noi said. "Her grandmother gave it to her. There were always notes added." She paused. "You will add notes. You will give it to whoever comes next."

"I can't read the —" An opened to a random page. The script was Vietnamese, her grandmother's handwriting, and then midway down the page it shifted into something that was not quite Vietnamese, older shapes, characters she recognized as the base of characters she knew the way you recognized a word's root in a language you hadn't studied. She could not read it. "The old script."

"You'll learn it without studying it," Ba Noi said. "That's how this works. You carry it in your hands long enough and the knowledge seeps in." She watched An turn the pages. "It is not magic. It is just a different kind of science. The kind your kind practices."

An looked up. "The cub. Is it — is what happened with the cub part of this? The thing beneath the lake, the — something old, something I keep feeling from the direction of Hoan Kiem —"

Ba Noi's face changed then. It was subtle and An caught it only because she was, for possibly the first time in the conversation, fully present without armor.

"The river is dying," Ba Noi said. "There is a wound. I have been feeling it grow for twenty years." She stopped. Measured what she was about to say. "The wound is old. It goes deeper than pollution. Deeper than what the scientists measure, though they are measuring the true thing. Something at the core of this land is —" she used a word An had not heard outside of old songs, a word for the process by which a living thing loses coherence, loses its grip on its own pattern. "And the creatures feel it. The birds. The fish. Even the rats." Her voice remained perfectly even; the precision of it was the grief. "You hear them because you are the one who can hear. You hear them more now because the need is larger."

An said: "You said I was the medicine."

"Yes."

"I'm a biologist. I do population surveys. I write reports that no one reads quickly enough."

"Yes," Ba Noi said again, with the same weight. "And now you are also this." She touched An's face. One hand, palm to cheek, the way she had done when An was a child — and An did not pull back, did not construct the professional distance that was her entire learned vocabulary for moments of contact, and the tears came then, not dramatically but with the absolute steadiness of something that has been waiting a long time for permission.

Ba Noi held her face and said nothing. The birds did not move.

An did not know how long they sat like that. Long enough for the shadows to move. Long enough for the garden to settle around them into a different quality of silence, the kind that comes after something important has been said.

Ba Noi let her hand down.

"You've been alone with it for fifteen years," she said. "You don't have to be alone with it anymore."

An wiped her face with the back of her wrist, a child's gesture, and did not apologize for it. She looked at the journal in her lap. She said: "Will you — can you still —" She did not finish the question.

"I'll be here a little longer," Ba Noi said, with neither comfort nor alarm, the calm of someone reporting a weather system. "Come back on Sunday. I'll tell you what I know about the lake."

An nodded. She stayed another hour. They did not talk about important things anymore — Ba Noi asked about her work, not the gift but the biologist's work, and An described the clouded leopard conservation program with more care than she usually brought to that description, because for the first time it seemed to her that these two things were not separate. The birds stayed for a while and then, one by one, moved on to their own affairs.

When An left, Ba Noi was watching them go.

## Part Five: The Night

She sat with the journal at the small table in her apartment where she ate her meals and read papers and occasionally assembled furniture badly. Evening came. Linh was out. The city went through its six o'clock transformation, the particular Hanoi hour when one kind of noise phased into another, traffic thinning, street food vendors thickening, the whole delta beginning to breathe at a different rate.

An opened the journal.

She did not try to read it systematically. That had been her first instinct — systematic, sequential, build a framework — and she set it aside and opened to the middle and looked. Her grandmother's handwriting, compact and even. Observations about birds. Notes on the state of rivers. Dates and names she did not recognize. And then, between two entries written in standard Vietnamese, a page in the older script, the script she could see was base Vietnamese the way Latin was base French but inflected with something else, something older, strokes that had the weight of a different relationship to land.

She had been right that she couldn't read it.

She held the page. She was not reading with her eyes.

Something happened that she would not be able to describe accurately to anyone, so she did not try, even in the notes she would later add to the journal. The closest she got, months later, was this: *like tuning to a frequency. Like the murmuration. The words were not decoded; they were conducted.* The older script did not yield meaning through translation. It yielded something closer to direct experience: the map from her dream, the lines of relation between living things, the pulse beneath the lake. The knowledge was not verbal. It was cellular.

She sat with it for a long time.

Her phone rang.

Her mother's voice came through before An had registered answering, and An knew — in the split second of hearing the flat quality in the first syllable — before the words assembled.

"An." A pause that was its own sentence. "You need to come."

An sat very still.

"Ma," she said.

"She — they said she went in her sleep. Peacefully. Just —" Her mother's voice was doing the thing it did when it was trying to be practical and could not quite manage it. "She didn't — they said she didn't seem to be —"

"I know," An said. "She wasn't in pain."

A longer pause. "How do you know that?"

An looked at the journal in her lap. Through the window, the city was breathing its evening breath, ten million lives in various states of being alive. "I just do," she said. "I'll come tomorrow morning. I'll drive."

"An —"

"I'm okay, Ma. I promise." She meant it, which was new. "Is she — are the birds still there?"

Her mother was quiet for a moment. "They said — the staff said the birds in the garden have been at her window. They won't leave." A soft, uncertain sound. "They're still there. It's been hours."

An did not say anything to this.

"I'll come in the morning," she said again. "I'll drive."

She hung up.

She sat on the floor — not dramatically, she just slid down from the chair because the floor was where she needed to be, which she understood was a pattern she was developing, floor-sitting as a response to enormity — and held the journal against her chest. The journal was warm with the heat of her own body now, but it had been warm before she'd been holding it, she was certain of this, she had been certain since the garden.

Outside, Hanoi continued its living. The rats in the walls moved through their specific architecture of concern. Somewhere above her a cat was performing the precise geometry of a leap from windowsill to table. Three streets away, the koi in the temple pond were doing what the koi had always done, which was to hold the memory of everyone who had ever fed them, patient and specific and loyal in ways humans did not know to expect from fish. And beneath all of it, beneath the motorbike engines and the street vendor calls and the layered human noise of a city that had been a city for a very long time, something breathed in the lake. Something old and specifically alive, its pulse slow and deep as a current, its attention turning — she felt this, she was not going to call it anything other than what it was — its attention turning toward her.

Her grandmother's voice was not in the room. That would have been too easy, too clean, the kind of ending that tied off grief like a suture. What was in the room was less and more than that: a thread. A single continuous thread of knowing, running back through Ba Noi and Ba Noi's grandmother and hers before that, all of them listening persons, all of them carrying this same weight with this same precision, and the thread ran forward too, into whatever An was going to do next, wherever she was going to go, and the weight of the thing had not changed — Ba Noi had been right that it cost, it would always cost — but she was not alone with it anymore.

She opened the journal to a blank page near the back.

She had a pen. She wrote the date. She sat with what to write and then wrote it:

*Ba Noi died tonight. She gave me the journal. I heard what she was trying to tell me. I think I have been hearing it for fifteen years and calling it something else.*

She stopped. She listened. Not fighting it. Not building the framework, not reaching for the explanation. Just listening — to the city, to the creatures in it, to the deep old breathing thing below the water, to the birds at her grandmother's windowsill who had not yet decided to leave.

The whole layered chorus of Hanoi came through. Not as noise. As voices. As the precise, individual, continuous testament of ten million lives and the hundreds of millions of others who shared the delta with them, each one specific, each one speaking in whatever language its body used, rats and koi and sparrows and cats and the enormous ancient patient entity that had been calling her name in a frequency below words since before she was born. She heard it all. She did not try to hold it or sort it or translate it into anything.

She sat in it.

There was a word her grandmother had used, years ago, that An had filed away as old-fashioned and pulled out now as true. *Tinh lang* — the stillness that comes not from the absence of sound but from being at last, completely, without resistance, inside it.

Not silence as the absence of sound. Silence as the absence of fighting what you hear.

She sat in it, and outside her window Hanoi breathed, and her grandmother's voice was woven through it like a thread that had always been there, and she held the journal, and she listened.
