# Chapter One: Subject 7

The pangolin was dying in the way they always died — slowly, curled inward, as if the world had already proven itself unworthy of being faced.

An adjusted the heat lamp two degrees and noted the time. 02:47. Subject 12's respiration had dropped again, shallow pulls of air that barely moved the armored flanks. She'd been monitoring the decline for three nights now. The blood work was clean. The enclosure temperature was optimal. The animal simply did not want to be here.

She wrote in her field log: *S12, Sunda pangolin, female, est. 3 yrs. Continued appetite suppression. No response to enrichment protocol C. Recommend extended observation before escalation.*

Clinical. Precise. The kind of notes that got approving nods from Dr. Hanh at morning briefings.

An did not write: *She keeps pressing her face into the corner where the wall meets the floor, like she's looking for a seam. Like she remembers a burrow.* She did not write that because it was not science. It was projection, anthropomorphism, the cardinal sin of wildlife rehabilitation, and she had committed it so many times in her own head that the guilt had worn smooth, like a river stone she carried in her pocket without thinking.

She checked the temperature again. Still optimal. She moved on.

The Hanoi Wildlife Rescue Center occupied a converted warehouse on the western edge of the city, where the sprawl thinned into something almost rural before remembering itself and thickening again. At night, the building had the quality of a submarine — self-contained, humming with filtration systems and fluorescent light, sealed against the outside world. An liked the night shifts. Fewer people. Fewer conversations. Just the animals and the protocols and the particular kind of silence that isn't silence at all but the layered breathing of forty-three creatures who did not choose to be here.

She moved through the rehabilitation wing with the economical steps of someone who has walked the same route hundreds of times. Checked the two gibbons in enclosure six — both sleeping, the younger one pressed against the older one's side in a way that would, in a peer-reviewed journal, be described as "affiliative proximity" and in any honest language would be called holding on for dear life. Checked the slow loris with the infected eye. Checked the three civets confiscated from a restaurant in Dong Da last Tuesday, still wild-eyed and furious in their quarantine cages.

She paused at the civets. The smallest one was watching her. It had been watching her every time she passed for four days, and she had been pretending not to notice, which was getting harder because the animal's gaze had a quality she recognized but refused to name. She'd seen it in dogs. In the monkeys. In the pangolin pressing her face into the corner.

*I see you,* the look said. *Do you see me?*

An moved on.

---

The motorbike started on the third try, which was optimistic for a 2019 Honda Wave that had seen better decades. An pulled out of the center's gravel lot at 03:20, helmet visor still fogged from the humidity, and turned onto the empty road that would carry her back into the city.

Hanoi at three in the morning was a different creature than Hanoi at three in the afternoon. The daytime city was an organism of collective will — ten million people moving in patterns that looked like chaos but were actually a profound and wordless negotiation, every motorbike a cell in a body that somehow never collided. The nighttime city was the same organism sleeping, and like all sleeping things, it revealed what it hid during the day.

She rode past the remains of a night market on Lac Long Quan, vendors packing their stalls into impossible configurations of tarp and plastic. A woman was hosing down the pavement where a pho stand had been, the water carrying the ghosts of broth and star anise into the gutters. Incense smoke drifted from a pagoda set back from the road, the kind of small neighborhood temple that had survived every war and every modernization by the simple strategy of being too modest to notice. The smoke mixed with exhaust and the jasmine planted along someone's wall and the particular mineral smell of the Red River, still a kilometer away but announcing itself the way rivers do — as a presence, a direction, a low pull in the air that you feel before you understand.

An took the long way home. She always took the long way home. This was not something she examined.

The route carried her through the old French Quarter, where the colonial buildings wore their crumbling plaster like old women wearing last decade's ao dai — faded, dignified, uninterested in your opinion. Past a bia hoi corner where two men sat on child-sized plastic chairs, drinking and saying nothing, their faces lit by a single fluorescent tube that made them look like a painting no one had thought to frame. Past the lake at the center of everything, Hoan Kiem, its water black and still, the Turtle Tower a pale shape in the mist that the city breathed out after midnight.

She stopped at a red light that no one was around to enforce. Waited anyway. A habit her mother had insisted on when An first got her license at eighteen, and which had calcified into something between discipline and superstition. *You don't run red lights, con. Even when no one is watching. Especially when no one is watching.*

The light changed. She rode on.

The bridge over the river gave her three seconds of open sky before the city closed back in. Enough time to see the water below, wide and dark and moving with the kind of patience that made human urgency look like what it was. The Red River did not care about Hanoi. It had been here before the city and would be here after. It carried silt from mountains An had never seen and deposited it in places she would never go, and the whole operation was conducted with such indifference to human schedules that An sometimes thought the river was the most honest thing in the country.

She was thinking this — or something adjacent to this, the kind of half-thought that motorbike rides generate — when she felt it. A flicker. Not a sound, not a sight. Something in her chest, behind the sternum, like a radio catching a frequency between stations. A burst of — what? Not noise. Not feeling. Something older than either.

She tightened her grip on the handlebars and the flicker stopped.

*Tired,* she told herself. *Twelve-hour shift. Low blood sugar. Move on.*

She moved on.

---

The apartment was on the fourth floor of a building in Tay Ho that had been built in the nineties and renovated never. The stairwell smelled like it always smelled — damp concrete, someone's com tam from dinner, the faintly chemical tang of the mosquito coils that Ba Hai on the second floor burned year-round. An climbed the stairs with the automatic competence of a body that has done this drunk, exhausted, heartbroken, and once with a fractured toe, and therefore no longer requires the brain's participation.

She unlocked the door quietly. Failed.

"I can hear you being quiet," Linh said from the couch. "It's louder than when you're just normal."

"Go to sleep."

"I am asleep. This is a dream. In the dream, you look terrible."

An dropped her bag by the door and kicked off her shoes. The apartment was small and dense with living — Linh's design work colonized every horizontal surface, printouts and sketches and three different tablets in various states of charge. The kitchen counter held a rice cooker, a jar of pickled mustard greens, and a half-eaten banh mi that could have been from today or last Tuesday. The ancestral altar on the shelf by the window was small, modern, functional: two photographs, a ceramic incense holder, three tangerines that needed replacing. An's mother called it "minimalist." An's grandmother called it "insulting." An called it what she could manage in forty square meters.

Linh was cross-legged on the couch in an oversized T-shirt that said FUJI ROCK 2024, watching something on her laptop with the focus of a surgeon. Her hair was piled on her head in a configuration that defied physics and three hair clips. She was half Vietnamese, half Chinese, and fully incapable of doing anything at low intensity.

"What are you watching?"

"The Bachelor Vietnam." Linh did not look up. "Do not judge me. I'm deconstructing the editing grammar."

"At three thirty in the morning."

"The patriarchy doesn't sleep. Neither do I." She glanced up. "You look like you got run over by a water buffalo. A slow one."

"Subject 12 isn't eating."

"The pangolin?"

"I don't name them."

"You just did. 'Subject 12' is a name. It's just a bad one." Linh closed the laptop. "Sit down. I made che earlier. It's in the fridge."

An sat. She did not get the che. She sat on the floor with her back against the couch, which was where she always ended up when she was tired enough to stop performing competence, and stared at the water stain on the ceiling that looked like either Italy or a running dog, depending on her mood.

"The new civet watches me," she said.

"Of course it does. You have a face that says 'I will save you but I will not enjoy it.' Animals love that. It's like catnip for traumatized creatures."

"That's not a real thing."

"It's absolutely a real thing. I dated three versions of you before I figured it out." Linh reached down and put her hand on the top of An's head, briefly, the way you'd check if a pot was still warm. "Eat the che. Sleep. The pangolin will be there tomorrow."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

"What, that she'll be there?"

"That she won't."

Linh was quiet for a moment. The apartment ticked and breathed around them — the refrigerator's asthmatic hum, the distant sound of a motorbike on the street below, the particular silence of a city that was already starting to wake up even though it hadn't properly gone to sleep.

"An."

"I'm fine."

"That wasn't what I was going to say."

"Whatever you were going to say, I'm fine before, during, and after it."

"I was going to say there's a cockroach in the bathroom and it's your turn."

An laughed. It was a small laugh, the kind that leaks out before the defenses can catch it, and it surprised her the way it always did, as if her body was reminding her it knew how to do things her mind had decided were unnecessary.

She got up. She dealt with the cockroach. She ate the che — coconut and taro, still good, Linh's mother's recipe. She showered in water that was barely warm and stood in the bathroom afterward, looking at herself in the mirror with the detached assessment she usually reserved for animals in intake. Dark hair loose and wet, hanging past her shoulders. Wide face, high cheekbones — her father's face, or so she was told. The chemical burn scar on her left index finger, from a formaldehyde accident in her second year of university, white and shiny and shaped like a country she couldn't name. Small. Five foot two in the boots she wore to the center, five foot nothing without them.

She looked tired. She looked like she always looked. She went to bed.

---

She dreamed of the dog.

She always dreamed of the dog when she was tired, as if her subconscious had a filing system and "exhaustion" was cross-referenced with "things you have not dealt with." In the dream she was nine and the street was Pham Ngu Lao and the dog was Mit, who was brown and joyful and not smart and perfect, and the motorbike came from the left, which was wrong because in real life it came from the right, but dreams have their own geography.

In real life, she had been holding Mit's leash. In real life, Mit had pulled free because he saw a chicken across the road and Mit's entire philosophy of existence could be summarized as "what if I went over there." In real life, the driver hadn't seen him, or hadn't cared, or hadn't had time, and the sound — the sound was not what people expected. People expected a dramatic sound. What they got was something wet and mechanical, like a problem being solved by the wrong tool.

In real life, An had run to Mit and put her hands on him and that was when the world broke open.

Not metaphorically. Not "she felt sad." Not "she empathized with the animal's pain." The wall between her and Mit — the wall between any two living things, the wall that lets you be you and me be me — dissolved, and she was inside his experience like a hand inside a glove. She felt his pain, yes, but pain was the least of it. She felt his confusion, which was vast and puppy-simple: *this was not how things were a moment ago.* She felt his body's systems shutting down in a sequence that had its own terrible logic. She felt him looking for her, not with his eyes, which were already going, but with something deeper, something that knew her smell and her voice and the specific weight of her hand on his head, and she felt the exact moment when the looking stopped.

She was nine. She screamed. They told her later she screamed for twenty minutes, which she didn't remember, because what she remembered was the silence afterward — the silence that was not the absence of sound but the absence of Mit, a hole in the world shaped exactly like a small brown dog who had wanted to see a chicken.

She told no one what she felt. Not precisely. She cried, and they understood that. She was sad, and they understood that. But when she tried to explain — *I was inside him, I felt him go, I felt the last thing he felt and it wasn't pain, it was looking for me* — the words came out wrong, or the adults heard them wrong, or both, and what they said was: "You loved him very much."

Which was true. But was not what she was saying.

After that, she built the wall. Not deliberately, not with any conscious technique, the way a child doesn't consciously decide to stop touching a hot stove. She simply — closed. The part of her that had opened with Mit, the part that had poured out of her body and into his and felt the whole unfiltered truth of another creature's experience — that part sealed itself behind something she would later learn to call professionalism, scientific objectivity, healthy boundaries. She got good grades. She studied biology. She chose wildlife conservation, which her mother found worrying and her father found baffling and her grandmother found hilarious for reasons she refused to explain. She learned to care about animals in the way that was acceptable: from a distance, with data, through protocols.

She had not named an animal since she was nine.

In the dream, Mit looked at her with the soft stupid trusting eyes of a dog who has never once considered that the world might hurt him, and he said, in a voice that was not a voice: *It's okay to come back.*

She woke up.

---

Sunday. Her day off, theoretically, which meant she would go to the center anyway but feel guilty about it in a different way. She made banh cuon in the kitchen, the rice batter spreading thin and translucent on the steamer cloth, the pork and mushroom filling measured by hand and instinct because Ba Noi's recipe had never been written down. It existed only in An's muscle memory, passed from her grandmother's hands to hers in the kitchen of the old house in Ha Dong, the one they sold when Ba Noi went into care.

Linh appeared at eleven, drawn by the smell like a cartoon character floating on scent lines. "I take back everything I have ever said about you."

"You haven't said anything yet today."

"Preemptive retraction. These are perfect."

They ate at the small table by the window, which looked out over the rooftops of Tay Ho toward the lake. From this height, the city looked almost gentle — the trees along the water, the fishermen who appeared at dawn and disappeared by mid-morning, the slow traffic of a Sunday when even Hanoi relaxed its grip by a fraction. Linh ate with the focused appreciation of someone whose own cooking tended toward the experimental and occasionally the inedible.

"I'm going to see Ba Noi this afternoon," An said.

"How is she?"

"The same. She talks to the birds."

"I mean, that's not the worst hobby. My ngoai noi talks to the stock market. At least birds talk back."

An smiled. It was the kind of joke Linh made to give An permission not to be sad about it, and An appreciated the engineering even as she saw through it. Ba Noi had been in the memory care facility in Cau Giay for two years now. She was comfortable. She was well-treated. She was disappearing, slowly, like a photograph left in sunlight, and the woman who remained bore the same face as the woman who had taught An to cook and told her stories about the river spirits and the forest guardians and the time An's grandmother had spoken to a tiger and convinced it to go around the village instead of through it.

*Fairy tales,* An's mother said, with the particular firmness of a woman who had fought her way to an accounting degree and a middle-class life in Hanoi and did not have time for tigers that listened to grandmothers. *Beautiful fairy tales. But fairy tales.*

An had believed them until she was nine. Then she had stopped believing in anything she couldn't measure.

---

She called her mother on the drive to the care facility. Me picked up on the second ring, which meant she had been waiting.

"Did you eat?"

"I made banh cuon."

"Ba Noi's recipe?"

"Is there another one?"

A pause. "You sound tired."

"I'm fine, Me."

"You always say that. Even when you had that fever in university, forty degrees, you called and said you were fine. I had to hear from Linh that you were in the hospital."

"It wasn't the hospital, it was the campus clinic."

"Nguyen Thuy An."

The full name. The nuclear option. An sighed. "I'm a little tired. Long shifts this week. We got a new intake — clouded leopard cub, orphaned. It's taking a lot of monitoring."

"Is it going to be okay?"

An paused. She thought about Subject 7, the cub, three months old at most, pulled from a collapsed den in Pu Luong after a brush fire. The cub had arrived yesterday morning in a cardboard box that had previously held a Samsung television, and it had been pressed against the corner of the box in a way that An had recognized immediately and tried not to.

"We're doing everything we can," she said, which was what she said when she didn't know.

"Your Ba Noi asked about you."

"She remembers me?"

"She remembers a girl who could hear animals. I told her that was you."

An said nothing. The motorbike carried her past a park where old men were doing tai chi, their movements slow and certain, as if they had made a private agreement with gravity.

"An?"

"I'll tell her you said hi."

"Tell her I'll come next week. And eat more. You're too thin."

"I weigh the same as I did in high school."

"You were too thin in high school. Eat more. I love you."

"I love you too, Me."

She hung up. She rode on.

---

Ba Noi was in the garden when An arrived, sitting in the wheelchair with a blanket across her knees despite the heat, watching the birds that gathered on the railing of the terrace. There were sparrows and a single bulbul and, for reasons that no one at the facility could explain, a spotted dove that appeared every afternoon at precisely two o'clock and left at four.

"Chao Ba," An said, crouching beside the wheelchair.

Ba Noi looked at her. Some days the look was blank, a stranger's polite confusion. Other days there was a flash of something beneath the surface, a fish turning in deep water. Today was a fish day.

"An," Ba Noi said. "The dove is upset."

"Is she?"

"Her nest was disturbed. A cat, maybe. She's been telling me about it." Ba Noi's hand, small and dry and surprisingly strong, found An's. "You look tired, con."

"Everyone keeps telling me that."

"Because it's true. You carry too much. You've always carried too much. Even when you were small."

An sat on the ground beside the wheelchair, Ba Noi's hand still in hers. The dove watched them from the railing with one eye, then the other.

"Ba," An said. "Do you remember the stories you used to tell me? About the river spirits?"

"Those aren't stories."

An smiled. "Me says they are."

"Your mother says a lot of things. She is a very practical woman and I love her very much and she is wrong about several important matters." Ba Noi squeezed An's hand. "The river remembers everything. Every voice, every creature, every prayer. It's been listening since before people had words. Your great-grandmother knew this. Her mother knew this."

"And you?"

Ba Noi was quiet for a long time. The dove preened. The sparrows argued about something.

"I used to hear it," Ba Noi said, very softly. "When I was young. Before I got scared and stopped listening."

An's chest did something — not the flicker from the bridge, not exactly, but a cousin of it. A tightening. A door rattling in a frame.

"What did it sound like?" she asked.

But Ba Noi was looking at the dove again, and the fish had gone back under the water, and when she spoke next it was about the food at the facility, which she felt was oversalted and personally offensive.

An stayed until four. She watched the dove leave at precisely the time Ba Noi had said it would. She kissed her grandmother's forehead and drove back to the center, and she did not think about rivers or listening or the door rattling in the frame, because thinking about those things led to Mit and to a nine-year-old girl screaming on a street in a city that did not stop, and she had built a wall to keep that girl where she belonged.

She went to work.

---

The call came at 11:47 PM.

"Subject 7 isn't eating." Tam's voice on the phone was the particular brand of calm that meant something was wrong. Tam was the night-shift veterinary tech, twenty-two, competent, and operating on the theory that if you said everything in the same flat tone, the bad things would lose their power. "She's refused formula, refused solid food, won't take the syringe. Heart rate's elevated. She's been vocalizing."

"What kind of vocalizing?"

"Distress. High-pitched. Intermittent." A pause. "It sounds bad, chi An."

An was already pulling on her boots. "I'm coming in."

She rode through midnight Hanoi and it was different again — not the sleeping city of three AM but the restless city of almost-midnight, still caught between the day's momentum and the night's surrender. She rode fast. She did not take the long way.

The cub was in the isolation unit, a small enclosure with a heat pad and a nest of towels and a stuffed animal that was standard protocol for orphaned cubs and that always struck An as both necessary and heartbreaking — a simulation of a mother, the way a flight simulator is a simulation of the sky. Close enough to learn from. Not close enough to save you.

Subject 7 was pressed into the corner. She was beautiful in the way that all clouded leopards were beautiful — the rosettes on her coat like smudged thumbprints, the tail as long as her body, the face that managed to look both ancient and infantile. She was also terrified. An could see it in the rigid posture, the ears flat, the eyes too wide. The cub was shaking.

"Okay," An said. "Run me through everything."

Tam ran through everything. Formula at four temperatures. Three different nipple sizes. Solid food cut to specification. The syringe technique they'd used successfully on the macaque last month. Skin-to-skin protocol with gloves. Pheromone diffuser. White noise. Nothing worked.

"Has she urinated?"

"Twice. Both stress-related."

"Defecated?"

"No."

An looked at the chart. Three months old. Weight below the curve. Cortisol likely through the roof, though they wouldn't have results until morning. The cub had been in the collapsed den for three days before the rangers found her. Three days beside her mother's body. An did not think about what that meant in experiential terms. She did not think about what it would be like to be three months old and have the heartbeat that was the entire world go silent and stay silent for three days.

She did not think about it. She got to work.

---

The hours passed the way they pass in a rescue center at night — in increments of hope and adjustment. An tried everything in the protocol and then tried variations of everything in the protocol and then tried combinations that weren't in any protocol but had worked once, anecdotally, in a paper she'd read about hand-rearing Borneo clouded leopards in a facility in Sabah.

Nothing.

The cub refused. Not aggressively — there was no hissing, no swiping. She simply would not. She stayed in her corner, shaking, her eyes tracking An's every movement with an expression that An, in a report, would call "hypervigilant" and, in the privacy of her own mind, would call something else entirely.

By 2 AM, Tam had gone to check on the other animals, and An was alone with Subject 7.

The isolation unit was small and warm and smelled like antiseptic and the particular musk of clouded leopard, which was sweet and musky and nothing like the big cats, more like something between a ferret and a forest after rain. The fluorescent light buzzed. The stuffed animal — a plush dog, absurdly — sat in the corner of the enclosure, untouched.

An sat on the floor outside the enclosure. She was tired in the way that goes past the body and into the infrastructure of the self, the deep fatigue that makes you honest because you don't have the energy to maintain the performance.

"I don't know what to do with you," she said to the cub.

The cub watched her.

"I've tried everything. Every protocol. Every trick. The textbook says you should be eating by now. The textbook says a lot of things."

The cub's shaking slowed, fractionally. Not because she was calmer. Because she was exhausted too.

An watched the cub and the cub watched An and between them was the enclosure wall, which was wire mesh, and also fifteen years of practice at not feeling what she was feeling right now.

"I had a dog," An said. She did not know why she said it. "When I was nine. His name was Mit. He was —" She stopped. Swallowed. "He was the last animal I named."

The cub blinked.

"Something happened when he died. Something I've never told anyone, not properly, not so they understood. I was holding him and I felt —" She pressed her hands flat on the floor. "I felt him. Not metaphorically. I felt what he felt. Every last part of it. And it was —"

She stopped talking because her throat had closed. She sat there, on the floor of the isolation unit, at 2 AM, in a rescue center in Hanoi, and she let the sentence go unfinished, and the cub watched her from the corner with eyes that held no judgment because animals do not judge; they simply see.

An stood up. She opened the enclosure door. She reached in, slowly, with both hands, ignoring every protocol about minimal handling for wild cats in acute stress, ignoring Dr. Hanh's voice in her head and the liability forms and the very sensible rule about not putting your hands near the mouth of a frightened predator, and she picked up Subject 7.

The cub went rigid.

An held her against her chest, one hand supporting the hindquarters, the other cupped around the back of the skull, the way you hold something that is both fragile and capable of destroying you. She could feel the heartbeat, rapid and thin, a hummingbird trapped in a cage of ribs.

"I know," she whispered. "I know."

She did not mean it the way people usually mean it. She did not mean *I understand your situation.* She meant *I know. I know what three days of silence sounds like. I know what it is to be left in the world after the heartbeat stops.*

And then the wall broke.

It did not break the way it had broken when she was nine — not a shattering, not a flood. It broke the way ice breaks in spring, from underneath, from the place where the current has been pressing all winter. A crack, and then a shift, and then the whole structure rearranging itself around a new truth.

She felt the cub.

Not the fur under her hands or the heartbeat against her chest. She felt *the cub.* The inside of her. The burning forest — not as a concept but as a sensory fact, the heat and the smoke and the terrible orange light that turned the night into something wrong. The sound of the mother's breathing changing, the rhythm that had been the entire architecture of the cub's world shifting and then slowing and then stopping, and the three days — the three days that were not days, because a three-month-old leopard does not have the concept of days, only the concept of *before* and *after,* and *before* was warmth and heartbeat and the smell of milk and *after* was silence and cold and the slow hardening of the body that had been everything.

An felt it all. She felt the cub's hunger, which was secondary, almost irrelevant, because hunger is a problem of the body and the cub's problem was deeper than the body. She felt the cub's terror of human hands, which were large and smelled wrong and had pulled her from the only thing she had left, which was the den, which at least still smelled like *before.* She felt the cub's exhaustion, which was the exhaustion of a creature that has been afraid for so long that fear has become the ground she walks on.

And underneath all of it, quiet and persistent and impossible to fake, she felt the cub's desire to live. Not a decision. Not courage. Just the body's dumb, holy insistence on continuing. The lungs that kept pulling air. The heart that kept beating. The cells that kept dividing. Life, arguing with death, not with logic but with sheer, stubborn repetition.

An was crying. She could feel the tears on her face and she could not remember when they started and she did not try to stop them. She held the cub and she cried and it was not professional and it was not clinical and it was not the kind of thing you put in a field report, and she did not care.

"Okay," she said. Her voice was thick and strange. "Okay. I'm here. I'm here."

She sat down with the cub still against her chest. She reached for the formula bottle, the one Tam had prepared, and she held it near the cub's mouth, not forcing, not inserting, just offering. The way you'd offer a hand to someone who has every reason not to take it.

The cub's nose twitched.

A pause. A century of a pause.

The cub latched.

She drank. Not much — a few milliliters, tentative, her eyes still wide and wary. But she drank. The swallowing sounds were small and rhythmic and they were the best thing An had heard in fifteen years.

An held her and the cub drank and the fluorescent light buzzed and the clock on the wall said 2:34 AM, and in the distance, through the walls of the building and the walls of the city, the Red River moved in the dark, carrying silt from mountains and history from centuries, and it did not care about any of this, and it cared about all of it, in the way that rivers do.

---

The cub fell asleep against An's chest at 3:15.

An did not move. She sat on the floor of the isolation unit with her back against the wall and the cub curled in the hollow between her arm and her ribs, and she listened to the breathing, which was slow now, and deep, and even, the breathing of a creature that had, for the first time in days, found something that felt enough like safe.

Her own hands were shaking. Not from the cold. From the aftermath of whatever had just happened, which she was already beginning to explain to herself in terms that did not require the world to be different than she had agreed it was.

*Empathy response. Heightened by sleep deprivation and emotional transference. Mirror neurons. Oxytocin release from physical contact with infant mammal. Completely explicable. Completely normal.*

The explanations were good. They were the kind of explanations that would satisfy Dr. Hanh and her mother and the version of herself that had gotten through university and a career and a life by treating the world as a series of problems with measurable solutions. They were sturdy explanations. They held weight.

They did not explain why she had known about the fire.

She had not been told about a fire. The intake report said "habitat destruction" and "den collapse." It did not say *burning.* It did not mention the specific quality of the light, the orange that turned the forest into a photographic negative of itself. It did not mention three days. The report estimated "24-72 hours" between the mother's death and recovery, which was a range, not a fact, and An had felt the three days as precisely as she had felt her own heartbeat.

*Inference. You read the report. You filled in the details. The brain does this. It's called confabulation. It's well-documented.*

Good. Fine. She noted the explanation and filed it and almost believed it.

The cub sighed in her sleep. The small body shifted, pressed closer.

An closed her eyes.

And heard something.

Not the cub. Not the building. Not the street outside or the motorbikes or the city's sleepless murmur. Something else. Something underneath all of it, the way a bass note is underneath a chord — not separate from the other sounds but deeper, more structural, the frequency that everything else was built on.

It was in the walls. In the pipes. In the foundation of the building, which sat on soil, which sat on stone, which sat on the bones of a city that was a thousand years old, which sat on the bed of a river that was older than language.

It was very far away. It was very close.

It was breathing.

An opened her eyes. The isolation unit was the same. The fluorescent light. The enclosure. The clock. The cub, sleeping. Everything normal. Everything explicable. Everything fine.

But the sound continued, just beneath the threshold of hearing — not a voice, not a word, not anything she could name. A presence. A frequency. The faintest suggestion that the world was paying attention to her in a way it had not, or she had not allowed, for fifteen years.

An sat very still. This was what she did when she was afraid.

The cub breathed against her chest. The building hummed. The city turned in its restless sleep. And underneath it all, patient and ancient and utterly unconcerned with whether she was ready, something waited.

An held the cub and did not move and did not sleep and told herself, repeatedly and with diminishing conviction, that she was fine.
