# What water will not carry

Mera put the page away before its corner could take moisture from the air.

Arion's left hand remained above the nearest basin. Lamplight appeared beneath his wrist. The pale ceiling seam continued through the place his fingers should have occupied. When he drew his hand back, the reflected stone did not change.

At this angle of light, the reflection lacked one hand. Mera had nothing else yet.

Emilia crouched between the first two basins and set a lamp on three chips broken from her plain chalk. She adjusted the lowest chip until the flame travelled across both surfaces without shining directly into Mera's eyes.

The water was mineral-grey where the light entered and colourless at the shallow edges. Neither basin carried dust. A third stood farther along the left wall, cut around a diagonal crack that ran through its lip and down into the floor.

Arion lowered himself against the dry wall opposite it. His left hand found the stone first. He kept the splinted right arm across his ribs and brought his weight down through his left thigh.

“That repair is either very honest or very tired,” he said.

Mera looked at the crooked rim. “Which?”

“They kept the crack and stopped pretending the basin wanted to be square.”

“Approval, then.”

“Conditional.”

Emilia turned the lamp by half a finger. “You have both become unbearable.”

Her repaired yellow cuff had collected a white bar of chalk dust. She noticed it, tried to brush it away, and spread the mark to her elbow.

Mera took the page out again.

The paper was ordinary Athenaeum stock cut down from a practice sheet. No source preparation stiffened it. No custody fiber ran through the pulp. One corner had bent inside her coat during the descent. She smoothed it against her thigh, then stopped before the pressure of her palm made the gesture look like sealing.

“I can compare pressure without touching the water,” she said. “If the boundary carries anything with a body, I stop.”

Arion shifted his left heel until it supported his bent knee. The small movement pulled his shirt against the rib binding.

“How long can you sit there?” Mera asked.

“Longer if I don't answer beautifully.”

“Answer usefully.”

He tested one breath. “Not long enough for you to be thorough.”

Arion's ribs had set their time. Mera placed the page on the dry floor within reach and held her left palm above the nearest basin.

The pale center of her scar faced the water.

At a hand's height she felt only cool air. She lowered until the chill separated into pressures. Some spread evenly beneath the skin. Others tightened toward the scar as if cold thread were being drawn through the old cable burn.

She stopped lowering.

The water remained still. The reflected lamp did not lengthen or dim.

Mera followed the first pressure sideways rather than down. It closed around an irregular interval no larger than two joined fingers. Beyond it, another interval pressed toward her palm at a different depth. Then another. There were too many to count without pretending they belonged to one system.

At the back of her mouth, salt gathered.

She lifted her hand. The pressure released. The salt remained for one breath and faded.

“Retained intervals,” she said.

Emilia moved neither lamp nor chalk. “Observed by you.”

“Yes.”

Mera made no mark yet.

The middle basin stood lower. Its rim reached only halfway up her calf, and one side had sagged with the floor. A shallow overflow cut connected it to the first basin. Beside that cut ran a narrower groove that held no visible water.

Mera moved to the lower edge. Her trousers touched stone darkened by old damp, not present water. She folded the cloth above her ankle and checked the paper behind her. Still dry.

Emilia picked up the lamp. “Angle?”

“Across the groove. Keep the reflection out of my hand.”

Emilia shifted until her shadow covered Mera's wrist while the narrow groove stayed lit. The awkward position pulled the yellow cuff across her burn. She changed hands without comment.

Mera held her palm above the second basin.

Cold drew toward the scar. Salt sharpened behind her teeth.

She moved along the rim one finger's width at a time. Pressure gathered, released, then gathered again. Where the dry groove left the basin, the pull changed direction. The cold no longer tightened inward. It loosened away from the scar in a narrow interval, and some of the salt disappeared.

Mera followed the relief.

The easy motion was downward. Contact would give the pressure depth. A deliberate opening would give sequence. Below that waited the distinction she had wanted since the rain first crossed the harbor awning: accident or removal; silence or voice; coincidence or source.

Her palm lowered by less than the thickness of paper.

Wet cloth cinched under her jaw, cold and heavy. Her next breath caught against it.

Her fingers locked open.

“Stop.”

Emilia took the lamp back. The light left the groove.

Mera lifted her hand. The cloth sensation came with it for half a breath, then remained below while her body withdrew. She sat back on both heels and put her scarred palm against her coat where no water had touched it.

Condensation had gathered along the stone beneath the page.

Emilia slid two fingers under the dry edge and moved the sheet to the higher rim. She did not hold it out to Mera. She left it where Mera could choose to reach.

Arion's left hand flattened against his own thigh.

“What did—” He saw her hand against her throat. “Are you stopped?”

“Yes.”

He nodded once.

Mera breathed until her coat collar felt like cloth again. The constriction had brought no face, word, or name, but it had crossed the boundary she had set.

“That carried more than I intended,” she said. “One bodily sensation. No sequence.”

Emilia rubbed chalk from her fingers. It remained in the creases. “Do you want the page?”

Mera looked at it on the higher rim.

The part of her that had entered the Academy through an engineered absence wanted the next depth. A name could support a petition for review. A name beside three receipt-form impressions would stop every careful person from using *similar geometry* as a polite form of disbelief. A name would let Mera accuse the Athenaeum of suspending the person who found what it preferred not to recognize.

Or it would give her another person's last private room and teach her nothing about permission.

At family meals, a pitcher now stood between Mera's hand and the blue cup that had once been returned wrapped in sailcloth. She had begun reaching around it without thinking.

She waited until her pulse no longer tightened the palm scar.

“My stop is clear,” she said. “I will continue farther from the surface.”

Emilia left the page where it was.

Mera did not lower her hand to the previous height. She began two fingers above it and followed only the change that moved away from her scar.

Cold tightened. Salt gathered.

Then, beside the dry groove, pressure loosened outward and the salt thinned.

The interval had edges.

Blunt at the upper reach. Irregular along the side nearest the wall. Narrow enough that surrounding pressures continued without spilling into it.

Mera had drawn that family of shape from rain condensation under a rope-shop awning. She had found related edges beneath neutral wax around a closed archive pool and across three derivatives that shared coordinates without authenticating a source.

This was not the same shape. The lower edge widened earlier. The irregular side carried two breaks instead of one. A person trying to flatter a conclusion could align them by choosing the correct angle.

Mera changed her angle.

The resemblance weakened, then returned.

Similar. Not identical.

She reached for the page with her right hand and drew the upper interruption first. The line came out heavier than she intended because the stone rim forced her wrist sideways. She left it. A corrected line would hide how the observation had been made.

The outward pressure ran beside the shape rather than through it. Mera followed the loosening cold along the dry groove. Salt diminished once.

In the stone beneath Emilia's lamp lay a worn impression no wider than a fingernail.

The groove crossed it.

Salt diminished again at a second impression, cut in another tool tradition. A third occupied the far side of the middle basin. Each resembled the authority families visible on the Chapter 24 plate. None carried a legible date, name, or complete seal.

Beyond the third, the dry groove entered a bronze-edged slot. A flat stone bar stood across the return channel. Mineral had joined bar and housing at one corner.

The cold tightened toward Mera's scar again. The salt stopped fading.

She lifted her hand.

“Same cut?” she asked Emilia.

Emilia set the lamp close and lay on one side to sight along the groove. Chalk whitened the shoulder of her coat. She traced the air above the line without touching it.

“One continuous groove from the second basin to the bronze slot,” she said. “Three impressions interrupt it. The bar holds the return closed.”

“Academy receipt marks?”

Emilia tilted her head until the yellow cuff almost met her cheek. “They resemble the three traditions on the exterior plate. They are worn, incomplete, and down here.”

“Do you call them receipt marks?”

“It is the answer I have.”

Mera's thumb tightened against the pencil until the cedar pressed a line into her skin.

She bent over the page.

*Recurring absence geometry beside an outward release state. Three Academy receipts. Mechanically retained return.*

She stopped at the end of the first line.

The impressions resembled receipts; resemblance was all she had. No complete seal, date, or name told her who made them or what the state meant.

Mera drew one line through *Academy receipts* and wrote *three receipt-form impressions; visual tradition only* above it.

Below the observation she began a second sentence.

*Request associated with—*

The word had already formed beneath her pencil.

She crossed out *Request*.

In the remaining space she wrote: *Conditional correlation: a similar absence geometry lies beside an outward release state that crosses three unauthenticated receipt-form impressions and ends at retained return.*

The sentence left almost no room at the right margin. She turned the page and crowded the limits down its edge in smaller script: *source and identity unknown; function unverified; no finding of receipt, denial, or personhood; no sample.*

Her final word climbed into the corner she had bent during the descent.

Mera signed her own name beneath the drawing.

Below her name, bare paper remained where an Academy note would have carried a seal and witness designation.

Arion shifted against the wall. His breath shortened before he corrected it.

“Do you need to move?” Mera asked.

“Yes.”

He did not apologize for the answer.

Mera folded the page once with the drawing inside. She did not touch it to the basin or hold it over the surface for condensation. The dry sheet went inside her coat.

“No sample,” she said.

Emilia retrieved the chalk chips from beneath the lamp. “Then we have a route problem and one page.”

“One personal page.”

“Your page,” Emilia said.

She carried the weighted cord spool to the mouth of the short gallery. The floor beyond remained level as far as their lamps reached. Mera stayed beside Arion while he brought his left foot under him and then chose not to stand yet.

Emilia paid out the cord alone.

At the gallery entrance she drew a white line across the stone where the cord touched down. Six paces farther, still within lamplight, she marked a second contact point. She walked no farther. The cord lay with a shallow curve between the marks and a larger loop of slack near her boot.

She returned to the basins, leaving the line on the floor and the weighted bell-board at the first landing behind them.

“Two contacts,” she said. “Slack at both.”

Mera watched Arion's chest until his next breath reached the same depth as the last. His splinted hand had not moved. Emilia stood with the chalk in one hand and the lamp in the other. Mera kept both hands away from the folded page.

No one stepped.

The loop near the gallery mouth shortened.

Cord slid over stone with a dry whisper. Between the two white marks, the shallow curve straightened. The line lifted clear of the nearer contact by the width of a grain.

Behind them, the weighted board rang once.

Emilia looked along the cord.

“Tension changed.”
