# Held for his own safety

Arion pressed two fingers into the mattress seam. The room pressed back.

The force came through warm stone beneath the bed, rose along its left frame, and settled below his ribs where the floor had struck him. He released the seam. The pressure withdrew slowly enough to count.

Four breaths.

He tried the right corner.

“If you dismantle the bed,” the physician said, “we will charge Slate Row for it.”

Arion kept his fingers in place. “The right corner does not answer.”

“Then dismantle that one.”

Physician Anet stood in the open arch with a breakfast tray balanced on one palm. She was broad through the shoulders, grey at both temples, and wore the pale working coat of the Luminary civic wards with its fastening ties visible. Every time she entered, she named what she intended to touch before she touched it. She had already repaired the bandages on his left hand twice because he kept testing surfaces through them.

She set the tray on the table. Warm bread. Pear cut into exact quarters. A bowl of something yellow that might have been porridge before the Academy improved it.

“The left frame is carrying load into me,” Arion said.

“It is reading pressure.”

“A reader does not push back.”

“This one stabilizes.”

“Through what?”

Anet looked at his hand in the mattress seam.

“The ward lattice.”

“Which part?”

“The south brace while you are still. The empty recovery room when you move. The outer buttress if both exceed tolerance.”

“And after the buttress?”

“We stop you before that.”

Arion took his hand away.

The infirmary made danger feel orderly. Sunlight entered through crystal channels in the ceiling and warmed the stone without glare. Every instrument bore a plain label. A volunteer had read him the night's care sequence before dimming the room: water, pulse, resonance check, bandage, sleep. When Arion woke choking on dust that was not there, someone arrived before he could find the call plate and sat beyond arm's reach until the wall stopped answering his breath.

He believed they had helped him.

The door still had no handle on his side.

“I want the terms slate,” he said.

Anet uncovered the porridge. Steam carried a smell of toasted seed.

“You have asked for it nine times.”

“Eight.”

“You asked once while unconscious.”

“That should not count.”

“It was your clearest request.” She slid a folded care sheet from beneath the bread. “The local copy was damaged. The joint council holds the mirrored record.”

“The people who could not tell me how many of them knew how to release the test.”

“Those people.”

“My terms required a complete copy for Slate Row.”

“The ward has submitted that request.”

“Submitted is not sent.”

“Correct.”

Arion stared at the bowl. A hairline chip in the glaze had been filled with dark resin instead of hidden. The repair was clean. He liked it enough to resent the cook.

“I am leaving,” he said.

“Show me.”

Anet moved the breakfast tray out of reach of his feet. She did not call an orderly or close the door. Arion pulled his knees toward him. His chest caught halfway through the motion. Beneath the bandages, the cable burn opened into a hot line across his left palm.

He placed both feet on the floor.

The room changed weight.

Warmth drained from the bed frame into the wall. A low tone sounded above the arch. Across the corridor, a stack of folded blankets shifted against its shelf though no one had touched them. The lintel over the next room flexed down the width of a fingernail.

Arion stopped.

The pressure kept traveling.

“Name the nearest safe point,” Anet said.

He followed the pull through the floor. The empty recovery room had begun to carry his left side. Its outer joint was broad and recently set. The next wall was older. Occupied. He could feel someone moving behind it, each step entering the stone as a small interruption.

“Back to the bed,” he said.

“Can you reach it?”

He had moved less than half a pace. The distance behind his knees felt wider than the measurement ring.

“Push the right corner toward me.”

Anet braced one foot and shifted the bed. The unresponsive corner reached the back of his legs first. Arion lowered himself without placing either hand on the frame.

The blankets across the corridor went still. The lintel lifted.

Anet waited until the wall tone ceased. “The person in that room has three repaired vertebrae and a Foundation graft holding the fourth. They did not agree to test whether your resonance prefers newer work.”

“I did not agree either.”

“I know.”

She set the breakfast within reach again.

Arion's hands shook too badly to tear the bread. Anet placed a short blunt knife beside it and waited.

“You can cut it,” he said.

“Permission to touch the tray is not permission to decide your breakfast.”

“Cut the bread, Physician.”

She did. Two uneven pieces. Her relief at failing to make them equal was almost convincing.

“Who else was hurt?” he asked.

“One recorder has crystal cuts. Master Renn has a burned hand. Emilia has Prism conduction through her right arm. Mera Tidecrest has a cable burn. Akamoto bruised both wrists taking your tool.”

“Slate Row?”

“No new collapse from the hall event. A district crew lowered Seventeen after dawn. The joint hall sent an inspector.”

“From which Academy?”

“All three.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is unfortunately the answer I have.”

Arion chewed the bread. It had a good crust and too little salt. His father would have called that wasteful restraint.

On the back of the folded care sheet, Arion found a pencil sketch he did not remember making. Four walls around an open yard. A public tool rack along one side. Two rooflines crossed in the wrong direction, forcing rain toward the entrance.

He turned the sheet and corrected the roof.

Anet watched without leaning closer. “What is it?”

“A repair house.”

“For hammers?”

“For people who cannot afford the hammers.”

He added a stone-cutting bench beneath the deeper roof and left enough room beside it for a child to watch without entering the tool path. The page had a printed diagram of the ward's morning meal on the other side. His public forge now shared paper with instructions to report nausea.

“The Academy has six civic workshops,” Anet said.

“Do they charge?”

“For material.”

“Then they have six shops.”

Someone outside laughed before disguising it as a cough.

A volunteer entered to collect the empty water pitcher. He wore a yellow sash over ordinary work clothes and had mortar under three fingernails. He paused at Arion's sketch.

“Your south wall is carrying too much roof,” he said.

“It is a first floor.”

“Then your second floor is carrying too much optimism.”

He took the pitcher and left before Arion could ask what crew had trained him.

Arion adjusted the wall anyway.

The room's workmanship remained infuriating. Each pale stone met the next without a lip. The bed brackets could be removed by hand. Light channels opened for cleaning, and the sill carried tiny date marks for every repair. People had built care into the place, then returned to maintain it.

Only the door refused to show its work.

Arion studied the arch while he ate. The left jamb carried ordinary load. The right carried ordinary load until the height of his shoulder, where a thin path turned inward and vanished. Not a hinge. A pin under tension.

“There,” he said.

Anet followed his gaze. “There what?”

“The lock.”

“The door is open.”

“The control is inside the wall.”

“To prevent a confused patient from opening it during treatment.”

“It has no handle.”

“It opens automatically when the ward clears release.”

“And when it does not?”

“Staff use the control.”

“Show me.”

Anet sat in the chair beside the table. Her weight made no difference to the room. “You are medically held because standing moved your load into an occupied wall. I will put my name on that decision. I will not damage ward joinery to make the decision look less like itself.”

“It already looks like an open door.”

“Does seeing a latch change whether you can safely cross it?”

“It changes whether I know who can close it.”

A shadow crossed the sunlight above the arch. Akamoto entered carrying Arion's hammer in a brass evidence bracket.

The ash handle had opened along its old seam. One brass pin remained in place. The other had pulled halfway through the wood, exposing the bright edge of a hole Arion's father had drilled nine years ago at their kitchen table.

Arion set the bread down.

“You brought the bracket,” he said.

“They would not let me bring the hammer.”

“You are holding both.”

“I have found a narrow form of obedience.”

Anet rose. “You have ten minutes before the council call.”

“Then I will require nine.”

She looked at Arion. “Do you want privacy?”

He felt the open seam in the hammer answering the warm stone, faint enough that the distance helped.

“No.”

Anet remained.

Akamoto placed the bracket on the table, beyond Arion's reach but inside his sight.

“Is the house still standing?” Arion asked.

“Seventeen is down. Nineteen and twenty-one are braced. The two outer cracks have stopped widening.”

“My father's workshop?”

“The north wall moved a finger. He says this improved a door he has hated since spring.”

“He wants the hammer returned,” Arion said.

“He wants you returned. The hammer is the argument he believes you will accept.”

“He knows me.”

“Yes.”

Arion looked at the opened handle. “Do not repair it.”

“I was not going to.”

“Do not let the Forge repair it.”

“They have asked twice.”

“Then you have refused twice.”

“I asked them to put both requests in writing.”

Arion picked up the pencil. The point snapped against the care sheet.

“You let the test continue,” he said.

Akamoto examined the broken graphite on the page. “I did.”

“You felt the structure change.”

“I felt the local hall change. I read it as one building with a concealed joint. The mirror made it two structures sharing a control surface. Your terms bound the room we could see.”

“The council knew.”

“Someone did.”

“You should have stopped it.”

“Yes.”

The answer arrived without defense. Arion had prepared himself to strike one.

“Why didn't you?”

“Because the five-minute state was contained, the monitors agreed, and stopping an unknown Foundation transfer without a safe path could have dropped the east wall through you. I chose continued observation while Emilia opened the local release.”

“Her release did nothing.”

“I know that now.”

“Mera knew enough to refuse before any of you.”

“She knew the requested act exceeded her permission. That was sufficient for refusal. It did not tell me how the hall would carry your load.”

The physician placed a fresh pencil beside the broken one.

“What happens at the end of ten minutes?” Arion asked.

“The joint council extends ward observation,” Akamoto said. “Or you accept transfer to the Roost.”

“Home.”

“Slate Row cannot carry an open resonance. Your district has temporary braces, water damage, and people sleeping two walls away from every available bed.”

“This place has people one wall away.”

“This place was built to move load into empty supports. The Roost has isolated practice foundations and fewer remote systems.”

“Fewer?”

“The dining-hall bell reports to no council I have been able to identify.”

Anet rubbed one thumb along the visible tie at her cuff. “The Roost medical court can take the current transfer if the route is cleared. I signed the receiving plan. I will not clear an unsupervised departure or a district return.”

“How long?”

“The medical hold must be renewed each morning,” she said. “Training participation requires a separate answer from you.”

“No measurement.”

“Routine care reads pulse, temperature, breath, and whether the building is moving,” Akamoto said. “Anything beyond that requires a named purpose and a new answer.”

“No remote record.”

“The council requires a hazard status.”

“Then it can receive open, contained, or released. Nothing from inside me.”

Akamoto looked at Anet.

“Medically sufficient,” she said. “Politically unpopular.”

“Manual stop on the transport.”

“There are three,” Akamoto said. “One with me, one with the ward escort, one inside your reach.”

“Visible.”

“Yes.”

Arion turned the care sheet over and wrote the terms around the roof of his repair house. His left hand cramped before the fourth line. He waited until the fingers opened on their own.

“Witnesses,” he said.

“The ward escort and I sign the transfer,” Anet said.

“Mera Tidecrest and Emilia.”

Akamoto's gaze shifted to the arch as if one of them might be standing there.

“Mera's Athenaeum account was ruled incomplete this morning,” Anet said. “Her witness standing is under review. Emilia operated the local instrument and is part of the Forge inquiry. Neither qualifies as independent for the transfer order.”

“I did not call them independent.”

“Then what authority do they have?”

“To see it. To object. To leave. To ask me if I am still choosing it.”

“Mera's refusal is one reason the council petition for continued observation succeeded,” Anet said. “If you name her, they will attach her disputed account to your hazard compact. If you name Emilia, her instrument breach travels with you as well.”

Arion looked at the hammer. The opened seam showed the pale interior of the ash, protected for years until the hall pulled it apart. His father had not hidden the repair. Time and handling had darkened everything around it.

“Will they be told that?” he asked.

“Before they accept,” Akamoto said.

“And if they refuse?”

“The council call begins. The ward hold continues while we find other terms.”

Arion wrote both names.

Emilia's family name remained beyond him. He had heard Renn use it in the hall and lost it under the east wall. He left space after her first name rather than guess.

Anet read the page. “This is a request for participant witnesses, not a medical authorization.”

“Add whatever words preserve that difference.”

She wrote beneath his terms in a hand much smaller than Mera's had been on the slate: *Each witness enters separately; may object, stop observing, leave, or refuse without adverse medical inference; and assumes no control duty by observing.*

“They may still face institutional consequences,” she said.

“Write that too.”

She did.

“Read the transfer,” Arion said.

Anet read every term from the care sheet, including the limits she had added. When she finished, she asked, “Do you accept medical transfer to Akamoto Roost under these terms, conditional on the receiving court and each requested participant accepting separately?”

Arion looked from the opened hammer seam to the roof he had corrected around the writing.

“I accept the transfer,” he said. “I do not accept training or further measurement.”

Anet wrote both sentences without shortening them. Arion signed beneath with the fresh pencil. Akamoto added his name as receiving authority; Anet signed the medical route.

Akamoto removed a small red handle from his coat and placed it beside the care sheet. It was plain metal wrapped in cord, small enough to fit Arion's bandaged grip.

“Transport stop,” he said.

Arion tested its weight without closing his hand. “What does it release?”

“The carriage anchors. The ward support remains until the receiving court takes it.”

“If both supports hold?”

“The carriage does not move.”

“If neither does?”

“We stay here and become unpopular together.”

Anet went to the right jamb of the open arch. She pressed the head of her stylus into a date mark Arion had mistaken for a repair notation. A narrow piece of flawless stone pivoted outward.

Inside the wall, a red manual latch waited beside the hidden pin.

“This releases the door control,” she said. “It does not clear the medical hold.”

“I know.”

She left the panel open.

The call plate above the arch brightened. Six council names appeared, followed by the same vacant line Arion had boxed before the test. Beneath them, Anet entered the accepted medical route and its two signatures. Its status read *conditional transfer approved—participant acceptance pending.* She named Akamoto, Mera Tidecrest, and Emilia, then attached the incomplete Athenaeum account and the Forge incident status in plain text. No warning color disguised which person carried which dispute.

The council call began to sound.

Arion placed the red transport handle on the care sheet, directly below the three names. Across the room, the exposed latch held the infirmary door open in its honest position.
