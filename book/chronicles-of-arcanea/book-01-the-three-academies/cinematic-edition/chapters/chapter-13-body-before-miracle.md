# Body before miracle

The first weight Akamoto handed Arion was the breakfast bowl.

It held cold oat mash, one dried plum, and a crack down the side repaired with two iron stitches. Akamoto set it on Arion's palm at the entrance to the lower service court.

“Name the load,” he said.

“A bad meal.”

“Mass, movement, body.”

Arion shifted the bowl away from the cable burn in his left hand. “Half a stone at most. Center low. It moves when my wrist moves. My ribs object before my arm does.”

“Nearest safe stop?”

“The table behind you.”

“Permission?”

“One Foundation field lesson. No source comparison, no remote state, no resonant-tool contact, ordinary work only. I can stop. You can stop for structural danger. Physician Anet receives entered, stopped, or released.”

“Movement clearance?”

“Reviewed after.”

Akamoto stepped aside. Arion put the bowl on the table and signed the lesson slate beneath the same terms.

The service court occupied the lowest level of the Roost between a retaining wall and a line of roofed stalls. It contained nothing worth singing about. Block piles. Rope. Chalk. Timber with old nails removed and sorted in jars. A handcart whose left wheel complained every third turn. Beyond the outer gate, the road descended toward the city and, eventually, Slate Row.

Arion had been at the Roost nearly a week. He knew the distance between his room and infirmary, infirmary and dining hall, dining hall and three supervised courts. He did not have clearance to cross the outer gate without an escort.

“What earns perimeter movement?” he asked.

“Completing this lesson without becoming part of the equipment.”

“Define complete.”

“You recognize it when we stop.”

“That is not a criterion.”

“It is the current one.”

Arion wrote beneath the lesson terms: *Completion criterion disputed. Participation does not accept discretionary clearance.*

Akamoto read the line and left it there.

Five other students waited beside the block piles. One Arion recognized from the dining hall as the person who stole crisp edges from every loaf and left the centers. He had close-cropped black hair, a narrow face built for disapproval, and a rescue-route manual tucked into the back of his belt.

“Lio,” he said. “I am told you are the reason our morning became remedial masonry.”

“I have not done anything yet.”

“That makes you the least efficient cause here.”

A student beside Lio snorted. She introduced herself as Cass and kept her eyes on Arion's bandages rather than his face. The remaining three gave names he lost while following the wall behind them.

It was a practice wall, shoulder-high and twelve paces long, built from mismatched blocks without mortar. Red chalk enclosed a clear fall lane on its eastern side. White chalk marked six work stations along the west.

“First task,” Akamoto said. “Move the loose block pile to the north platform. Four blocks each trip. No Gate practice.”

Lio lifted five.

“Four,” Akamoto said.

“Rescue examinations grade time.”

“Then fail accurately.”

Lio lowered the fifth block onto one corner. It rocked twice before settling. He watched Akamoto instead of the block.

Arion chose four blocks. Their grain ran in different directions, causing the top pair to slip when he turned. He shifted the load against his chest. The turn dragged his ribs against their binding and emptied half a breath.

At station two, Cass's bottom block began sliding from her forearms.

Arion felt the movement through the court before he saw it. The block's weight entered the compacted ground under Cass's right boot. Without thinking, he compressed the soil beneath the outside edge of her heel and tilted her stance back under the load.

The block stopped slipping.

“Reset,” Akamoto said.

Everyone returned their blocks to the original pile.

“Why?” Lio asked.

“Station two received unrequested support.”

Cass looked down at her boot. “I did not feel anything.”

“That is the problem,” Akamoto said.

“It kept the block off her foot,” Arion said.

“And taught her the stance was safe.”

“It was safe after I corrected it.”

“Were you assigned as part of her stance?”

Arion put his four blocks down harder than required.

They began again.

On the second trip, Lio caught a trailing rope under his heel. Arion planted his left foot. The compacted ground hardened in a narrow line between them and rose beneath Lio's outer heel before the stack could turn. Akamoto saw it.

“Reset.”

On the third, another student loaded the handcart with the dense grain facing outward. Its complaining wheel struck a rut. Arion opened the packed earth by the width of a fingernail so the wheel could pass.

“Reset.”

“At this rate,” Lio said, “we will finish after the wall becomes historical.”

“You are training for rescue work?” Akamoto asked.

“Inter-Academy field license.”

“Then learn the difference between a route that holds and a person secretly holding the route.”

“If the person can hold it, use the person.”

“Ask the person.”

“He is using himself.”

Akamoto looked at Arion.

Arion lifted his blocks again. “I want to finish.”

“That answers why. It does not answer who accepted the risk.”

The next time Cass's stance slipped, Arion let her see it. She dropped the top block onto the marked clear ground, swore at him as if he had moved it, then adjusted her feet.

No one reset the pile.

The work continued.

By the tenth trip, dust had entered Arion's boots and the oat mash had become a cold weight in his stomach. The court gave him a map of small errors he refused to correct. Rope dragging. A wheel striking its rut. Lio's stack tilting every time he turned north because he led with the shoulder carrying the rescue manual.

Lio moved the rescue manual to his other shoulder. The handcart student unloaded two blocks before wrenching the wheel free.

They finished last among the morning groups.

“Second task,” Akamoto said. “Practice wall.”

Lio wiped his forearm across his face, leaving a pale line of dust. “Are we moving it?”

“You are reading it.”

“With or without Gate practice?” Arion asked.

“Foundation attention only. No reinforcement. No transfer.”

The students took the white stations along the west side. Arion stood at station three. The wall looked stable because its blocks leaned against one another in two shallow opposing curves. Its lowest joints held dark tracks from earlier exercises.

Akamoto touched one track with the end of a chalk stick.

“A structure remembers the last direction in which it moved freely,” he said. “Before you trust apparent strength, find that path.”

“Stone does not remember,” Cass said.

“Then call it friction, grain damage, or the laziness of builders. The wall does not care which word gets you out of its way.”

Arion placed two fingers against the block at his station.

The wall carried the day's warmth near its surface. Deeper in, cool pressure followed the joints down and east. One lower block had slid that way many times. Its western edge was rough. Its eastern edge had been polished by movement. If the opposing curve released, the entire wall would fold toward the red lane.

“East,” Arion said.

Others named south, down, nowhere.

Lio crouched near the end block. “East, then north at the last third.”

“Why?” Akamoto asked.

“The end was rebuilt. Its free path turns around the platform.”

Akamoto marked both routes in red chalk. “Clear the lane.”

The students stepped west.

“Now what?” Cass asked.

“Remove the west chock at station three,” Akamoto said.

The chock lay beside Arion's boot.

The polished joint held the wall's weight committed eastward. Pulling the chock would let the lower block travel. The first curve would open, strike the second, and lay the wall inside the red lane.

“I can hold it,” Arion said.

“That is not the task.”

“I can lower each section.”

“That is not the task.”

“Why build a wall to waste it?”

“The blocks are reusable. Your body is also reusable if you stop treating that as permission.”

Lio glanced between them. “If he can lower it without damage, the rescue license would award control marks.”

“This is not the rescue examination.”

“It should be better than one.”

Akamoto handed Arion the clearing bar. “Remove the chock or stop your participation.”

Arion wrapped both hands around the bar. The hammer remained secured in the Roost evidence room, its seam open. The bar was plain iron with rust under the grip and belonged to anyone who signed it out.

“Everyone outside the red line?” he asked.

Each student answered.

“Nearest safe stop?”

“Chock removed, body west, bar released,” Akamoto said.

Arion fitted the bar beneath the block.

He lifted.

The chock came free.

For half a breath, the wall did nothing.

Then the lower block slid east.

Stone struck stone with the exact pitch of Twenty-One's facade striking the lane.

The service court vanished beneath dust.

Arion's father was shouting from a doorway that had no room left around it. Roof weight entered Arion's shoulders. Three homes pulled against one another through his ribs. Someone cried for a ledger. Someone else called him useful.

The clearing bar remained in his hands.

The practice wall moved.

Arion drove his attention into the falling joints. He could still catch the first curve. Foundation opened along his palms, ready to turn his body into the west support the wall had lost.

“Body west,” Akamoto said.

Present dust crossed from right to left. At Slate Row it had fallen straight down.

Arion stepped west.

“Bar released.”

He opened his hands.

The wall folded east along the polished joint. Its final third turned north around the platform exactly as Lio had read. Blocks struck the marked ground in sequence, loud enough to shake dust from the stall roofs, and stopped inside the red line.

No one was there.

Arion counted six students, Akamoto, two kitchen workers at the high door, and himself. He counted again because the first number belonged to the wrong street.

Cass stared at the fallen wall. “He almost caught it.”

“He did not,” Akamoto said.

“The ground changed under him.”

Arion looked down. Grey lines had spread from his boots into the packed earth and ended at the white station mark.

“I stopped,” he said.

Cass met his eyes now. “You are dangerous even when you stop.”

Lio picked up a clearing hook. “The grey reached the edge of your station.”

Akamoto checked each fallen course and marked the blocks safe to lift. “Third task. Sort reusable material by damage and grain.”

Cass moved to the north end. The others chose positions with a little more room between themselves and Arion than before.

Arion wanted to leave. The outer gate stood open for a delivery cart. Beyond it, the road descended through sunlit roofs. No one had placed a guard in the opening because the lesson slate and his own answer were supposed to hold him.

Lio put the other end of a clearing bar into Arion's hands.

“This block first,” he said. “It has a clean corner.”

Arion gripped the bar. Together they rolled the block onto its broad side.

“You read the turn at the last third,” Arion said.

“The old end blocks were stacked beside the north platform. They would rebuild toward the route that made clearing easiest.”

“You inferred it.”

“Rescue work is mostly arriving after the useful evidence has been buried.”

They sorted in silence until Arion's breath matched the present court.

When the reusable pile reached his waist, the folded repair-house sketch slipped from his pocket. Lio caught it before dust covered the open yard.

“This is yours?”

Arion took it back. “Not yet.”

“Which doorway binds?”

Arion pointed to the south yard. “That frame takes the tool floor's weight. If the floor shifts, both leaves pinch.”

Lio turned the page toward himself without touching it. “You need a door designed to break outward. If the tool floor shifts, people push once and the frame leaves with them.”

“Then weather enters through the first warped joint.”

“Visible pin. Replace it each winter.”

Arion found the fresh pencil Physician Anet had given him, worn down now to half its length. He drew a door at the yard's south corner. Lio added the arc of its failure path in chalk, careful not to cover the procurement note from North Quarry.

“Test it with full weight,” Lio said. “If it survives, you built it wrong.”

Across the court, Cass watched them and did not come closer.

Akamoto updated the movement slate. *Perimeter clearance unchanged. Local stop completed after open response.*

He placed it beside the sketch without asking Arion to sign immediately.

Fallen practice blocks surrounded the page. On it, Lio's chalk door broke outward into an empty, marked path.
