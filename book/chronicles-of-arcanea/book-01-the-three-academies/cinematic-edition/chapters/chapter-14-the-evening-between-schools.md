# The evening between schools

The Roost had repaired Emilia's coat where everyone could see it.

Yellow thread crossed the dark cuff in a diagonal too bright to mistake for decoration. The patch beneath it pulled when she bent her wrist, proving that the cloth now carried strain around the Prism burn instead of across it. The repair offended every continuous line she had ever drawn.

It also worked.

She set the brass exit witness on Akamoto's desk with the patched arm.

Green, red, and amber windows faced him. The return spring rested in its neutral position. Tavin's clear wafer showed through the open side of the casing beside Emilia's own maker mark. Renn's denial tag remained tied to the mounting hook.

“Empty fields only,” Emilia said. “Three known states. Mechanical isolation verified by the field operator before each trial. No source comparison. No body reading. No automatic control.”

Akamoto turned the device over without touching its shutter. The exchange evening gathered around them in the upper service court: boots on stairs, cookware striking tables, a string instrument losing the same argument with its lowest note. Roost students carried patched packs and split chairs past Forge apprentices whose tool cases looked prepared for inspection. Athenaeum visitors had already begun attaching source tags to a pile of donated manuals.

“Who is the subject?” he asked.

“The exit the device occupies.”

“Who accepts the risk?”

“I accept the device risk. The operator accepts the field isolation. You retain stop authority for the court.”

“Arion?”

“Is not part of the test.”

“You built it because of what happened to him.”

“That does not make him a component.”

Akamoto put the witness back exactly where she had set it. “Ask him whether he wants this used in his training environment.”

“The lower fields belong to the Roost.”

“His refusal belongs to him.”

Emilia had brought a seven-step protocol, three control faults, two spare springs, and a written answer to every question Akamoto had asked except that one.

“Where is he?”

“Working until the exchange begins. Leave the device here.”

Emilia glanced at the open court behind him. “Chain of custody?”

“You distrust us.”

“I label variables.”

He slid a blank custody tag across the desk.

She entered the time, drew the device's three-window face, and signed the knot after he tied it through the mounting hook. Akamoto added his mark beneath hers.

“Now we distrust each other legibly,” Emilia said.

“Improvement.”

The exchange occupied every dry surface above the lower courts. One stall repaired boot soles in return for onions. Another accepted cracked bowls and refused payment if the owner agreed to keep the repair visible. A Forge apprentice sharpened kitchen knives beside a Roost student teaching three children how to coil a rescue rope without trapping a hand. On the western wall, repair tags hung from pegs by school, material, and urgency. The unclaimed peg had the most work.

Mera stood behind a copper tea heater with both sleeves folded above her wrists.

The heater resembled an overfed kettle mounted on four short legs. Scale furred its lower seam. Three different alloys patched its belly. Someone had scratched instructions, names, serving counts, weather complaints, and one anatomically ambitious bird into the copper.

“The stall owner says it spits boiling water when the north wind changes,” Mera said.

“That is not a fault description.”

“It is attributed observation. You may improve the category after you have observed it.”

Emilia set down her tool roll. “Who authorized disassembly?”

The food-stall owner lifted one hand from a table six paces away. “Anything but cutting the names out.”

“May we copy them?” Emilia asked.

The owner stopped arranging cups. “Why?”

“Repair history.”

“The repairs, yes. The names stay there.”

Mera had already placed an empty slate beside the heater. Its heading read *physical interventions*. No owner column, no transcription field.

“You designed that before I arrived,” Emilia said.

“I listened before you arrived.”

Emilia crouched beside the heater. A dent near the return tube held an overlapping set of marks: a brazing date, two initials joined by a line, and *half-turn for N.* Below them, a later hand had added *not after rain*. The copper around the valve carried five arcs worn by repeated adjustment.

“If we omit settings, the next repair repeats the failure.”

“If we copy the full note, the next repair receives a relationship the owner declined to share.”

“A valve does not have a private life.”

“Its owners do.”

Emilia removed the outer collar. Mineral scale fractured under the wrench and fell across her knuckles. The return tube had been narrowed intentionally with a hammered insert. It was either a crude pressure control or the remains of a bad repair. The scratched half-turn pointed toward it.

Mera held a cup beneath the drain and opened it. A ribbon of dark water entered the vessel, hot enough to send condensation over her fingers.

“May I read only the pressure change?” she asked the owner.

“Not who drank from it.”

“Agreed.”

Mera closed her hand around the cup. The water's surface rose at one edge, fell at the other, then settled with a faint tremor each time Emilia moved the return tube.

“The restriction interrupts a pressure pulse from the lower chamber,” Mera said. “I observed the interval. I did not access an image or voice.”

Emilia entered *hammered restriction; pulse reduced* on the physical-interventions slate.

“And the valve setting?”

Mera looked at the joined initials. “Not copied.”

“Then we infer it again in ten years.”

“Or we record the new setting we establish now.”

They cleaned the return tube, replaced its leather seat, and left the hammered insert in place until they could test its purpose. Emilia calibrated the valve by present pressure rather than the old scratched instruction. Mera filled the tank from a covered pitcher and marked the water line with chalk.

The heater warmed quietly.

For twenty-three breaths, it behaved better than anything repaired during an exchange evening had a right to behave.

Then the return tube knocked once.

Mera reached for the valve. Emilia reached for the lower vent. They saw each other's hands and stopped.

“Valve,” Mera said.

“Vent first.”

The heater spat a clean arc of boiling tea into an empty wash basin.

The basin had been placed there by the stall owner before either of them began.

Around them, three Roost students applauded without looking up from their repairs.

Emilia closed the vent. Mera turned the valve to the worn half-turn mark. The knocking stopped.

“Present observation,” Emilia said.

“Now attributable to us.”

They entered the setting without copying the initials.

The ruined tea smelled of scorched leaf and copper. The stall owner poured it into two chipped cups anyway.

“First pot belongs to the repairers,” she said.

Emilia tasted it. Every bitter part arrived at once.

Mera took a second drink.

“You cannot like that,” Emilia said.

“I prefer tea after it has given up persuading me.”

“You drink punishment for flavor.”

Mera watched Emilia add pear syrup until the surface became reflective. “You drink preserves with a tea problem.”

Emilia added another spoonful. Pear syrup drew a slow spiral across the black tea.

Mera's next laugh tipped tea over her thumb. Emilia looked at the anatomically ambitious bird scratched into the heater's copper belly, inhaled syrup, and coughed into her yellow cuff until Mera pushed the wash basin toward her.

The stall owner reclaimed the cups before either of them could make the tea worse.

“Tomorrow,” Mera said, wiping condensation from the physical-interventions slate, “someone will ask why the original half-turn note was not copied.”

“We will say the current setting was established under test.”

“And if the old note contained a condition we did not reproduce?”

“Then the record remains incomplete.” Emilia tied the slate to the heater.

Mera adjusted the knot so it did not cover the joined initials. “I can sign that word.”

Lio arrived carrying a rescue-route board under one arm and six volunteer slips in his mouth.

He removed the slips. “You are both competent and present.”

“That greeting usually precedes unpaid labor,” Emilia said.

“Short-route rescue trial tomorrow. Weighted casualty, divided access, timed second team. I need one observer who can distinguish a delayed route from a collapsed one, and one person who will argue with the scoring rubric until it improves.”

Mera took the observer slip. “Which am I?”

“I am recruiting for outcomes, not methods.”

His attention moved to Emilia's empty custody cord. “Did you bring the window device?”

“Exit witness.”

“Can it mark whether a rescue path remains open?”

“It can mark whether the path through its own position remains physically continuous.”

“Useful distinction after the ceiling chooses a new position.”

“Uncertified distinction.”

“Most ceilings are.”

Akamoto called Lio's name from across the court.

Lio put a volunteer slip beside Emilia's tool roll and left before she could return it.

Arion came through the lower stair carrying a crate of cleaned clearing hooks with Cass at the other end. He wore no school coat. Dust still pale from the morning wall marked his trousers below the knees. They put the crate beneath the rescue peg. Cass left for the boot-repair stall without looking toward Emilia.

Arion saw Mera first. His attention moved to the sealed vial in its red-threaded external cradle at her belt, then the heater slate, then Emilia's yellow cuff. He tested the edge of the worktable before resting one hand on it.

“The patch is visible,” he said.

“I have received several reports.”

“Does it work?”

Emilia raised her arm until the cuff pulled around the burn. “Infuriatingly.”

He nodded as if this qualified as a familiar building material.

The volunteer slip stayed beneath her tool roll. Emilia set both hands on the table where he could see them.

“I built an exit witness after the Forge practicum,” she said. “I would like to test it on three empty Roost fields. It will not attach to you. It will not read you. Akamoto asked me to seek your acceptance before using it in your training environment.”

“What does it store?”

“Nothing. State and time are written by observers.”

“What does green mean?”

“Both external mounting anchors are joined by a continuous physical exit through the position occupied by the witness.”

“Red?”

“Its own shutter blocks that exit.”

“Amber?”

“It cannot verify itself.”

“What color means I am safe?”

“None.”

“Dangerous?”

“None.”

He looked toward Akamoto's office, where the brass face sat out of view.

“If it changes when I enter the court?”

“We stop. The record says uncontrolled instrument response. It says nothing about your state.”

Mera tapped the blank observer column on her slate. “All entries visible to everyone present?”

“Yes,” Emilia said.

Arion loosened his left hand from the table edge. “I accept that test. I do not accept it for tomorrow's rescue exercise.”

“I did not ask for tomorrow.”

Lio folded the unused volunteer slip over the edge of his route board until the paper held a sharp white crease.

Akamoto returned the exit witness to Emilia after checking both custody marks. He assigned Nemi, the evening field operator, to the test and closed the lower courts to everyone else. Mera entered state and time. Arion chose the upper boundary, beyond the isolation line. Lio remained beside him with his route board. Akamoto stood where he could see the operator's hands, Emilia's tool, and both exits.

The first field used a waist-high ring of black stone with a removable opening on the west side. Nemi disconnected its source, showed the empty couplings, then fitted the exit witness between two anchor pins across the break. Each approved mounting pair enclosed one defined segment of the exit. Green required continuity from one pin, through that segment and the witness, to the other. A break anywhere in that path returned amber unless the witness's own shutter supplied the known red state.

Emilia checked Tavin's wafer, the continuity fork, and the return spring.

“Known open,” Nemi said.

Green filled the first window.

Emilia closed the device's manual shutter. The wafer shifted. Green vanished and a red bar entered the second window.

“Known closed,” Mera said, and wrote the time.

Emilia opened the shutter, then loosened one spring turn. Both colors disappeared. Amber showed through the third slot.

“Unable to verify itself,” Arion said from the boundary.

Mera wrote his exact words and attributed them.

On the second field, Nemi introduced a hidden break beneath one of three non-overlapping segments. The witness showed amber across the first pair of anchors, green across the second, and green across the third. Emilia located the interrupted segment without the device claiming why it was broken or whether anyone should cross it.

Lio drew all three placements on his route board.

“Not for tomorrow,” Arion said.

“The board is mine,” Lio replied.

The third field lay below the oldest retaining wall. Its ring had been closed for repairs long enough that moss filled the carved channel around its northern half. Three surface isolation bars crossed the feed line, each secured by a stamped seal.

Nemi checked the stamps against the field ledger. “Field inactive. Source detached. West, north, and return isolation closed.”

Emilia mounted the witness across the designated exit break.

The green window lit.

“Open control?” she asked.

“Closed control,” Nemi said.

Emilia touched nothing. “Repeat the isolation.”

Nemi tested the west bar, then the north, then the return. Each struck its stop. Akamoto inspected the seals before she moved to the next one.

Green remained.

Emilia closed the witness's own shutter. Red replaced it. She opened the shutter. Green returned. She loosened the spring. Amber. Restored it. Green.

The device knew only its own continuity. Something beyond it was completing the path everyone had closed at the surface.

Mera crouched outside the ring. Condensation from her test cup drifted along the stone toward a hairline seam beneath the retaining wall.

“Pressure moves inward there,” she said. “Observation only.”

Akamoto removed dust from the nearest isolation seal with his thumb. The stamp beneath it carried a maker date six years old. Its wire had not been cut.

He checked the other two. Same wire. Same date.

“No one opens a field,” he said, “until the lower route is mapped.”

Lio lowered his route board. “Tomorrow's trial?”

“Upper course only. Revised access plan before first bell.”

“The lower route is part of my—”

“Revised.”

Lio's mouth closed. The volunteer slips remained pinned beneath his thumb.

Mera had not moved from the boundary. She drew the folded meal story from the document sleeve behind her vial's external cradle. One corner still carried a pale tea ring from the Athenaeum hall.

“I found a recurrence in a Hollow Root service record,” she said.

Arion looked from the story to the sealed witness. “A route?”

“Missing custody coordinates around a restoration interval. Similar geometry to another engineered absence I am studying. The archive would not authenticate a shape-only comparison.”

“Does it map this field?”

“No. I have not compared locations, materials, or dates. This is a continuous buried path. The record describes a living structural field with an incomplete restoration history. They may be unrelated.”

“But you do not think they are.”

Mera held the folded story closed between both hands. “That is an inference. The coordinate copy was not authorized for removal.”

Emilia looked at the observer slate. No coordinate sequence had entered it.

Arion tested the upper boundary stone with his thumb. “Why tell me?”

“Because I accepted participant witness in my own capacity. No Academy assigned me to you. I should have stated that directly.”

Arion looked at the violet line healing across her palm. “The pressure that crossed your hand at intake. Did you read it?”

“No. My deposition marks it unaccessed.”

“Keep it that way.”

“Unless you give me a different answer, I will.”

The lower court stayed quiet except for the field operator checking isolation bars behind them.

“Do you want the coordinates entered?” Mera asked.

Arion looked at the meal story. “Do you?”

“Not without an attributed copy and a citation seal.”

“Then enter that a disputed recurrence exists. Keep the coordinates.”

Mera loosened her grip on the folded story. She wrote: *Participant witness reports an unauthenticated archive recurrence potentially relevant to route mapping. Source coordinates withheld pending lawful citation.* She signed her own name, not the Athenaeum's.

Emilia reached for the witness.

Akamoto put one hand between her and the mounting hook. “Leave it in state.”

“The witness is my property and Tavin's contributed component.”

“It is also the only visible instrument on an uncharted live path.”

“That does not transfer ownership.”

“No.” Akamoto looked to Nemi. “Clear evidence shield. Numbered seal. No contact with the field or witness.”

Nemi brought a thick-walled box without a base and lowered it over the device where it remained mounted. Akamoto threaded seal wire through the box and both anchors. He wrote the number on a fresh custody slate.

Mera read over his hand. “Record Emilia as owner and contributing maker Tavin. Roost as temporary custodian. Access only with the owner or her named maker representative, the field operator, and one independent witness.”

“Add no testing,” Emilia said. “Observation only until we agree a new protocol.”

Akamoto entered both conditions.

“And the lower-field ledger,” Mera said.

“Sealed in the same investigation, separately indexed.”

Emilia checked the number on the wire against the slate. She checked that her maker mark remained visible through the shield. She signed custody, not transfer.

Above them, the exchange continued. Someone had repaired the string instrument's lowest note. The tea heater knocked once, received the correct half-turn from its owner, and settled. Lio began moving his volunteer markers to the upper route.

Nemi extinguished the third court's work lamps. The ring disappeared first, then the moss, then Akamoto's hand on the evidence slate.

Inside the clear locked box, the green window remained visible. Its light reached Emilia's sleeve and turned the yellow repair thread the color of new leaves.
