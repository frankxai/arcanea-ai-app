# What the instrument saw

At four minutes, Arion lost the east wall.

It remained upright. The crystal ribs carried their share, the black braces held, and the water channel continued toward the dock without spilling a drop. But the wall vanished from the structure he could feel. One moment its weight entered the foundation beneath his right foot. The next, that weight traveled somewhere below the hall and returned through the repaired handle of his hammer.

Arion lifted the tool from the floor.

The wall came with it.

Pain crossed both palms under the bandages. The hammer weighed no more than before, yet his shoulder dropped as if he had hooked the whole east gallery through his belt.

“Structural shift,” he said.

Akamoto was already moving. He put one hand against the dark line outside the ring and looked toward Rector Vey. “Clear the east gallery.”

Sol touched the wall speaker. Doors opened beyond the observer stations. The officials did not leave.

“All personnel,” Akamoto said.

“The gallery remains inside tolerance.”

“Its load path changed.”

“The instruments show no displacement.”

Akamoto turned to Arion. “Can you name the wall’s nearest safe stop?”

“It does not have one.”

The answer emptied the nearest observer station faster than any alarm. Two scribes gathered their slates and went through the rear door. Rector Vey began the full evacuation.

The mirror timer continued.

Four minutes, eleven seconds.

Emilia’s relay showed violet along all three rails. “The local field is stable. The mirror has opened Water and Prism comparison. Void-adjacent is receiving without a source label.”

At Venn’s station, the red-wax district basin lit blue. Mera’s sealed vial stayed dark within the amber route.

“There is no authorized Water source,” Mera said.

Steward Venn moved to a control basin at the edge of the gallery. “The public hazard record is authorized under emergency classification.”

“Authorized for custody,” Mera said. “Not comparison.”

“The distinction is under review.”

“The water will not wait for your review.”

Venn’s hand paused above the basin. “That is precisely why I am containing it.”

Arion shifted the hammer. The east wall moved through his shoulder again.

He looked at the timer. “Stop the test.”

The recorder touched the speaker. “The ten-minute baseline remains active.”

“I extended to five.”

“The original consent term states ten minutes.”

“The new answer was five.”

Six council names remained blue on the terminal. Under them, a new line had appeared in small script: *Emergency continuation pending retrospective review*.

Arion’s slate still lay on the floor outside the ring. His five-minute extension was written in the recorder’s hand and signed only by him. No official had countersigned because no one had thought the shorter limit benefited them.

“Four minutes, thirty,” Emilia said.

Arion felt the older structure turn beneath the hall. It did not move like settling stone. A building settled toward available support. This moved around unavailable space, carrying pressure along the edge of something that had been removed and never filled.

The shape reminded him of the empty line beneath the council names.

He set the hammer down again, carefully, and followed the pressure no deeper than one course.

The hall opened through his senses.

Foundation first: bearing surfaces, joints, compression, the small flex that kept rigid things from becoming brittle.

The ring's hum climbed into his molars. One of the anchored chairs knocked against the floor in time with his pulse.

Then Water—not water itself, but pressure retaining the path of everything that had crossed it. The control basin at Venn’s station answered with the district lane, cellar runoff, rain passing through the collapsed facade.

Prism arrived as a difference between two accounts of the same state. Emilia’s relay reported the ring. The mirror reported a pattern wider than the ring. Both readings were exact.

The fourth response did not feel like darkness. It felt like room. The unoccupied interval inside a joint. The necessary space that let one fitted piece release another.

Arion stopped following.

“Close Void comparison,” he said.

The recorder’s voice came from the speaker. “Can you identify a Void response?”

“I can identify the space you opened for one.”

Pens moved behind glass.

“That is not consent,” Mera said.

“It is safety-relevant observation.”

“By a subject who requested closure.”

“Five minutes,” Emilia said.

Arion pulled the hammerhead from the floor.

Nothing released.

The local ring stayed bright around his boots. The east wall held inside his shoulder. Somewhere below, the open field had become a support path for the older structure, and missing load flowed into it.

Akamoto came to the edge of the ring. “Speech?”

“Yes.”

“Consciousness?”

“Unfortunately.”

“Nearest safe stop?”

Arion looked toward the east gallery. The last staff were moving through the far door. One technician remained at the wall terminal, trying to disconnect the observer stations without ending their feeds.

“When that door clears.”

“Can you hold until then?”

The bandages on Arion’s right hand had begun to grey. Fine powder worked through the cloth from the inside.

“Yes.”

“Duration?”

“One minute.”

Akamoto faced the council glass. “Subject continues for evacuation only. No additional comparison authority.”

The recorder marked something. Arion could not see what.

He held.

The technician reached the far door, then turned back for a case of witness glass.

“Leave it,” Rector Vey ordered.

The technician lifted the case anyway.

Arion felt its weight enter the floor. Forty-eight plates in fitted slots, each carrying an account someone had considered too important to trust to memory. The technician braced the case against one hip and stumbled when the east wall shifted through Arion’s arm.

“Put it down,” Arion said.

The technician could not hear him through the glass.

Sol opened the speaker. “Case down. Now.”

The technician looked at the observer stations, at the case, at the exit. He put it down and ran.

The far door closed.

“Release,” Akamoto said.

Arion tried.

He gave the east wall its weight back. The Foundation line refused it. He offered the newer floor, the crystal ribs, the Forge braces. Every path returned through the hammer seam.

“It has made me the joint,” he said.

Akamoto’s face changed. “Break contact.”

“I did.”

“Tool?”

“Not the tool.” Arion could feel the brass pins warm through the handle. “The repair.”

The mirror found that word before the officials did. A bright line appeared on the terminal, mapping the hammer’s broken seam against the missing interval below the hall. Water pressure curved toward it. Prism readings split around it. The empty fourth channel held it open.

The four traces aligned.

The mirror assigned no name. It rendered one irregular gap bearing pressure along four different edges. On the terminal, the hammer's repaired seam repeated in stone, current, reflected light, and empty joint. Each trace narrowed toward the same absent edge.

Every observer station lit.

“Close it,” Arion said.

No one answered.

The recorder stared at the pattern. Rector Vey stared at the wall. Renn moved toward Emilia. Venn lowered both hands into the control basin.

Akamoto stepped across the structural line.

An alarm struck the hall so hard Arion felt it in his knees.

“Do not enter,” he said.

“You no longer have a safe stop.”

“Neither do you.”

Akamoto halted one pace inside the line. The ring pushed against his boots, trying to read him as another support. He did not move farther.

“Then find me a task,” he said.

Arion looked at the hammer. The repaired seam had become a route for everything the hall could not place. His father’s brass pins held. The ash around them was beginning to split again.

“Take the tool when I let go.”

“You said it was not the tool.”

“It is still the thing I am holding.”

Akamoto crouched within reach.

At the control basin, Water rose around Venn’s wrists. The sealed hazard record remained below the surface, red wax intact. Threads of memory pressure lifted from it and turned toward Arion.

Mera left the amber route.

The mark at her wrist flashed. Glass gates closed in the floor channels behind her, one after another. She set her own vial outside the test boundary and reached Venn’s basin.

“You are not authorized for this source,” Venn said.

“Neither is the mirror.”

“If you touch it, you contaminate the district record.”

“If I do not, it becomes his interior record.”

“That is an inference.”

“Look at the pressure.”

Venn did.

The memory threads were no longer returning to the basin. The mirror drew them along the mapped repair seam, converting environmental trace into cross-domain comparison.

Venn’s fingers tightened around the submerged record. “I can close custody.”

“The mirror will call that source loss and preserve the active feed.”

“What do you propose?”

Mera put one hand into the water between the record and the ring. “A discontinuity.”

“You will be inside the evidence.”

“Only as pressure.”

“That was your argument at the quay.”

“It was incomplete there too.”

Venn released one side of the record.

Mera turned her palm. The water did not rise or strike. A narrow interval opened through it, no wider than a thread. Memory pressure met the interval and broke sequence. The mirror’s Water channel filled with white noise.

The control basin alarm changed pitch.

“Water comparison lost,” the recorder said.

“Water comparison refused,” Mera replied.

The red wax on the district record clouded. Its contents remained sealed. Its custody history did not.

Emilia pulled the brass tab.

Her relay opened at the cuff. The three local rails went dark.

The mirror pattern remained.

She looked at Renn. “You said mirrored oversight.”

“The hall has a redundant state feed.”

“Where?”

Renn’s attention moved to the floor behind the instrument bench.

Emilia was there before Arion found the line. She tore open a narrow service panel. Inside, six color-coded conductors ran from the ring to the wall. The local relay connected to three. The mirror occupied the others without a maker’s mark.

“That is not redundancy,” she said. “That is another instrument.”

The council speaker opened. “Do not disrupt the state feed during structural transfer.”

Emilia took a pair of cutters from Renn’s belt.

He caught her wrist. “If you cut blind, you may close the ring with him inside it.”

“Which line?”

Renn looked into the panel. The conductors carried no labels because the remote design treated the entire assembly as one certified path.

“I do not know,” he said.

Emilia’s expression altered less than the relay had when it lied about release.

She put the cutters aside and reached into the panel with her bare fingers.

“Emilia,” Renn said.

“The field states have different heat.”

She touched the first conductor. Amber. The second. Blue. The third made her hand jerk away.

Violet light traveled beneath her skin for an instant.

“Found it.”

She picked up the cutters and severed the line.

The prism channel collapsed.

The cut conductor whipped across the floor. Arion caught it with his bandaged left hand before it struck Mera’s open water. Pain went white to the elbow. The east wall drove downward through his shoulder.

Emilia caught the other end.

“Let go,” she said.

“It will cross the channel.”

Mera closed her wet hand around the cable between them, grounding the last prism charge into the control basin. Water flashed violet, then clear.

Three hands held the same broken line. Mera and Emilia kept memory and current apart. Arion kept it off the floor because it was the only task his body still understood.

The fourth channel remained open.

Its pull found every repaired place he could feel: the brass pins in the hammer, the shifted grain in its handle, the old break beneath Slate Row. The buried load traveled toward him because he was still joined to all of it. Under his left palm, the cut cable bucked. Mera and Emilia had its weight between them now. The hammer remained in his other hand, binding him to the floor.

He looked at Akamoto.

“Take the hammer.”

Akamoto wrapped both hands around the repaired handle.

“On three?” he asked.

“No. When it moves.”

Arion stopped holding the cable. Mera and Emilia kept it above the water between them.

He placed both palms flat against the floor.

The grey lines in his bandages touched the measurement ring. Foundation rushed up through him: walls, ribs, braces, channels, anchors, the old absent space beneath them. He did not offer a better support. He withdrew the one the test had made of him.

The east wall dropped into its own foundations.

Crystal cracked overhead. Forge braces took the lateral strain with a sound like struck bells. Three anchored chairs tore free of the floor and fell on their sides. The control basin split along one edge, spilling neutral water across the stopped ring.

Akamoto pulled the hammer clear as the ash handle opened at the old seam.

Arion released.

For a fraction of a second, the buried structure moved without passing the load through him. He felt a vast unfinished movement under the hall, held open long after every visible support had gone.

Then the floor struck his chest.

The last thing he saw was the mirror terminal saving the pattern under six council names and one vacant line.
