# Terms of measurement

The chair had nine joints and none allowed it to move.

Arion found this by leaning forward, shifting his weight, and reaching beneath the seat while the intake officials argued behind glass. The back flexed just enough to suggest comfort. The legs were anchored through the floor. A brass crosspiece under the cushion prevented anyone from lifting the chair without tools.

It had been built to feel less like restraint than the alternatives.

He stood.

The chair gave a quiet alarm.

Emilia silenced it by pressing the brass release on her prism relay. “It is linked to the intake state.”

“It is furniture.”

“The hall has a generous definition of instrument.”

“Can you unlink it?”

“Yes.” She looked toward the officials. “May I is the slower question.”

Mera remained on the amber water route with her vial held at waist height. The mark on her wrist glowed whenever she stepped near the ring. She had refused to read the district trace and stopped the intake. When he thanked her, she checked the wax seal and walked away.

Arion did not trust her. The refusal had still given him a door.

He moved his hammer from the adjacent chair to his own tool belt. A clerk had protested the first time. The second time, Akamoto entered and the protest ended.

Rain had dried on Akamoto’s field coat in pale lines. He carried three slates bound with red cord and a wrapped parcel that smelled of bread, wet paper, and the smoke from Arion’s father’s workshop.

Arion’s hands began hurting before Akamoto crossed the room.

“From Slate Row,” Akamoto said.

He gave Arion the parcel. Inside were two folded pages from the repair ledger, the heel of bread Arion had left in his satchel, and one brass pin the north crew had found in the cellar dust. His father had written no letter. At the bottom of the second ledger page, beneath the crew assignments, he had added six words in the same square script he used for timber orders.

*Drain packed. Eight jacks. Arch still holding.*

Arion read it again.

The bread had gone damp along one edge. He tore that part off, ate the rest, and put the brass pin in his pocket.

“Who carried this?” he asked.

“District runner to the north relay. Roost rider from there.”

“Name?”

“The runner?”

“Both.”

Akamoto turned one of the bound slates over. Two receipt lines were written on the back. Talia Venn, district runner. Daro Ashfen, Roost rider. Time accepted. Seal condition. Every hand the parcel had crossed.

Arion folded the ledger pages and put them with his repair-house sketch.

“Good,” he said.

Akamoto looked at the bandages. “Can you feel the hall?”

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“That is not a useful quantity.”

“Which parts?”

Arion placed one foot across the nearest dark joint in the floor. The hall answered through the sole of his boot: crystal ribs drawing load toward the high center, Forge braces distributing lateral strain, water channels cut through the surface but isolated from the deeper foundation. Beneath those systems, older stone carried the building in a direction none of the visible architecture acknowledged.

“The intake ring,” he said. “The chair anchors. Three walls. Something below the east channel.”

Mera glanced at the water beside her foot.

“Moving?” Akamoto asked.

“Everything moves.”

“Toward failure.”

“No.” Arion waited for the deep pressure to repeat. “Toward me.”

The officials behind the glass stopped arguing.

Akamoto pulled the empty chair away from Arion and sat in it himself. It alarmed again. Emilia left it sounding.

“Then we need a baseline,” he said.

“You need one.”

“So does everyone above this floor.”

“They were not included in the agreement.”

“There was no agreement. The first intake was suspended before terms.”

“Good.” Arion held out his uninjured forearm. “Write them now.”

Akamoto gave him the top slate.

It already held three columns. The Luminary column required confirmation that Arion could occupy a shared structure without displacing its load. The Athenaeum required clearance that no foreign memory source was using him as a vessel. The Forge required a stable material-state reading under instrument observation. Below them, seven House observers had each marked a smaller concern: control, contamination, bond response, recovery time, public risk, witness integrity, and what one observer had called *unknown purpose*.

Arion tapped that phrase. “Who wrote this?”

No one behind the glass moved.

“It is an observer question,” Akamoto said.

“An observer has a name.”

The speaker opened. A Luminary official said, “House observations are institutionally attributed.”

“Then the institution can sit in the ring.”

Mera lowered her face toward the vial. It took Arion a moment to realize she was hiding a smile.

The official closed the speaker.

Arion turned the slate over and wrote on the blank side. The pencil slipped between his bandages, so he braced it against the wrapped thumb.

1. *Foundation state only.*
2. *No memory channel.*
3. *Hammer in reach.*
4. *Mera Tidecrest present as independent witness; Emilia present as named local operator and participant witness.*
5. *Any witness may request stop. Arion decides whether he can release safely. Akamoto may order release for immediate structural danger.*
6. *Ten minutes. Any extension requires a new answer.*
7. *Slate Row receives the complete test record and a named contact for damage caused by transfer or intake.*

He handed the slate to Akamoto.

“No,” Akamoto said.

“Which line?”

“Five. If you lose speech or consciousness, you do not decide whether you can release.”

“Then Emilia’s instrument decides?”

“No.”

“The council?”

“No.” Akamoto placed the slate on the chair between them. “I order release if you lose speech, consciousness, or the ability to name the nearest safe stop. Any witness can trigger that review.”

Arion’s burned palms pulsed under the cloth. He did not want Akamoto’s judgment added to the agreement. He wanted even less to discover what the hall would do with an empty line.

“Write it,” he said.

Akamoto did.

The speaker opened again. “Ten minutes is insufficient for cross-domain comparison.”

“It is a Foundation baseline,” Arion said.

“The origin signature exceeds Foundation classification.”

“Then you can fail to classify it in ten minutes.”

Emilia covered a cough with the back of her hand.

The officials entered the gallery. Once they had bodies, they became easier to question. Rector Vey could clear the building, Venn the source, Renn the local relay. None could release Arion. That belonged to the remote joint council.

Arion wrote each name and limit on the slate, then looked at the mirrored terminal. “If the council can hear me, put their names on the test before mine goes inside it.”

The terminal remained blank long enough for the chair alarm to become intolerable. Then six names appeared beneath *sitting council*. A seventh line read *vacant—emergency quorum accepted*.

“Six people can place me here,” Arion said. “How many can let me leave?”

No one offered a number.

He drew a box around the vacant line.

Emilia came to stand beside the slate. “The local relay can follow your first term. Foundation state only, no memory.”

“The mirror?”

“It receives local state and compares automatically if something crosses profile.”

“Can you stop it?”

“I can stop my instrument and sever the local line.” Her finger rested on the brass tab. “I cannot erase what has already reached the mirror.”

It was the first answer anyone had given him that became less comforting as it became more precise.

“Why is it here?” he asked.

“Because I signed the intake agreement.”

Master Renn shifted his weight. Emilia did not look at him.

“Did you know it could continue?”

“Yes.”

Mera’s vial touched its carrier with a small glass sound.

Arion studied Emilia’s face. She did not look away or offer the better reasons she must have had. The brass release broke the relay’s smooth case line. Someone had added it late.

“Write that on the slate,” he said.

Emilia wrote: *Local stop does not guarantee remote stop.* She signed beneath.

Mera stepped closer, staying on the amber channel. “Independent witness needs definition. I am already in a custody dispute with this hall.”

“That sounds independent,” Arion said.

“It may also make my testimony easier to dismiss.”

“Will you tell me if they open memory?”

“Yes.”

“Will you stop them?”

“I can refuse my participation. I can break a channel I am authorized to touch. I cannot promise control over what they do.”

He rolled the hammer handle against his palm until one brass pin pressed the old scar there.

“Write it.”

Mera added: *Witness may observe, object, refuse, and act only within direct custody authority.* Her script was small enough to fit without turning the slate.

Rector Vey read all seven terms. “Slate Row cannot receive the complete test record. It may contain protected Academy method and third-party source data.”

“Then send every part that describes what you did to me and what damage followed.”

“Subject data may itself be restricted.”

“Restricted from the subject?”

“From uncontrolled distribution.”

Arion thought of the warden’s wax-covered slate. *Hazardous transfer* written dry before anyone told him. “Send it to my father through the same named route as the repair ledger. He does not distribute anything he has not been paid to carry.”

Akamoto inspected the ceiling while Rector Vey decided whether this was disrespect.

“A subject copy with structural effects and decisions, sealed to the named recipient,” she said at last.

Arion wrote the exact words beside term seven.

Sol, Venn, and Renn initialed only the terms inside their authority. The remote names changed from white to blue. Akamoto kept the final signature while the mirror accepted the new profile.

Arion used the back of a rejected consent form to redraw the repair house.

He sat on the floor rather than trust the anchored chair. The first version of the building had one long workroom, a lending wall, and a central column thick enough to steal useful space from every table. In the new version he divided the roof load between exposed trusses and left the floor open.

Emilia looked over his shoulder. “That joint fails in snow.”

“It is not the final joint.”

“Then why draw it?”

“To see where the final one has to work.”

She crouched, took the pencil before he decided whether to give it, and added a secondary brace in three quick lines. “Now the upper span fails first.”

“That is worse.”

“It is visible.”

Arion studied the brace. “Still worse.”

“Your gratitude needs calibration.”

Mera stayed on the amber route, but the channel bent close enough for her to see the paper. “Why are the tools on an outside wall?”

“So people can borrow them when the workroom is closed.”

“How do you record custody?”

“You write your name.”

“On what?”

“The wall.”

Mera looked at him as if he had proposed storing rain in a net. “What prevents a false name?”

“Nothing.”

“Loss?”

“You replace what disappears.”

“From whose funds?” Emilia asked.

Arion took back the pencil. “I have not built it yet.”

Neither woman laughed. Emilia shifted the secondary brace one inch and returned the pencil. Mera adjusted her carrier so the vial sat farther from the edge of the channel.

“The open floor is good,” Emilia said.

“The custody system is indefensible,” Mera said.

Arion folded the drawing before either could improve it further.

Akamoto signed when the profile turned ready.

He stood from the anchored chair. The alarm stopped.

“Ten minutes,” he said.

The intake ring opened at one side.

Arion stepped into it with the hammer at his belt. Emilia fastened the prism relay over her own forearm rather than his. Mera moved to the witness line outside the ring, her vial centered in its carrier. Akamoto took the nearest position with a clear route to Arion and the door.

The hall lights lowered until the crystal ribs became visible as separate load paths. On the floor, thin lines appeared around Arion’s boots: one circle for the test boundary, another for structural clearance, a third marking the reach of the local instrument.

“State your terms,” the council recorder said.

Arion read them from the slate.

“Do you consent to a ten-minute Foundation-state baseline under those terms?”

“Yes.”

The word left his mouth and entered seven records. He watched each stylus move.

“Begin.”

Nothing happened.

Then the stone beneath his right foot warmed.

Arion tested the surface with his heel. The visible ring had been cut into a newer floor laid over the older foundation. The upper stone invited a downward reading. The deeper structure pulled east, toward the water channel he had felt before.

Emilia’s relay showed amber. “Source detected. Foundation profile.”

“Name the nearest safe stop,” Akamoto said.

“Standing still.”

“Useful work?”

“Listening.”

Arion placed the hammerhead against the floor.

The hall arrived all at once.

Crystal ribs above. Forge braces through the walls. Chair anchors. Water gates. Seven observer stations behind glass. Beneath them, the older stone opened into a shape wider than the building, its load traveling away from the mountain instead of into it.

He did not reach for that shape. He followed the test line down one course and stopped.

“Foundation response stable,” Emilia said.

One minute passed. Then two.

The first change came through the hammer.

The repaired seam warmed under Arion’s bandaged hand. He felt the brass pins, the dark fill his father had pressed into the split, the pressure line carried differently because the handle had once broken. That line continued beyond the tool into the floor.

Then it answered from the amber water channel.

Mera’s head lifted. “My source state is unchanged.”

A second answer came from the prism relay, violet under Emilia’s wrist.

“Outside assigned profile,” she said.

The mirror terminal woke.

The council recorder said, “Emergency comparison authorized.”

Arion looked at the timer. Three minutes and eleven seconds.

“No,” he said. “New answer required.”

“The origin signature has crossed Water and Prism domains.”

“My terms did not.”

Behind the glass, one House observer station filled with a darkness that reflected no hall light. Void-adjacent comparison opened before anyone named it.

Mera saw it. Emilia saw the mirrored feed divide into four channels.

Akamoto moved closer to the ring. “Can you release safely?”

Arion should have said yes.

If he released now, the Foundation field would end. The council would record non-completion under cross-domain response. The warden would receive a hazardous transfer who had failed the only test offered. Slate Row would remain a place he could describe from a distance.

The folded ledger pressed against his ribs when he breathed. *Arch still holding.* His father had fit six words into the only space left on the page. The work was continuing without Arion; a failed classification might keep it that way.

The older stone beneath the hall shifted toward him by the width of a thought.

“I can continue to five minutes,” he said. “Foundation only.”

The recorder marked *subject extension*.

Mera said, “He did not authorize the other channels.”

Emilia reached for the mirror controls. “They are already receiving.”

The third line on the floor brightened and moved inward until it touched the toe of Arion’s boot. The measurement ring contracted around the hammer, the repaired seam held between four converging colors.
