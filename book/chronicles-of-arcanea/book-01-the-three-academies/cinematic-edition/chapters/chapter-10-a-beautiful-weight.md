# A beautiful weight

The model roof had no center post, which was how Instructor Belis got Arion to read the fourth page.

Six thin stone ribs met over an open square no wider than the refectory table. Each carried a different grain. None had been cut to fit perfectly. When Belis pressed one corner, the ribs tightened around the gap and sent the weight into four walls without asking the center to hold anything.

Arion turned the model to inspect the underside.

“No hidden brace,” Belis said.

“I was checking your mortar.”

“There is no mortar.”

“That is why I checked.”

Across the table, Akamoto kept both hands around a cup of bitter Roost tea and pretended not to have brought Arion under witnessed escort for exactly this conversation.

Mera and Emilia had accepted the Roost transfer on separate records three days earlier. Those signatures had moved him between wards. They had not chosen this page.

Belis slid the fourth page closer. *One supervised Foundation exchange. Radial load practice. No element classification. No source comparison. Local state only. Manual release at each station.*

The first page had offered a full Luminary assessment. Arion refused. The second called the assessment an orientation. He refused with the same pencil. The third removed assessment and retained an observational profile. He had drawn a door through the paragraph and returned it.

The fourth page described a roof useful to Slate Row.

“This is one exercise,” Arion said.

“One.”

“Accepting it does not accept training.”

“Correct.”

“No mirror.”

“The hall has no remote feed.” Belis turned the page and showed him the service diagram. Three local monitors, each with a physical stop. “A paper state record goes to the Roost medical court because you are still under daily review. It reports entered, released, or stopped.”

“Who can stop me?”

“You. Me. Headmaster Akamoto for immediate structural danger. Every participant controls their own station.”

“Who can extend?”

“No one. The load ends when the sand weight empties.”

Arion looked beneath the table. A squat glass vessel stood beside Belis's boot, full of dark sand. Plain enough to carry into the exercise. Impossible to extend without everyone seeing it turned over again.

“Why this method?” he asked.

“Because you told the ward physician you want a public repair house. A roof without a central post leaves the floor open for shared tools.”

He glanced at Akamoto.

“Your sketch was attached to the medical sheet,” Akamoto said. “You put transfer terms around the roofline. It became difficult to separate care from architecture.”

“You sent it?”

“Physician Anet did. I objected to the drainage.”

The model's open square would fit a stone saw, two benches, and a clear path for people carrying material. It would also let the city convert a narrow lot instead of buying a full warehouse. Arion could see the work happening there: borrowed hammers returning late, children sorting offcuts by size, his father declaring every donated chisel unsalvageable before repairing it.

He wrote beneath Belis's terms: *Accepted for this exercise only.*

Belis signed. Akamoto added *witnessed departure and return*. Arion signed last.

“Now may I eat?” Belis asked.

The Luminary refectory did not divide students from civic crews. Long tables ran beneath a ceiling patched by seven generations of public-work classes, each repair dated at the edge. Yellow-sashed volunteers ate beside licensed masons, bridge readers, street stewards, and children carrying meal tokens from nearby work yards. No one lowered their voice for the Academy.

Arion had expected ritual. He found arguments about lime.

At the next table, a crew leader stood on a bench and offered half a loaf to anyone who could find his team's repair in the west wall. Six people got up at once. A woman used reflected light. A boy pressed his ear to the stone. Someone threw a pea at a patch above the serving arch and claimed the sound proved everything.

Arion remained seated for three breaths.

Then he went to the wall.

The repair was excellent. The original stone had been cut around a narrow vertical fracture. New material matched its density, temperature, and surface grain. The mortar had been tinted with dust from the removed piece. Arion ran two fingers close to the wall without touching.

“The corner above the arch,” he said.

The crew leader stopped smiling. “Where?”

Arion pointed to an unremarkable span a hand above everyone else's guesses. “You matched the joint. You did not match the load. That piece is carrying toward the kitchen because the old one carried toward the door.”

The leader climbed down, tested the span, and looked offended by his own work.

“Half a loaf,” he said. “No butter. I dislike his method.”

Someone passed Arion the bread. It was still warm at the center.

“Toma loses this contest every week,” a young woman said from the end of the table. “He chooses his own repairs.”

“The city chooses my repairs,” Toma said. “I merely preserve morale.”

The young woman wore work trousers patched at both knees and boots resoled with three different leathers. She shifted a stack of bowls so Arion could sit without moving his ribs too far. Her forearms carried the faint grey lines of long Foundation practice.

“Nara,” she said.

“Arion.”

“We know.”

Toma spread Arion's repair-house sketch beside the food before Arion could stop him. “Belis says this is why he is here. Find what he forgot.”

Four civic workers leaned over it with the concentration of people invited to damage a stranger's hope.

“Drainage,” said the woman with reflected light.

“Already corrected,” Arion said.

“Stone dust collection,” said the boy who had listened to the wall.

Arion added a trap beside the cutting bench.

“Tool tempering costs,” Nara said.

He looked at the rack. Every hammer, chisel, saw, and pin would require maintenance even if the labor were free. The public forge had begun in his mind as a room full of available tools. It now acquired a ledger, a waste channel, a drying cabinet, and someone who understood why a cheap handle cost more after three winters.

“Material access?” he asked.

Toma tore his remaining bread into the stew. “The North Quarry sells nothing small enough for you. They donate offcuts to civic training because transport costs more than disposal.”

“Who receives them?”

“Crews that complete a public exchange and carry a district sponsor.”

Arion looked toward Belis. She was eating pear with a knife, watching without appearing to.

“This exercise counts?”

“If your group completes,” Toma said. “I can sign the district line. Your row needs a material yard more than we need a seventh pile of beautiful mistakes.”

Arion wrote *North Quarry offcuts* beneath the roof. The words changed the sketch. A future he had carried as private punishment acquired an address someone else knew.

He ate the half loaf before the stew arrived.

The exchange hall stood beyond the refectory court. Akamoto walked the route with him and signed both threshold slates. The escort felt excessive until Arion saw the hall roof.

It was the full-sized version of Belis's model.

Twelve pale stone ribs met above a circular floor without column or truss. None touched at the exact center. A small opening admitted sky, rain, and a bright column of air that moved when the roof carried load. The ribs rested on twelve radial wall sections, each aligned to a station marked in the floor.

Belis led the participants around the circle. Nara took station four. Two students wore first-year cords. One was a civic bridge reader older than Arion's father. Another had the smooth palms and nervous attention of someone whose work rarely required stone to answer back.

“The roof is self-supporting at rest,” Belis said. “When the sand begins, the outer braces withdraw in sequence. We meet the changing load at our stations and allow the group to distribute it. No one reaches across the center. No one corrects another person's line. If your local pressure exceeds what you accepted, engage the red brace behind your heel.”

Arion checked. A red iron pedal stood clear of each station. Mechanical. Visible.

“Assignments?” he asked.

“Each station receives a band appropriate to its recorded practice.”

“Show me.”

“Afterward. Anticipating load causes students to hold before it arrives.”

“My terms say no hidden state.”

Belis pointed to the roof. “The state is visible in the stone. The assignment is another participant's training record.”

Akamoto stood outside the circle. He did not answer for Arion.

The roof offered no pressure yet. Nara rolled one foot over the floor grain as if finding familiar ground. The first-year beside her kept glancing at the red pedal.

Arion could refuse and return to the Roost with an untouched sketch. The words *North Quarry offcuts* waited in his pocket.

“Local state only,” he said.

“Local state only,” Belis confirmed.

He stepped onto station seven.

Belis turned the sand vessel.

The first outer brace withdrew with a low stone scrape. Weight entered Arion's boots, light and clean. He placed his palms against the waist-high wall section and felt the roof rib above him settle toward its neighbors.

The second brace released.

Pressure crossed the circle in a slow curve. The bridge reader accepted one portion, changed his stance, and passed the remainder to a student whose station met it at a better angle. No voice called instruction. Each adjustment arrived through warm stone and left through another body.

Arion had never felt a building trust so many people at once.

At Slate Row, every failure had found the nearest willing support and stayed there. Here, the load moved because movement had been designed into the structure. It did not require the strongest person to become a post. The roof flexed by fractions and held its open center.

The third brace withdrew.

His station accepted a diagonal pull from the nervous student and returned it toward the bridge reader. Arion's bruised ribs complained. The pressure did not catch there. It continued.

Across the circle, Nara sank the thickness of one boot sole into the warm floor.

She adjusted her stance. Grey lines deepened along her forearms.

The student beside her carried almost nothing.

Arion followed the roof instead of the person. Three ribs sent their secondary loads toward station four. Nara caught each below the visible surface and divided it through her legs. The first-year's line touched hers, found the stronger route, and emptied into it.

Another brace withdrew.

Nara's second boot sank.

“Station four is carrying three assignments,” Arion said.

Belis walked the outside of the circle, hands close to the manual brace rail. “Remain in local state.”

“Her local state includes their load.”

Nara looked at him. “I have it.”

Nara drew each breath through her teeth, faster now, while the roof held level above her.

“Show the assignment map,” Arion said.

“We cannot expose another student's recorded tolerance during a live exercise.”

“Then equalize it.”

“Equalization now would send full bands into two novice stations before they establish a response. One has a healing wrist. The other has never carried a roof.”

The nervous student stared across the circle. Arion could feel his line harden. Fear made it less able to release, sending more toward Nara.

Belis was right about the immediate load.

Station seven could carry the excess. Arion knew the route. He could take the pull out of Nara's floor, cross it beneath the open center, and hold until the sand emptied.

His palms grew warmer against the wall.

On the nearest safe stop, Akamoto had made him let a house fall. In the measurement hall, three people had held a broken line until he released the support no one should have made of him.

Arion left Nara's load where it was long enough to ask, “Do you want me to take one band?”

Her answer came through clenched teeth. “No.”

“Do you want to stop?”

“I want the map.”

Belis reached the station-four brace. “Nara, I can end your line. The adjacent loads will return to their assigned stations.”

“And fail the group.”

“Possibly.”

Nara kept both feet below the floor plane.

Arion looked at the sand. More than half remained.

He removed his right hand from the wall.

His station alarm gave one warning tone.

“Arion,” Belis said.

He crouched without breaking left-hand contact and took a chalk stub from the edge of his station. Each radial joint carried a different vibration. He marked his line first, drawing from station seven to the roof center. Then the bridge reader's. The nervous student's line bent away from its own station and disappeared into Nara's.

“Return to stance,” Belis ordered.

Arion drew the second novice line. It bent the same way.

The marks darkened where chalk crossed warm stone. Twelve spokes appeared, but five ended at station four.

Everyone in the circle could see them.

“Those are adaptive safety routes,” Belis said. “They prevent overload at unproven stations.”

“They create overload where the record says a person survives it.”

“Stand, or use your release.”

The alarm sounded again. Arion could return his hand and finish. The procurement line remained in his pocket. The roof above him was more useful than any method he had known at home.

He pressed the red pedal behind his heel.

The local brace drove up through station seven. His portion of the roof transferred into fixed stone. Adjacent stations jolted as their paths changed.

Belis moved before the first-year's healing wrist took the return. She opened the outer rail, brought two wall braces back into contact, and called each participant's nearest local support. Nara answered first. The bridge reader second. Around the circle, bodies released into structure without the roof dropping a finger.

The exercise ended with most of the sand still in the upper bulb.

The nervous student kept both palms against the fixed brace until Belis touched his shoulder and told him the load had ended.

The ceiling remained perfect overhead. On the floor, the chalk map showed why.

Belis waited until every participant had stepped clear before approaching Arion.

“You used a manual release within your terms,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You also mapped private training assignments in front of the group after I refused disclosure.”

“I mapped where the roof was going.”

“The route identifies who carries more and who is protected. You knew that.”

“They knew it in their legs.”

Belis looked toward Nara, who was rubbing warmth back into one ankle. “The assignment kept two students below injury tolerance. We did not hide a dangerous load from the person carrying it. Nara's durable band includes that range.”

“Did she know five lines would end there?”

Nara did not look up.

Belis answered. “She accepted adaptive weighting.”

“That is not a number.”

“No. It is a practice method that responds to twelve bodies. The exact route cannot be predicted before live state.”

Belis folded Arion's terms page along its center. “I am removing you from this exchange sequence. Your interruption was safe because I restored the braces. In another hall, the same act could injure the students whose load you intended to reveal.”

“Will the assignment map remain with the record?”

“The local state record includes it.”

“Will the participants receive a copy?”

“They may request their own station data.”

“The whole map.”

“No. That exposes other students' records.”

Toma waited beyond the threshold with the district sponsor form rolled beneath one arm. He had watched the last half of the exercise.

Arion looked at the roll.

“Completion is required,” Toma said. His regret sounded practical rather than kind. “I cannot sign what did not complete.”

He tucked the form into his sash.

The North Quarry line on Arion's sketch became pencil again.

Akamoto signed the departure slate without comment. He did not tell Arion the interruption had been right or wrong. On the walk back through the refectory court, he stopped beside a wall with an invisible repair and waited while Arion failed to admire it.

Nara found them at the outer gate.

She had changed into dry boots. One ankle remained stiff.

“You left your chalk,” she said.

She handed him the stub.

“Will you request the whole map?” Arion asked.

“No.”

“You wanted it.”

“I wanted it in the room. I do not want my name on a complaint about adaptive weighting.”

“Why?”

“Because Belis puts me on civic roofs no other instructor will clear me for. Because the novices needed the route. Because I can carry that band.”

She glanced back at the hall. “And because if I object, they will call the weighting protective and remove me from the next roof for my protection.”

Arion turned the chalk between his bandaged fingers. “I will not say you asked me to stop it.”

“I did not ask you.”

“I know.”

Nara shifted her weight off the stiff ankle. “Thank you for asking before you took it.”

She went back through the gate before he could make the answer useful.

Inside the exchange hall, the ceiling still held its flawless radial curve. The chalk map remained beneath it until the floor cooled, five dark spokes ending where Nara had stood.
