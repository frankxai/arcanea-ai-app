# The exit that was not there

Mera's lamp moved one handspan through the grey water.

Beneath the cord Emilia had laid over the glass, a brass shutter clicked. Her thumbnail found the service screw. She took it away.

“Breath?”

Arion drew one as far as the binding. It stopped, settled, and left him with his jaw tight against the last part of the exhale.

“Same,” he said.

“Hand?”

He looked at the ring finger and smallest finger folded above the splint. “Same.”

His coat still held the padded forearm off the wall. The cord across the nearby floor joint kept its shallow curve between Emilia's two chalk marks. Three shorter marks waited beside his left hip: hammer, west hatch, the seam that had taken Mera's return from her.

Across the water, Mera's altered light narrowed.

The strike against the channel regained its harder note. Emilia could no longer tell which lower shutter had clicked. She had not seen enough to call the motion a relation, let alone a cause.

She put her mouth close to the curtain without entering the spray.

“Mera. The lamp—water only?”

The current broke the answer. Emilia heard *water*, then a torn syllable that might have belonged to anything.

The strike softened again.

“Water only,” Mera called. “Thin now.”

“Do you want one local opening attempt?”

Mera did not answer at once. She gathered the two pages from the ledge and placed them inside one side of her coat. The separate fold already there remained beneath the other lapel. She tucked the pencil beside the first two pages, stepped clear of the dry housing, and raised an empty hand.

“One attempt,” she said through the thinner water. “I call what I see. I keep my stop.”

The water thickened over the last word.

Emilia turned to Arion. “I need someone to read two bearing faces without improving them.”

“Left hand only.”

“If I put a loose part within reach. No stone contact. No load.”

“And no other method.”

“That was already not invited.”

He dipped his chin. The movement pulled his next breath higher. Emilia waited until he found the previous depth before she touched the cord.

She lifted it from the paired chalk marks first.

The joint did not change. Chalk striped the cord twice. As Emilia drew the remaining coils from the glass, she laid her yellow cuff over the upper row before the lamp could resolve its routes. The service screw and side catches remained exposed.

The service screw carried the pale half-moon her thumbnail had left earlier. Its slot was fine enough for the driver locked in the third Forge drawer and too fine for anything she had brought below.

Arion saw where she was looking. “You wanted the correct driver.”

“I still do.”

“You don't have it.”

Emilia turned the lamp guard toward her. A flat brass key sat below the wick collar, warm from the flame. She worked it free with two nails.

“I have a lamp key I am about to ruin.”

The flame stayed at its present height. It would stay there until the key went back or the oil ran out.

The brass blade entered the screw for less than half its width. Emilia pressed, turned, lost the slot, and caught the key before it crossed the glass. A bright burr rose along the screw edge.

“Excellent fit,” Arion said.

“Your contribution is structural.”

“That was structural.”

She tried from a lower angle. The key bowed against her thumb. The screw gave one dry fraction, then another. By the time its threads released, the key had taken a permanent curve.

Emilia put the bent piece beside the lamp. The flame no longer had an adjustment. It burned evenly enough to show the mineral pane lifting against a gasket that had gone dry and grey with age.

She set the pane flat beyond the chalked joint.

Without the pane, the upper carrier showed its side catches. Below it, three lower rods ended in a common yoke weighted by three small counterweights. The bay's sideways shift had exposed the yoke against the floor-slot collar. Emilia touched the crossbar; the collar shifted by less than a grain. She could see how the lower row reached it. Nothing there told her why.

On a level Forge bench, with the fine driver and white felt under every removable piece, Emilia could have taken the carrier out without changing one state. She could have marked the four bent guides, measured the old gasket, and wrapped the kitchen-brass shim separately with the bowl-maker's crescent still visible on its edge. Renn would have disliked her conclusions and admired the disassembly. The carrier might have entered review under her name. One object no one else had understood yet could have put a tool back in her hand before the council decided whether she deserved one.

She slid her fingers beneath the side catches.

The upper carrier lifted as one piece. Emilia did not follow the shutters across it. She turned their faces inward and folded the thin brass until one row struck the other.

The kitchen-made shim fell into her palm.

She put the folded carrier against the square edge of the recess and leaned through her left shoulder. Brass bowed. The forward rods tightened in their sleeves. One snapped. The second tore longer, leaving a pale tongue at the break. The last held until Emilia changed her grip and pulled without looking at the route it had served.

It broke with less sound than the lamp key had made in the screw.

The shutters collapsed across one another. No position remained clean enough to read.

Emilia lowered the ruined carrier to the floor.

Arion looked at it, then at her face. “Gone?”

“The lead is.”

The lower row clicked once. No upper shutter moved before it.

Emilia threaded the full surviving operator-bay length through the common yoke, leaving the glass and chalked joint bare. Only the two white contact marks showed where the cord had crossed.

She raised the yoke by less than a nail. Counterweight tug travelled through the cord and flattened its twist against her palm. The lower rods drew at the floor-slot collar. One current shutter climbed cleanly. Another hesitated between notches. The third tilted across its guide.

Emilia returned the weights to their physical stops.

“One movement,” she said. “Possibly less.”

“Show me the faces.”

She moved the ruined carrier near Arion's left hand without letting it cross the line of his splint. Then she lifted the loosened edge of the floor-slot collar herself. The two bearing surfaces met at an angle. One had worn round. The other still carried a sharp corner where the bay shift had exposed fresh stone.

Arion turned the carrier with his left fingertips. His right side remained on folded cloth.

“It won't close cleanly,” he said.

“It has to move.”

“Then leave the gap.”

“That is not a specification.”

“Mark it.”

Emilia almost told him the Forge did not certify *gap visible from here*. The Forge was several closed walls away, and its proper driver was still lying in felt.

She placed the kitchen-brass shim at the widest point between the two faces. The shallow crescent from the bowl-maker's snips remained visible. With chalk she marked one line on the sill and one on the folded carrier, leaving a visible width where the brass could bow before the stone found a hidden support.

Arion moved his left hand away.

Emilia seated the carrier against the collar. It rocked once, stopped against the shim, and left a white line of chalk showing through the open seam.

“Breath?” she asked.

“Same.”

“Hand?”

“No better.”

“No worse?”

He waited through the fingers before answering. “No farther in.”

Across the barrier, Mera shifted her lamp.

The yellow band widened through the mineral-grey sheet. Water softened against the channel. Beneath Emilia's hands, one lower shutter moved by the width of its worn notch.

Emilia took the cord in both hands and lifted until the three counterweights cleared their stops. She fed back half a finger. The yoke descended. Its rods pulled the floor-slot collar against the folded carrier.

The first shutter travelled.

The tilted third leaf caught early. Its edge scored the guide with a sound like a knife on an empty plate.

“Still?” Arion asked.

“Still moving.”

The carrier began to turn. The shim kept its narrow bright edge. Along the channel, the floor slot shifted sideways from the ceiling slot, and the falling water bent toward the dry return groove.

Mera's lamp narrowed.

Emilia closed both hands on the cord.

“Stop.”

The yoke struck tension. One counterweight swung and settled. The collar held short of the first chalk line.

Arion did not touch it. Mera's empty hand remained raised beyond the water.

Emilia's palms had taken the shape of the cord. The yellow repair on her cuff caught against a torn fibre when she eased one hand closer to the yoke. She freed it one thread at a time. Pulling harder would have been faster and would have opened the seam across her old burn.

She watched Mera's side without counting the wait.

Mera stood beyond the retained-return bar, well clear of the fixed slips and sealed water. Her lamp stayed at the narrower angle. The water struck hard enough to erase her mouth.

Arion's next exhale broke once against the rib binding.

Emilia looked at him.

“Cord only,” he said. “I followed it.”

“Your hand?”

“Unchanged.”

She held the mechanism where it was. “Do not improve the answer.”

“Wasn't planning to.”

“You have a documented history.”

His mouth twitched. The next breath caught before the reply came, and he shut it. Chalk loosened from the cord into the sweat on Emilia's palms. Mera's lamp held its narrower angle.

Mera lowered her empty hand. She looked once at the housing, once at the channel, and then back at Emilia. The lamp moved one handspan.

Water softened.

The damaged lower shutter travelled after it.

“Water only,” Mera called. “I want to continue.”

Emilia loosened her grip. Cord passed across her cuff in rough increments as the counterweights descended. The yoke drew the collar farther against the sacrificial bearing.

The kitchen-brass shim held.

The folded carrier did not. Its outer edge bowed around the worn bearing face, slowly enough for Emilia to see the chalk marks move out of line. The gap remained visible. No load disappeared into a clean joint.

The first counterweight bottomed. Then the second.

The tilted shutter twisted against its guide and stopped the third weight a finger higher. The whole assembly gave one short sideways kick. Emilia took it through the cord, not the housing, and kept both knees off the joint.

Stone moved beneath the vertical water.

The floor-slot collar turned away from the ceiling slot. Their aligned pressure broke. Water struck the collar's new edge and divided, most of it driving into the return groove, some continuing down in a thinner sheet.

At the base of the barrier, a black line appeared.

It widened to the height of Emilia's thumb. Then her hand. Dry stone showed beyond it.

The bent carrier settled around the shim and would go no farther. The counterweights hung at their uneven stops. When Emilia tested the cord with one small return, nothing reset.

The gap would pass light and speech, but not Mera's head or shoulder; folded paper would drag through the water along its upper edge.

Emilia kept her hands on the cord.

“Mera?” Arion called.

The answer passed under the water instead of through it.

“Here.”

Emilia heard the whole word.

“Pages?” Emilia asked.

Mera touched the two folds beneath one side of her coat, then the separate fold beneath the other. “Dry.”

“Keep them there.”

Mera crouched without approaching the water. She set her lamp on the far stone behind the low opening.

Its light entered the operator bay beneath the thinned sheet. It crossed the bowed brass and stopped on Arion's still-closed fingers.
