# Exit paths

The red credential cord stopped against the toe of Emilia's right boot.

On the other side, one arm's length away, the borrowed Forge console waited beneath a row of maker marks. Renn's opened it. Tavin's had loaded the exercise. Even the transport carpenter who built the crate had permission to touch its lower panel.

Emilia's ring produced a flat red pulse.

“We established this at the calibration floor,” Renn said.

“That was a different console.”

“Credential closure is admired for its portability.”

The Roost common workshop smelled of hot mineral, old rope, and breakfast onions from the kitchen wall they shared. Its broad doors stood open to a service court where students hauled timber without magic. Akamoto had removed the weapon racks for the exchange and left their bolt holes visible. Forge instruments now occupied three long benches, each tool still marked by the person who made it and the person responsible for its return.

The console remained inside a rectangle of red cord pinned to the floor.

Emilia stood outside it with her right sleeve loosened around the healing Prism burn.

Renn read from the exchange terms. “You may observe, make verbal recommendations, stop your own participation, and handle uncertified components beyond the credential boundary. You may not operate, configure, repair, or physically stop certified equipment.”

“If the certified equipment is harming someone?”

“You call stop. A credentialed operator acts.”

“Latency?”

“Tavin is less than two paces away.”

At the console, Tavin lifted one hand without turning. “One and a half. I measured because I knew you would ask.”

He had shaved one side of his head since Emilia last saw him and braided the rest through three copper rings. His work coat bore a fresh patch at the shoulder in unapologetic yellow. As journeymen they had spent a year trading the calibration-floor record for fastest blind assembly. Tavin still held it by six breaths because he considered breathing part of the task and Emilia did not.

“Your route crosses a bench corner,” she said.

“I can move around furniture.”

“The user inside the cascade cannot move around your confidence.”

Tavin faced her then. “Good. We have reached the argument before the material is hot.”

Renn placed his credential ring against the terms. “Do you accept observer access under these limits?”

The original incident instrument remained in a locked cradle at the Forge. The copied pattern remained in the council model. Emilia had no approved project, no laboratory bench, and no authority to test a design under her own name. This exchange existed because Renn had argued that incident responsibility required ongoing safety education, and because the Roost could receive borrowed equipment without reopening her credentials.

It was narrow access.

The cascade rig beyond the red line was narrower still: one material channel, six hand stations, three visible exits.

Emilia had entered the exchange to answer one design question: could a person inside a field see whether an exit remained available without a device measuring the person or choosing for them? The cascade gave her a live route, visible hands, and enough automatic authority to argue against.

“I accept,” Emilia said.

Renn and Tavin signed. Emilia added her name under *observer* and did not improve the noun.

Four Roost students and two Forge apprentices took the manual stations. Each stood beside a physical route lever large enough to operate in gloves. The central trough began under a ceramic hopper, passed through a glass-form chamber, narrowed between two metal-state rollers, and ended in a clay quench bed. A neutral mineral compound would enter as powder and leave as six interlocking support tiles.

If the transitions aligned, the process produced useful material with almost no waste. If heat, pressure, or timing drifted, the same channel could hold glass tension inside metal density and break without warning after installation.

The certified console watched twenty-four values and could close the hopper, vent heat, separate the rollers, flood the quench, and lock every manual lever before an operator chose the wrong sequence.

“Why not let it?” Tavin asked when Emilia requested manual mode.

“Because the person at station three can see one indicator.”

“The console sees twenty-four.”

“The person has one body inside the route.”

“Bodies become unconscious.”

“Then automatic stop is useful.”

Tavin waited for the rest.

“Useful is not the same as sufficient,” Emilia said.

He turned to the manual team. “You heard her concede the first point. Record the date.”

One Forge apprentice scratched it onto the exercise slate.

Renn handed Emilia the observer pane. Unlike the incident mirror, it displayed every state received by the console. She could rotate the view but not select or alter a value. Tavin enabled the cascade.

Powder entered the hopper with a dry hiss.

At station one, a Roost student opened the heat gate. The mineral turned clear, then pale gold as it crossed into glass state. Station two adjusted the channel width. The liquid stretched into a ribbon thin enough that Emilia could see the workbench grain refracted through it.

“Pressure rising at roller two,” she said.

“Within band,” Tavin replied.

“Approaching manual lag.”

The Forge apprentice at station three leaned closer to his single indicator. Green. He kept both hands on the roller lever.

On Emilia's pane, three hidden values moved toward amber. The automatic system would intervene in four breaths. The operator's green light would change only after it did.

“Open secondary vent,” Emilia said.

The student at station four reached for the blue lever.

“Hold,” Tavin said.

“The roller is loading.”

“The glass edge is blue.”

Emilia looked up from the pane.

A narrow blue cast ran along the ribbon where it entered the metal-state rollers. She had read increasing pressure as trapped heat. The color showed premature metal alignment. Opening the vent would cool the outer surface and lock the misalignment inside.

“Hold vent,” Emilia said.

Tavin moved one control. “Reduce roller field by three.”

The station-three apprentice pulled the lever back. The ribbon loosened with a ringing note. Its edge returned to gold.

“My call was wrong,” Emilia told the exercise slate.

The recorder wrote it before Renn could.

Tavin glanced at her. “The console agreed with you.”

“The console was wrong too.”

“Its automatic sequence would have separated the rollers rather than opened your vent.”

“Then it was wrong more safely.”

“That is most of certification.”

The material entered metal state. Its clear ribbon clouded from the center outward and began to ring against the channel guides. At station five, a Roost operator placed both palms on a manual pressure wheel. She could feel the change before any light reached her.

“There is a knock under the left guide,” she said.

Emilia's pane showed equal pressure.

Tavin's console showed equal pressure.

The operator removed one hand and touched the guide housing. “It returns every third turn.”

“Foreign material?” Renn asked.

“Or a seam closing out of phase,” Emilia said.

The automatic system did nothing. All watched values remained green.

The operator had one red lever labelled *manual isolate*. She looked to Tavin.

“If you isolate now, the tile set fails,” he said. “No predicted injury state.”

“Predicted by what?” she asked.

“The rig.”

The knock returned through her palm.

She pulled the red lever.

Mechanical shutters closed before and after station five. The ribbon snapped in the empty space between them. One bright fragment struck the inside guard and opened along a dark inclusion no pane had reported.

Tavin stopped the cascade.

The hopper closed. Heat vented. Rollers separated. Quench water flooded the final bed. Every automatic action completed cleanly.

Inside the isolated chamber, the broken fragment continued to ring.

The manual operator lifted both hands from the wheel. “Do I fail?”

“The tile set fails,” Tavin said. “You found material the instrument missed.”

“That was not my question.”

He looked at the slate, then at Renn. “The practicum grades safe shutdown. Pass.”

Emilia handed back the observer pane. “The person inside had one useful state. The console had twenty-four wrong ones.”

“Twenty-four incomplete ones,” Tavin said. “And it still stopped heat, pressure, rollers, and quench in the correct sequence after her decision.”

“Because she decided.”

“Because automation made one person's decision survivable.”

Renn opened the isolated chamber after it cooled. The inclusion was a sliver of old guide material, same density as the new ribbon until the state change reached it. No calibration would have distinguished it before live transition. The operator had felt the recurrent knock because her palm received rhythm, not averaged pressure.

Emilia wanted to make a tool that preserved that difference.

The workshop meal arrived while the apprentices disassembled the failed tile set. Roost tables unfolded from the wall on iron brackets. Bowls of onion stew, hard cheese, apples, and flat bread appeared among tools that no one bothered to hide. The Forge visitors placed clean cloth beneath their plates. The Roost students did not.

Emilia sat outside the red line.

Her dark coat had been cut to fall cleanly over the bandage at her arm. The lining carried a hidden opening so the cloth never rubbed the Prism burn. Only the loosened cuff admitted that anything beneath it had changed.

Tavin tore bread and looked at the sleeve.

“That seam will open when you lift a hammer,” he said.

“I am not permitted to lift a hammer.”

“Your tailoring has become legally dependent.”

A Roost student across from them wore a coat whose left half came from a grain sack and whose right sleeve had once been a sail. She put a spool of yellow thread beside Emilia's bowl.

“Patch the outside,” she said. “Then it will stop trying to prove it is not a patch.”

Emilia looked at the thread. It matched nothing.

“Do you have blue?”

“Yes.”

“May I see it?”

The student produced a dark blue spool.

“The yellow is better,” Emilia said.

Tavin choked on his stew.

Emilia unpicked two hidden stitches with her eating knife, set the yellow patch over the stress point, and asked the student to sew it because her right hand still cramped. The repair crossed the cuff in a bright diagonal. It ruined the coat's continuous line and kept the fabric off her burn when she raised her arm.

“Acceptable,” Emilia said.

“Record the date,” Tavin told the slate.

This time Emilia threw a piece of bread at him.

After the meal, the manual operator placed the fractured inclusion on the uncertified bench outside the red cord. Tavin brought a Prism shutter assembly to the boundary and opened its casing himself. His personal maker mark sat on the clear inner wafer beside a hairline repair. He removed the wafer, returned the certified casing to the console bench, and placed his component in front of Emilia.

“Wafer belongs to me,” he said. “You may use it for the exercise.”

Emilia arranged it beside a mechanical continuity fork and a small return spring. None sensed heat, pressure, identity, source, or body state.

The fork answered one question only: did the physical exit path connect through the device?

Emilia cut three narrow slots into a scrap-brass faceplate. She seated the fork behind the first two, aligned Tavin's wafer across its contact points, and linked the return spring to a manual shutter tab. Green could reach the first slot only when the fork carried a complete path. Closing the shutter shifted the wafer and put red behind the second. If the spring, fork, or wafer slipped out of alignment, neither color could reach the faceplate; plain amber backing remained visible through the third slot.

Open meant the path existed through the aid. Closed meant the aid's own shutter blocked it. Unknown meant the fork, shutter, or spring could not verify itself.

Tavin crouched beside the bench. “Where is the automatic stop?”

“There isn't one.”

“Then this is an indicator.”

“Yes.”

“You described a field-state aid.”

“It reports the state of the exit it occupies.”

“It does not report whether a person should use it.”

“Correct.”

“Or whether the field is dangerous.”

“Correct.”

“Or whether the person is conscious.”

“Tavin.”

He held out both hands. “I am locating the product.”

Emilia closed the shutter. The first window darkened and the second showed a red bar. She opened it; green appeared. Then she loosened the return spring by one turn. Both colors vanished, exposing the amber backing. The device offered no conclusion beyond the failure of its own answer. A person looking at it would know whether the aid offered a path and whether it had lost the ability to say.

Renn brought the certification slate to the outside bench.

“An unconscious user cannot act on an indicator,” he said.

“Then a separate rescue system may act under its own authority.”

“The Forge certifies complete safety functions.”

“That is how a continuity service became part of a local release.”

“This review is not the incident hearing.”

Emilia stopped. The old answer had begun pulling every new tool into its shape.

Renn tested the three windows. “As an exit indicator, it is legible. As a fail-safe, it cannot guarantee stop.”

“It is not a fail-safe.”

“Then the practicum cannot certify it.”

The word *denied* appeared on the slate before either of them signed.

Tavin tapped his maker mark on the clear wafer. “The exercise loan is over.”

Emilia waited for him to remove it.

“I am leaving it as a contributing maker,” he said.

“You disagree with the design,” she said.

“I disagree with calling it a fail-safe. If you call it an exit witness, I disagree less.”

Renn added the phrase to the personal-custody line. *Uncertified exit witness. No automatic control. Maker Emilia. Contributing maker Tavin. Personal liability accepted; institutional protection none.*

Emilia read it twice.

“If this fails near Arion,” Renn said, “your incident closure will be cited as notice that you understood the risk.”

“If it works?”

“It remains uncertified until a new review.”

She signed.

Tavin signed the component. Renn signed as witness, not sponsor.

Emilia loosened the return spring again. The amber *unknown* window warmed her palm. Beside her, the certified cascade rig displayed a perfect stopped state across twenty-four values. The red credential line still lay between Emilia and the only console that could declare it so.
