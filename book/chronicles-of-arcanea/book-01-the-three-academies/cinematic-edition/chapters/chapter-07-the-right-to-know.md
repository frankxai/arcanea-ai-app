# The right to know

The paper seal tightened around the blue vial with a sound smaller than cracking ice.

Mera saw it happen from the other side of the table. The amber band drew inward first, wrinkling where it crossed the black witness seal. A crescent of air appeared beneath the paper. The water inside the glass lifted toward it.

“That tray is too cold,” she said.

The registry clerk held up one hand. “Evidence has entered placement. Do not touch it.”

“The stopper is lined with neutral silver. The glass will contract faster than the lining. If the band parts, the silver becomes the last unbroken boundary.”

“Then it will hold.”

The water climbed another finger toward the neck.

Mera pressed her burned palm flat against the stone instead of reaching. The severed cable had left a violet line from the heel of her hand to the base of her smallest finger. In the cooled room, it felt as if the wire were still there.

“The silver prevents direct exchange,” she said. “If pressure crosses sympathetically through the glass, it marks the transfer. It cannot stop it.”

The clerk looked toward the inner door. It did not open.

“You have perhaps six breaths,” Mera said.

“You are not the placing officer.”

“No.” She watched a bubble form beneath the stopper. “I am the person who will be held liable when your tray opens my source.”

Footsteps approached along the gallery outside, unhurried until the final three. Archivist Orrel entered with his hat under one arm and rain still darkening the shoulders of his coat. He took in the vial, the clerk's raised hand, and Mera's palm on the table.

“Has she touched it?” he asked.

“No.”

“Has anyone recorded that she warned you?”

The clerk's mouth tightened.

Orrel removed a silver stylus from the table well. “I am recording it now.”

He wrote the time on a narrow strip of slate, signed beneath, then nodded to Mera. “Correct the placement. I will witness.”

She lifted the vial by its carrier rather than the glass. Cold crossed the metal into her fingers. The tray beneath it was veined white, an arrest bed meant to still active memory in transit. Two shelves behind the clerk stood open. Mera chose a dark green cradle marked for sealed personal source and set the carrier inside.

The water descended. The bubble beneath the stopper flattened without touching the silver.

“Eight breaths,” Orrel said.

“I allowed for argument.”

The clerk drew the original slate toward himself and added three lines in writing severe enough to score stone.

Orrel watched him. “Include the wrong tray.”

“The source was not breached.”

“Include why.”

The clerk added a fourth line.

The Lower Registry occupied a ring of rooms below the city waterline. No windows opened to the morning. Instead, narrow channels ran through the walls at shoulder height, each fed by a different district cistern. Water moved behind black glass with the slow pressure of a tide held inside architecture. The stone drank sound. A dropped stylus would have seemed intimate there.

Mera had expected to resent the place.

Every shelf made resentment difficult.

Vessels stood in individual cradles with their histories visible. Maker. Source. First witness. Every hand that had carried them. Every question asked. Every access granted or refused. Green thread marked return to a living source. White marked a public record. Red did not mean dangerous, as it did at the harbor. Here it meant disputed.

Nothing appeared ownerless.

The clerk shut the shelf around Mera's vial but left the lock open. “Examiner Vale will determine final placement after deposition.”

“The harbor compact already determined placement.”

“The harbor compact did not anticipate a witness entering a live cross-domain event while carrying the source.”

“It anticipated contamination.”

“It anticipated weather.”

Orrel returned his stylus to the well. “The compact anticipated institutions improving their authority by changing nouns. Leave the lock open.”

The clerk's gaze moved from him to Mera and back. He left it open.

Examiner Neris Vale arrived carrying two cups of tea and a transcript case. She wore the dark blue working coat of the Athenaeum without rank embroidery. Its cuffs had been replaced twice, the newer cloth chosen to match and failing by half a shade. She placed one cup beside Mera and the other at the empty end of the table.

“Orrel drinks harbor coffee,” she said. “It has made intervention useless.”

“It has made me difficult to poison.”

“The tea is strong enough to offer similar protection.”

Mera tasted it. Bitter leaf, a little salt, no sweetener. Whoever had brewed it understood cold rooms.

Neris opened the transcript case. Inside lay four sheets of pressed white fiber, a neutral-water dish, and a stylus tipped with clear glass. She read the clerk's placement slate before looking at the vial.

“Good correction,” she said.

“Bad intake,” Mera answered.

“Both will remain.”

That answer pleased her more than it should have.

The inner door opened again. Two porters entered carrying a long green bottle between them. The glass was old enough to have softened at the base. Names circled it in silver script, dozens of them, the first almost hidden beneath the last.

An elderly woman followed. She kept both hands inside the sleeves of her coat.

The porters set the bottle in a return cradle. Neris stopped the deposition without apology. One porter read each transfer aloud: dock infirmary, South Channel inquiry, civic tribunal, Athenaeum appeal, council review, three sealed remands, final restoration order. At every name the woman said yes or asked for a date to be repeated. When the porter reached a council recorder's refusal to return, the woman leaned closer.

“That was the winter they said I had never given it,” she said.

The porter rotated the bottle. A jagged interval interrupted the silver names. Beneath it, older writing remained visible through the green glass: *source present; transfer refused by Recorder Pell.*

“Your original mark held,” Neris said.

The woman drew one hand from her sleeve. Three fingers were missing above the first knuckle. She placed what remained against the glass, not breaking the seal.

“So did I,” she said.

No one in the room softened the moment by answering.

The return continued. The woman identified the private-room token tied around the bottle's neck. She confirmed the access limits. Only then did the porters lift it again and accompany her through another door.

Neris waited until it closed.

“A council official removed her statement from the dock record seventeen years ago,” she said. “He could not remove the vessel's transfer history. Without the embodied source, the surviving mark would have proved only that something changed hands.”

Mera turned her tea once between her palms. “You arranged for me to see that.”

“The return was scheduled.”

“You delayed my appointment until it occurred.”

“Yes.”

Orrel took the empty chair. “You asked to know why the Athenaeum requires source memory from a witness-participant. That is one reason.”

The violet burn in Mera's palm tightened. She set down the cup before the water in it could answer her grip.

“Whose interest do you believe surrender serves?” Neris asked.

“Mine, if the council disputes my account. Arion's, if the account prevents them from classifying him through an unopposed record. Yours, if a later inquiry accuses the Athenaeum of altering either.”

“And whose interest does refusal serve?”

“Arion's.”

“Only his?”

Mera looked toward the green shelf. Condensation silvered the vial's carrier but did not touch the seals.

“Mine,” she said. “If I decide that carrying a trace gives me the right to open it, I will eventually become indistinguishable from the people I am trying to document.”

Neris made no approving mark. “Describe the trace without reading it.”

“Structural pressure entered the Water channel after the hidden mirror continued past local release. Some of it crossed the district hazard basin. Some crossed the severed conductor while I was grounding residual Prism charge.”

“Did the trace originate in Arion?”

“I cannot attribute all of it to him.”

“Did any of it?”

“Yes.”

“How do you know?”

Mera felt again the instant the cable entered her hand: the control water clearing under a violet flare, Emilia bracing the far end, Arion's wrapped fingers holding after there was no reason except that release had not yet become possible. Beneath those events, a wordless pressure had moved like a structure recognizing a repaired absence and loading itself toward it.

She had stopped herself before following that pressure inward.

“Contact sequence,” she said. “Timing. The trace changed when he released the floor.”

“Observed timing or remembered pressure?”

“Both.”

Neris placed the neutral-water dish between them. “This does not receive content. It records the boundary around content so the two can be separated in your account. You may stop at any point. You may mark a private interval. The refusal will remain visible.”

“And the boundary map?”

“Enters protected custody.”

“Accessible to whom?”

“The three-person inquiry panel. A respondent named in the account. A source subject with standing. A medical officer under immediate-harm authority.”

“Arion has not been granted source standing.”

“He has not completed classification.”

“Then everyone deciding what he is may read the edge of his memory before he may object.”

Orrel folded his hands on the table. Rainwater had dried from his coat, leaving a pale tide around the collar.

“If you submit only a procedural account,” he said, “the council will call the pressure impression contamination. They will say the mirror stopped because a Water practitioner interfered with a Foundation event she did not understand.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

“Can you prove that?”

His jaw shifted once. “Not without the remote record or your source boundary.”

“And the remote record belongs to the council.”

“At present.”

Neris moved the dish no closer. “The boundary map would be sealed from publication. You heard its access limits.”

“Three inquiry members, a named respondent, a source subject with standing, or a medical officer,” Mera said. “Arion has not been granted standing.”

“Then your objection is to the access list.”

“My objection is that the list becomes real before he can enter it.”

“The unanswered question remains whether this is his memory, your memory of him, or environmental trace from a public hazard.”

“Who decides?”

“That is what a custody inquiry is for.”

“After you possess it.”

“If we do not preserve the distinction now, there may be nothing honest left for an inquiry to decide.”

Mera looked at the old bottle's empty cradle. The Athenaeum had protected a dockworker's statement for seventeen years because it had refused to let authority become invisible. Neris was offering that same machinery to her. Mera drank the cold tea. The salt was stronger now.

She drew the first transcript sheet toward her.

“I will give a procedural account,” she said.

Neris removed the neutral-water dish.

“It will be ruled incomplete.”

“Rule it after you read it.”

The clear stylus made no mark until Mera spoke. Then her voice appeared in narrow grey script across the pressed fiber.

She gave her arrival time, the condition of the public-weather source, and the two seals on the vial. She described the measurement ring and named each person she had directly observed. When she reached the district basin, she distinguished what the steward claimed from what Arion said. She recorded her refusal to compare the hazard trace without his consent.

Neris interrupted only for source.

“You state the local instrument could operate without Water comparison. How do you know?”

“Emilia said so in the presence of the intake officials.”

“Did you verify it?”

“No.”

The stylus added: *attributed; unverified.*

Mera continued through the anchored chairs, the hidden mirror, and Arion's unanswered request to stop. She recorded Emilia locating the unmarked conductor by heat, Renn admitting he could not identify the line, and her own decision to take the cable before memory crossed the open water.

At the collapse, the script slowed.

“What did the mirror display?” Neris asked.

“A repeated seam across four reports.”

“Four gifts?”

“The display assigned no such category.”

“Four Elements?”

“It showed reports. Any conclusion beyond that requires the instrument method, which was not disclosed.”

“Did you perceive the same pattern through Water?”

Mera's burned hand had begun to sweat against the stone. A damp print formed under it, crossed by the violet line.

“I perceived pressure I chose not to examine.”

The clear stylus waited.

“Mark a private interval,” Orrel said quietly.

Mera looked at him.

“If you mark it,” he continued, “the record proves you encountered content and withheld it. That is stronger than pretending there was none.”

The blank would also announce that something inside her might be worth taking.

“Mark unaccessed,” she said. “Not private. I cannot classify content I did not read.”

Neris drew the classification seal toward her, stopped, then left it beside the water dish.

The stylus left a clean rectangular space in the transcript. Around it appeared: *witness reports trace; access declined; content and source classification undetermined.*

Mera finished with Arion's release, the split control basin, the opening of the hammer's old seam, and the mirror saving its record after local stop.

Neris read all four sheets. Orrel read them second. He changed one instance of *council order* to *speaker instruction* because no named official had claimed the words. Mera initialed the margin. The correction made the document weaker and more accurate.

Her tea had gone cold.

Neris placed a square seal beneath the final line and pressed her thumb to it. Dark blue spread across the fiber.

*Incomplete witness account: embodied source boundary withheld.*

Mera had expected anger. What came instead was the uncomfortable relief of seeing the right damage named.

A chime sounded inside the transcript case. Neris opened a side compartment and removed a narrow medical slate.

“The Luminary ward has filed for continued observation,” she said. “Their petition cites an uncontained resonance and the absence of an independent complete account.”

Orrel looked at Mera. He did not say that surrender could have changed it.

“Is he awake?” she asked.

“Long enough to reject another test.” Neris turned the slate. Beneath the medical notation, a separate line had been cut in rough block letters. “And to name requested witnesses.”

*Mera Tidecrest. Emilia. No council substitute.*

The second name had no family name. Mera could not tell whether Arion did not know it or had run out of strength.

She read the line twice and returned the slate.

“Add this deposition to the ward petition,” she said.

“The incomplete ruling will support continued observation.”

“The account also records that the last observation continued after he asked it to stop.”

Neris closed the transcript case. “Both will travel.”

The registry clerk came to the green shelf carrying red thread.

“What is that for?” Mera asked.

“Your source custody is under review. Witness-held status depends on standing. Your standing now depends on the inquiry.”

“The vial did not enter the measurement.”

“Your hand entered the active Water system while you retained sole custody. The source may contain sympathetic transfer.”

“You have no evidence that it does.”

“That is why the mark means disputed.”

He reached for the open lock.

Orrel stood. “Review does not revoke possession. Harbor seal, clause nine. She retains the vessel unless you record immediate source danger or loss of capacity.”

The clerk looked to Neris.

“He is correct,” she said.

“Then the thread travels on the carrier.”

“With an external cradle,” Mera said. “No contact across the paper seals.”

The clerk chose a narrow transport frame and began tying the red thread around its corners.

Mera placed one finger on the transcript case. “The record is not complete.”

“You declined the missing portion,” Neris said.

“Your request is missing.”

“The source-boundary request is an internal examination step.”

“It changed the ruling.”

“Your refusal changed the ruling.”

“A refusal cannot exist without a request.”

The clerk stopped tying.

Neris studied her for a long moment. “You want an adverse-action notation.”

“I want the next reader to know what the institution attempted to obtain before it called my account incomplete.”

“No transfer occurred.”

“Neither did the one in the wrong tray. You kept that.”

Orrel took the silver stylus from the well.

“I witnessed both,” he said.

He wrote on the outer sheet: *Examiner requested embodied source-boundary preservation. Witness declined pending source-subject standing and consent. Declination materially limited evidentiary classification.*

He signed his name.

Neris read the notation. “That will place you inside the review.”

“I was in the room.”

She signed beneath him.

The transcript case chimed again. The Luminary ward had amended Arion's request while they argued. Neris opened the notice and turned it toward Mera.

*Participant witness, personal capacity. Enters separately. May object, stop observing, or leave. Assumes no control duty. Disputed standing and institutional consequences remain attached.*

“Transfer proceeds only if both requested witnesses accept,” Neris said.

Mera checked the sending physician, receiving authority, and manual-stop terms. Each carried a name. “If I object during movement?”

“The escort records it. Medical authority must answer it before continuing, unless stopping creates immediate physical danger.”

Mera selected *accept in personal capacity* and signed beneath.

The clerk returned the vial in its external cradle. The red thread crossed in front of the blue glass without touching it. Mera adjusted the frame until the amber and black seals faced outward and every knot remained visible.

On the table, the word *incomplete* dried across the transcript. Above it, the clear rectangle held its shape—one deliberate blank among hundreds of vessels bearing names.
