# A vessel at the gate

The joint hall received the submersible without letting its water touch the floor.

Mera approved of this before she approved of anything else.

The vessel rose into a flooded dock at the center of a circular chamber. Bronze doors closed beneath its keel. Pumps drew the sea away through witness grates, separating outside water from every drain, basin, and drinking cup beyond the gate. Only when the stone around the hull stood dry did the boarding bridge unfold.

Someone had understood contamination. More unusually, someone had given that understanding a budget.

Mera stepped onto the bridge with the blue vial upright in its padded carrier. Her clothes were dry now. Her boots were not. Each left a dark print on pale stone until warm air rose through the floor and took the moisture from the leather.

Orrel came behind her with the public-weather case chained to his wrist.

“Do not admire the drains too openly,” he said. “The intake office will add them to your fees.”

“My letter states there are no entry fees.”

“Then they will give you a tour.”

He had been less talkative during the descent. The submersible passed below the storm shelf into water deep enough that morning became a color remembered above them. Mera spent the journey with the vial locked between her feet, watching schools of silver fish turn around the craft without touching its wake. Orrel spent it completing six custody forms. Neither mentioned the appendix in her invitation.

At the inner end of the bridge, a woman in a white-and-blue steward’s coat waited beside a narrow table. Behind her, the joint hall opened through three kinds of workmanship that did not pretend to agree. Crystal ribs carried light down from a height Mera could not see. Black Forge braces crossed them at load points, each bolt stamped by its maker. Water traveled through shallow channels cut into the floor and paused at glass gates before entering the next room.

The channels interested her more than the light.

Every gate bore a custody plate.

“Collector Orrel,” the steward said. “Case first.”

Orrel placed his case on the table, presented the chain seal, and read the collection sequence aloud. The steward checked each mark before cutting the chain. When she reached for Mera’s carrier, Mera moved it behind her hip.

The steward’s hand remained extended.

“Mera Tidecrest?”

“Yes.”

“Provisional Athenaeum entrant, unlicensed Water-memory practice, witness-held source under harbor compact?”

“Yes.”

“Place the source on the table.”

“For inspection?”

“For registration and classification.”

Mera set the carrier down but kept one hand on the strap. “The vessel is registered. The contents are sealed.”

“Harbor registration ends at the harbor.”

“The compact does not.”

“This is a joint intake facility. A Foundation-active hazardous transfer is in the adjoining hall. No unclassified memory source enters an active diagnostic perimeter.”

Mera glanced at Orrel. “You did not mention an active intake.”

“It was not active when we departed.”

“It is not active now,” the steward said. “It is waiting on source clearance.”

On Venn’s clipboard, the line marked *subject* remained blank while *source clearance* had already been stamped pending.

Mera returned her attention to the woman’s coat. Her name was stitched beside the inside seam: Venn, First Memory Steward. The choice to hide names from the public face of a uniform appeared institutional rather than personal.

“Steward Venn,” Mera said, “the vial contains public-weather trace under protective seal. I am registering the vessel as present and the contents as unavailable.”

“Unavailable content is still content.”

“Not to the hall.”

“Water does not observe your paperwork.”

“This water does. The seal is neutral silver.”

“Silver prevents direct exchange. It does not prevent sympathetic response.” Venn indicated the floor channels. “The active transfer has already displaced shared structure across a connected row. If his field reaches an unknown memory source, I am responsible for every person captured in it.”

Mera’s hand tightened on the carrier strap.

“Is the transfer conscious?” Mera asked.

“Yes.”

“Has he agreed to a memory comparison?”

“His consent review is in progress.”

“Then there is no memory comparison to contaminate.”

Venn looked at Orrel. “You brought me an advocate.”

“I brought you a source with legs,” he said. “The opinions attached themselves.”

Mera unfastened the carrier. She removed the vial without touching the glass directly and set it in the center of the table, equidistant from both edges. Rainwater filled it to the neck. The black seal band remained intact. Her dry thumbprint from the quay had survived beneath a protective strip.

Venn read the one-witness clause already fixed beneath the black band. “Harbor liability travels with you. This facility adds isolation.”

She wrapped an amber band over the first seal without covering Mera’s signature. It marked the vial as a possible contaminant, kept it within three paces of a witness channel, and required Mera to surrender it or return to the dock if the source responded.

“A response from the vial,” Mera said. “Not fear from the transfer.”

Venn took a stylus. “Visible, thermal, pressure, seal, or witness-glass change required.”

“Record that.”

The steward did. She wrote *witness-held* in the ownership field, left classification blank, and pressed a matching amber mark to Mera’s wrist. The nearest floor channel opened its next glass gate.

“Stay on the route,” Venn said. “Do not touch another source.”

“People?”

“Three paces, Tidecrest.”

Mera lifted the vial into its carrier and followed the amber water line.

The joint hall widened beyond the dock. She had expected institutional grandeur and found working beauty instead. Light from the crystal ribs changed according to which gates were open. Forge braces carried small plaques naming the apprentices who had tested them. Beside each door, a water column held the most recent consent status in plain script. No murals. No heroic founders watching entrants become small.

At the center of the gallery stood a ring of dark stone wide enough for six people. One quarter belonged to each Academy’s equipment. The remaining quarter held an ordinary chair, a pitcher, a folded blanket, and a wooden tool tray.

A hammer lay in the tray under evidence cloth.

Mera stopped.

The cloth concealed most of it, but the handle extended beyond the fold. Ash wood. Two brass pins. A dark repaired seam. Through the sealed vial, she felt no content and no voice. Only a slight gathering of pressure against the glass nearest the tool.

The amber mark on her wrist remained cool.

“State change?” Venn called from the dock.

Mera watched the waterline. “None I can support.”

A young woman at the instrument bench looked up.

She wore a Forge work coat with one sleeve rolled and the other buttoned, as if she had started dressing for the review and then encountered a more important screw. A narrow prism relay lay open before her. Its case followed a precise curve except for a brass tab protruding from the cuff.

Her gaze moved from Mera’s vial to the amber route, then to the hammer.

“The mirror requested Water comparison,” she said.

“Did you?” Mera asked.

“No.”

The answer came too fast to be strategic.

“Can your instrument use memory?”

“My instrument reports field state.” The young woman touched the brass tab. “The mirror compares anything the hall supplies.”

“Emilia,” said a tired voice from the chair. “Who are you arguing with?”

The occupant had been hidden by the nearest crystal rib. He leaned forward now, elbows on his knees, both hands wrapped in grey cloth. Dust remained in his dark hair despite the clean blanket around his shoulders. His eyes found Mera, then the vial, then the tool tray. When Venn crossed between him and the hammer, his wrapped right hand tightened against his knee.

Emilia said, “A witness-held source with an objection.”

“Mera Tidecrest,” Mera said.

The man did not offer a hand. Good.

“Arion.”

Behind the gallery glass, three intake officials took their places. A memory steward entered carrying a shallow basin sealed with the same red wax used on public hazard records.

“We can clear the Water domain without opening the private channel,” the steward said. “Tidecrest, you will compare the transfer trace against your public-weather sample for shared discontinuity.”

Mera looked at the basin. “Whose memory?”

“Transfer record from the origin event.”

Arion’s bandaged fingers closed against his knees. “I did not give them a memory.”

The steward turned toward him. “The district recorded environmental trace during emergency containment. It is a hazard record, not a personal deposition.”

“It came from my house.”

“From a public lane.”

“Through me.”

The officials behind the glass began writing.

Mera kept her place on the amber route. “What comparison do you require?”

“Read for the engineered absence registered in your source. Nothing else.”

“Has Arion agreed?”

“The record does not require subject consent.”

“I did not ask about the record.”

The steward’s expression hardened. “The intake cannot proceed while two unclassified sources may be interacting.”

“Then do not proceed.”

Silence moved through the gallery without becoming empty. Water pressed against glass gates. The prism relay gave a low, waiting hum. Somewhere inside the evidence cloth, wood tapped once against the tray as Arion shifted his foot.

Emilia looked toward the mirrored terminal. “My local test does not require the memory channel.”

One of the officials touched the speaker. “Cross-domain clearance does.”

“Then call it the mirror’s test,” Emilia said.

The official did not answer her.

Arion looked at Mera. “Do you know what is in that basin?”

“No.”

“Do you know me?”

“No.”

“Then why refuse?”

Mera adjusted the vial until the amber and black seals aligned beneath her thumb. “Because I know who asked.”

Arion looked down at the two seals. His thumb pressed the bandage across his palm as if testing whether pain had changed. Emilia kept one finger on the brass release. Neither thanked Mera. The refusal had not made them allies.

“Record my refusal,” she told the memory steward. “No comparative read without direct consent from the person carried in the trace.”

“Your witness standing is provisional.”

“Then the record should be easy to dismiss.”

The steward sealed the basin again. Behind the glass, one official left. Another continued writing. The third watched Arion rather than the evidence.

Venn came from the dock with a new entry sheet. “The active intake is suspended pending terms. The source remains witness-held. Tidecrest, sign your facility liability.”

Mera took the stylus.

The form offered boxes for Academy, House, owner, custodian, subject, and instrument. None applied cleanly. Venn had crossed out *owner* and written *witness* above it.

Mera signed there.

When she looked up, Arion had moved his wrapped hammer from the tray to the chair beside him. Emilia had closed the prism cuff around its unfinished brass release.

Mera set the sealed vial inside the amber channel. Hammer, release, and vessel stood at three points around the suspended ring. The ring remained open between them.
