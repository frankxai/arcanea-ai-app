“You may use the name,” the registrar said. “You aren't required to answer to it.”

Selene turned the token between her fingers. The porcelain was warm from the woman's hand. A tiny painted ladder marked one edge.

“What does it do?”

“Admits you to the upper registry. The same door, each time. No other district.”

“And what do you keep?”

The registrar looked at her with a little more interest.

“For a visitor? Nothing. For refuge, an original record goes into escrow. Those are different agreements.”

“I'd like to see the difference written down.”

The woman brought a second sheet.

Her name was Nara. She said it while pointing out the terms, as if remembering that names could still be given without being managed.

Below the orchard, a stair descended between limestone walls. Selene went with Tomás. Damián remained above to speak with Leda and find out whether Ors's roads were still watched. Brío stayed in the paddock under Inés's eye.

The Palace opened beneath them in a long flooded hall. Black water lay shallow across the main floor, deepening only in the sunken service channels where the record carts had once run.

Selene had imagined rooms. What she saw was a city turned inward: narrow aisles between standing records, bridges over black water, balconies where a person crossing could be seen only as a pale interruption in the dark. Porcelain masks hung in a few niches. Most niches were empty. Their absence made the occupied ones harder to look at.

Nara stopped at a door marked with the same ladder as Selene's token.

“Go through and come back.”

Selene did. A pale thread tightened in the doorframe, then relaxed. On the second attempt it did exactly the same thing.

The next door bore a cup. It stayed shut.

Tomás lifted his own token. It carried a wheel.

“That's an inconvenient arrangement for a meeting.”

“You may wait on the dry ledge,” Nara said. “Visitors can always return along the dry route.”

She pointed it out: a continuous lip of limestone above the flood line, narrow in places but unbroken. Selene followed it with her eyes. At the far end, pale new masonry filled an older arch.

“Always?”

Nara's face changed.

“That exit was closed last month.”

“By the Palace?”

“By order.”

Vesrane received Selene in a room with no throne.

There was a worktable, a lamp, a coat hung carefully on a peg. Vesrane wore black, with one pale fold at the collar. She was older than Selene had expected and more tired than the portrait stamped on her notices. A porcelain mask hung from a taut thread beside her hand.

“You see living light,” she said.

“Yes.”

“What does mine tell you?”

“That you're alive.”

Vesrane smiled.

“Good. The last person claimed to see my intentions. He was selling something.”

She showed Selene a dry side room. Its shelves held the freely offered names of the dead, identities whose families had asked the Palace to make them useful. Some had sheltered people whose pursuers controlled every public road. A potter had crossed a border beneath the name of a woman who had died before he was born. A pair of brothers had reached work where their own village names would have barred them.

“And when they want their names back?” Selene asked.

“Some borrowed names have living dependants. Some are protecting another household. Returning one record can expose more than its owner.”

“Then tell the owner what's at risk.”

“People hear permission more clearly than consequences.”

Selene looked at the shelves.

“I came to speak with Berta and Simón.”

“Berta is being assessed for transfer. Simón has made himself useful.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Vesrane laid the mask flat on the table.

“No. It isn't.”

She took Selene to a lower gallery. Behind a barred opening, people were lifting soaked record chests from a sunken loading channel onto a narrow platform. Simón stood waist-deep in the channel beside one. His shirt clung to his ribs. Two fingers on his left hand were wrapped together.

Selene called his name.

He looked up so quickly that the chest slipped. The man beside him caught it.

“Don't,” Simón said. “They add anything we drop.”

“To what?”

He looked at Vesrane.

She did not answer.

At the upper end of the gallery, Berta stood beside a guard carrying a short staff. She had a healing cut beneath her chin, just as Serevra had described.

“Do you want to leave?” Selene asked her.

“Yes.”

No qualification. No hidden meaning for Selene to find.

Vesrane began explaining the transfer account.

Berta reached for a small green folder on the registrar's tray.

The guard struck her forearm with the staff.

Selene moved before she thought. She caught the staff with both hands and drove it sideways into the rail. Berta snatched the folder and pulled its sealed cord free.

Ripples crossed the water beneath them.

The guard's reflection raised its staff.

“Dry stone,” Nara said sharply.

The reflected blow came up through the place where Berta had been standing. She stumbled onto the ledge. A second shape rose: Selene's movement, two hands driving the staff sideways. It struck the rail from below and broke a piece from it.

Selene backed against the wall. In the water, her face wavered under the borrowed token. There was no destello where her own image stood.

The guard lifted his staff again.

“Put it down,” Vesrane said.

He did. The ripples widened, thinned, and ceased.

Nara took the folder from Berta and fastened the cord. Berta let her. Her arm was swelling.

“The original records are warded,” Nara said. “Unsealing them wakes what the water has just witnessed. Attacks repeat. The dry stones don't carry it.”

Selene looked at the broken rail.

“You hit her before there was any danger.”

The guard said nothing.

Vesrane dismissed him from the gallery. He went only as far as the next door.

Selene knelt beside Berta.

“I heard you,” she said.

“Then don't leave with a better explanation.”

Upstairs, Tomás took one look at Selene's face and put his notebook away.

They returned on the dry ledge. Nara escorted them to the stair. At the blocked arch she stopped, one hand resting on the fresh mortar.

“This used to be an exit,” she said.

“I know.”

“No. I mean I used it. Every day. We carried chairs through here when the lower hall flooded.”

She took her hand away.

Above them, the orchard's gold light lay across the stair like the edge of another world.
