The bull had eaten the notice explaining why it was safe.

One corner remained between his teeth. The rest of the paper, including the Academy seal and the reassuring phrase accustomed to human company, had disappeared into a mouth large enough to contain Selene's hand.

“That doesn't mean friendly,” she said.

“I know.”

The young man beside her climbed another rung of the fence.

Across the courtyard, the bull lowered his head. Rain had darkened his cream-coloured hide along the spine. Each horn curved outward before turning forward, and the broken lead rope swung below his jaw like a piece of unfinished business. Six students stood between him and the open cattle gate. Beyond the gate, two farmhands were trying to turn a herd off the road before the arriving carts divided it.

Selene had spent the morning practising attention with a bowl of water. The bowl, she felt, had not prepared her adequately.

“Nobody run,” called a man on the far side of the yard.

He said it without shouting. People heard him anyway.

He was holding a rolled coat in one hand. With the other he pointed the students toward the feed shed, making a path with his arm rather than walking into the bull's path himself. He had dark hair cut unevenly at the nape and a small scar that interrupted one eyebrow. A cart blocked the shed door.

“You in the green scarf. Can you move that?”

The student in the scarf stared at him.

“The cart. Leave the bull to the people with worse judgement.”

That got him moving.

The bull's destello shivered against his neck, low red under a pale sheet of gold. Selene resisted the old impulse to name the colours. She watched his feet. One hind hoof lifted and settled awkwardly. The rope had wound around a projecting hinge.

“He's caught,” she said.

The man glanced at the hinge, then at her. “Can you open the small gate behind you?”

“Into the road?”

“Into the empty pen.”

“It isn't empty.”

He looked properly this time. Two calves were lying in the long grass behind the rails.

“Right.” He drew breath. “Then don't.”

It was a small correction, promptly made. She found herself liking him for it, which was not a useful thing to notice while standing in a bull's way.

Master Iolen emerged from the stone workshop carrying a length of fencing. His student had already taken the other end. Together they placed it between the feed shed and the bull, leaving a wide, curving exit toward the holding yard. A farmhand began lifting the heavy latch at that end.

The animal tried to turn. The hinge caught the rope shorter.

Selene saw the moment before he lunged: the weight travelling through his shoulders, the disturbed earth under the good hind foot. She stepped behind the upright post. Wood struck wood. Something cracked hard enough to silence the courtyard.

On the fence, the young man made a noise he would later claim had been a warning.

The stranger came alongside Selene on the safe side of the rails.

“I'm Damián,” he said.

“Is this really the moment?”

“In case you need to blame someone.”

He put his coat over the broken rail where splinters pointed into the passage. Then he drew a short knife. He waited until the bull shifted away from the hinge, leaned through the outer gap, and cut the rope where it lay against the iron.

The freed end snapped back. The bull swung his head. Damián was already behind the post, close enough that Selene smelled rain and leather and something faintly burnt.

They stayed there while the farmhands, speaking in the unhurried voices of people whose work this actually was, moved the bull through the opening. Nobody applauded. The latch dropped. A woman in a straw hat walked past the students, collected the uneaten corner of the notice, and looked at Iolen.

“You signed off this yard?”

“I did,” he agreed.

“Then inspect the rope next time.”

“Yes.”

She went to look at the bull's foot.

Selene let her back rest against the post. Her pulse continued as if nobody had told it the emergency was over.

“You all right?” Damián asked.

“Ask me something easier.”

He nodded toward the feed shed. “Coffee?”

“Much easier.”

Four months into her chosen life, Selene still sometimes expected instruction to announce itself. A teacher would raise a hand; a chamber would brighten; whatever she needed to understand would arrive wearing its importance. Instead there were ropes that should have been replaced, people who knew more than she did, and a Luminor who had once spent half a lesson making her admit she needed lunch.

She found Brío under the long stable roof, eating with the concentration he brought to most worthwhile activities. A dark horse occupied the adjoining stall. It had a black coat with a blue sheen where the light reached it, and one lower lip that hung in frank enjoyment while a groom scratched its chest.

“Tizón,” Damián said. “He likes to make a dignified first impression.”

Brío lifted his head long enough to inspect the stranger's pocket.

“No,” Selene told him.

“I haven't offered.”

“Your hand was halfway into your pocket.”

“Is that how it works? Can you tell what people are about to do?”

She looked up sharply.

He stopped smiling. “Renata said you read light. I didn't mean—”

“She should stop introducing me with the interesting part.”

“What should she start with?”

Selene considered him. The question had no obvious correct answer, which made it a better one than most people asked after hearing about her sight.

“I make books look as if somebody cared where the words went.”

“Useful. My crew's route book looks as if it lost an argument with a roof.”

“It probably did.”

“Three roofs.”

She laughed. Brío, having determined that nobody was feeding him, returned to his hay.

Damián led the eastern passage crew. He told her this while carrying two chipped cups from the kitchen, and with less ceremony than another man might have used to explain his coat. His work involved surveying walking corridors and repairing waystations. He was also back at the Academy for Flow instruction.

“My teacher says I turn every current into an obstacle.”

“Do you?”

“I tell her they're badly positioned.”

His smile was quick. It left something tired behind.

They sat on the stable step. Beyond the yard, cattle moved through grass beaded with rain. Yellow birds worked the fence posts. Farther off stood the great orchids of Avilara, their pale throats tilted toward a sun that had not yet cleared the clouds. Water collected along a petal's edge until its weight pulled the whole flower down. A sheet of rain fell from it, briefly luminous, and splashed into a trough deep enough for three horses to drink abreast.

Selene thought of the office lamp she had left on her desk in Caracas. Her mother had sent a picture of it beside a note asking whether plants could develop opinions about being neglected. Selene had written back that the lamp had never been a plant. Her mother had sent a second picture: the actual plant, unmistakably offended.

She had an answer waiting in her pocket. She liked having an answer waiting. For years, leaving a message unanswered had felt like proof of some defect; now she could sometimes let it be a thing she would do after coffee.

“You're far away,” Damián said.

“My mother's interrogating a fern.”

“Should I ask?”

“Probably not.”

He took that easily too.

Then Renata came through the yard with a courier bag over her shoulder, and Damián set his cup down so quickly that coffee ran over his fingers.

Selene saw his light draw tight along his wrists. She kept her eyes on his face.

Renata placed the bag between them. One strap had been repaired with green thread. There was no blood on it, no sign of a blade. The wax seal remained whole.

“Where?” he asked.

“The east watch found it at the end of the old causeway. No horse. No footprints beyond the stone.”

He touched the green thread.

“That's Leda's.”

Renata opened the flap. Inside were three folded shirts, a tin of salve, and the route book. Its cover had indeed lost an argument with a roof. When she turned the first page, Selene saw clean rectangles where names should have been.

Someone had scraped the ink away without cutting the paper.

“All six?” Damián asked.

Renata did not answer at once.

The rain started again, lightly. In the next stall Tizón shifted his weight, and Damián turned toward the familiar sound as if it might contain an instruction.

“We leave today,” he said.

“You left three days early last time,” Renata replied.

Selene looked from one to the other.

Damián's hand stayed on the green thread. “Then this time someone else can choose the hour.”
