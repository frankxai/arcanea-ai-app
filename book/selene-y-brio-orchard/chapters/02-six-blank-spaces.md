Ada made them put the names back before she would look at the map.

“Leda,” Damián said, and wrote it on a clean sheet. “Marín. Dora. Teo. Berta. Simón.”

He wrote quickly until the last name. The pen remained against the paper long enough to leave a small blue swelling beneath the final letter.

“Who asked you to look for them?” Ada said.

“Their families.”

“All their families?”

“Berta's sister. Simón's husband. Leda's mother came to the watchhouse.”

“And the others?”

“I lead their crew.”

The Luminor sat back. She had a length of green thread wound around one finger, taken from the repaired bag. At the high window, a moth kept landing on the wrong side of the glass.

“That gives you an obligation,” she said. “It doesn't tell you what happened.”

“Six people don't scrape their names out of a register and walk away from their horses.”

“How do you know they walked?” Selene asked.

He turned toward her, annoyed, and for a moment she regretted speaking.

Then he looked down at the map.

“I don't.”

Tomás arrived carrying the missing crew's schedules. His wrist had healed with a stiffness he noticed most in wet weather. He set the papers down with his good hand, gave Selene a nod, and asked whether anyone had eaten. The question interrupted the room's developing taste for urgency. Ada ordered bread.

They learned more over the next hour than they had in the first ten minutes of being frightened. The six couriers had been assigned to a reopened passage beyond the Thorn Meridian. Two had asked to be moved. Leda's application contained a single sentence: I will not certify another crossing I have not inspected.

“She was always particular,” Damián said.

Selene glanced at him.

“Particular how?”

He rubbed a thumb over the edge of his cup. “About doing things properly.”

Nobody improved the answer for him.

The bag had come from the old causeway. The causeway lay five days east by the inhabited walking routes; farther, if Serevra refused them passage through the Meridian. Beneath it were older water channels, but those carried the deep current and its resonance, not travelers. Damián crossed out the blue line a student had drawn between two wells.

“You can send a measurement through that,” he said. “Not a person.”

Selene studied the remaining paths. Their familiar Avilaran valley occupied a small corner. Beyond it, three narrow crossings joined country she had never seen. No helpful border surrounded the difficult part.

Renata divided the work. She would maintain the watch at the first corridor and send messages west. Ada arranged a second party by the longer inhabited road, carrying supplies and a healer. Selene, Damián, and Tomás could move sooner if they traveled light. They would leave a dated record at every staffed station. If a corridor closed, they would stop improvising promises about the other side and report through the watch nearest them.

“Your job is to find out,” Ada told Selene. “Bring help to people who want it. Come back with what you actually know.”

Selene could feel Damián's urgency across the table. She could also feel her own. Six blank spaces looked like an invitation to prove she had been right to leave Caracas.

She wrote find out at the top of her page, underlined it once, then put a question mark beside the word rescue.

Afterward, Tovan found Damián behind the stable, trying to move water from a bucket to a trough without spilling it. A narrow arc rose between his palms, trembled, and slapped itself against the trough's edge. Most of it landed on his boots.

“You're tightening your fingers again,” Tovan said.

“They're attached to the rest of me.”

“Yes. A continuing difficulty.”

Selene stayed in the doorway. Damián noticed her and began the working again, more forcefully. The water climbed higher. So did his shoulders.

“Stop,” Tovan said.

He did not stop immediately. The arc broke against his sleeve.

The Luminor waited until Damián lowered his hands.

“How much of that was for the trough?”

Damián looked at Selene. She wished Tovan had chosen a less observant moment.

“Most of it,” he said.

Tovan took the bucket and poured.

Later, as they checked the packs, Damián explained that he had opened Foundation, Flow, and Fire. Three Gates made him a Mage. They had not made him equally competent at all three.

“I can heat a rivet without burning the hand that holds it,” he said. “I can brace stone. Water objects to my attitude.”

“It seems to be expressing that clearly.”

“You're enjoying this.”

“A little.”

He grinned, but there was colour in his face. She liked that too. It was becoming inconvenient.

Her own working that afternoon was less impressive. Iolen asked her to hold the two sides of a cracked pack buckle together while he fastened the repair. She found the weight of the metal in her attention, let it settle against the pressure of her fingers, and held. Three heartbeats. Four. A fine tremor began beneath her thumbnail.

“Enough,” she said.

Iolen finished with an ordinary clamp.

She had once imagined that progress would feel like discovering the part of herself that could do everything. More often it was discovering a little sooner when to use the clamp.

At the stable, she checked Brío's back with her palm before laying on the saddle. She knew the small rough patch behind his shoulder and the place where he leaned toward a scratch. His familiar dark coat was warm beneath her hand. He had carried her through enough rain that she could recognise him by the smell of wet mane before she reached his stall.

Renata brought a different saddle blanket.

“Thicker?” Selene asked.

“Cleaner. Great discoveries are sometimes very simple.”

They checked grain, salt, spare straps, dry cloth, and enough food for the uninteresting days. Selene packed the shutters that narrowed her peripheral sight, her ordinary notebook, and the cold stone at her chest. The lens had not changed its habits to suit the urgency of six missing people. Two breaths of clarity, then a day's warmth. She would have to choose when to use it.

Before bed she answered her mother. She described the actual assignment, named the people going with her, and gave the route station through which messages would come. She did not mention the bull. Then, after a moment, she added the bull and the broken rope and the fact that she had stayed behind a post.

Her mother's reply arrived the next morning while they were tightening the last straps.

I appreciate the post. Stay near other sensible objects.

Selene folded the letter into the dry inner pocket.

Beyond the stable, the road lay under a low grey sky. Damián was waiting with Tizón, black against the pale grass. Tomás checked the strap on their spare packhorse. Nobody asked Selene to lead because of her gift. They asked whether she was ready.

She put a foot in the stirrup and swung into the saddle.

Brío turned his head toward the feed room.

“You have eaten,” she told him.

He considered the weakness of this argument, then followed Tizón onto the road.
