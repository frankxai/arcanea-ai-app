There was room beneath the ruined roof for the horses or for three reasonably dry people.

The horses won.

Tomás found an old bench and slept under it, wrapped in their least wet blanket. Selene sat against a column with her knees drawn up. Damián held his burned hand in a basin while rain made dark circles on the stone around his boots.

“How bad?” she asked.

“I can still—”

“That wasn't the question.”

He looked at the hand.

“Bad enough that I shouldn't use it tomorrow.”

“Good. Now say it to someone who might stop you.”

“You're becoming very inconvenient company.”

She smiled before she could help it.

They divided the oats, checked the horses' legs, and hung the wet straps wherever the roof would permit. Damián did his work with his left hand. Once, fastening a buckle defeated him. Selene waited until he asked.

The rain thickened. Somewhere in the dark, a frog made a sound like a small wooden wheel turning. Brío chewed hay with an expression of deep private importance.

“Did you always ride?” Damián asked.

“When we went to the plains. Then lessons, when I could pay for them.”

“What do you remember first?”

She had expected a question about fear. People often arrived there quickly, once they knew about the destellos.

“A woman repairing a gate,” she said. “Everybody else was talking about the horses. She was saying the hinge would kill someone. Nobody listened until she took the gate off.”

“What happened?”

“They listened.”

He laughed, quietly because Tomás was asleep.

She remembered other things: a calf leaning its whole weight against her shin; dust in the seam of a saddle; the immense seriousness with which a child could be entrusted to carry one bucket. She had liked being useful there. She had also liked going home, bathing, putting on clean clothes, and having a city outside the window. Neither pleasure had cancelled the other.

Damián worked a strip of cloth around his hand.

“My first horse stepped on me.”

“That explains the coat.”

He looked down at the long, badly repaired hem.

“What's wrong with the coat?”

“Nothing, if you need to survey a chimney from inside.”

He looked affronted for nearly three seconds.

Then he laughed properly, and Tomás, beneath the bench, said, “Both of you could be less happy about being wet.”

Later Selene helped empty Damián's map case onto a dry board. They had recovered three folded sheets from the bank after Orryx. A fourth was trapped in the case's lining. She loosened it with the end of a spoon.

The seal was his. Beneath it she read a sentence certifying the eastern approach fit for passage.

The date was older than Leda's refusal.

“Is this the crossing she wouldn't sign?”

He took the sheet too quickly.

Rain tapped the basin.

“Part of it.”

“Which part?”

“I'll tell you when we've found them.”

She waited for him to hear himself.

He did. She saw it in the way he set the paper down again.

“I need to put it in order,” he said.

“All right. Don't put me in the place of someone who agrees to that order.”

The small warmth between them did not vanish. That would have been easier. It stayed, making the silence awkward and alive.

Near dawn, Selene woke to a cough.

Tomás was still beneath the bench. Damián had fallen asleep sitting upright, his bandaged hand resting open on his knee. The cough came again from the dark below the ruined wall.

She took the lantern and went to the edge.

Wet leaves caught its light. Beyond them she could see the ordinary living glimmers that crowded a forest, too many and too close together. A human-sized dimness lay under a fallen branch, but she could not separate it from the thick blue glow of a flowering vine.

She lifted the stone from beneath her blouse.

One breath. The overlapping colours drew apart. A shoulder, a bent arm, the quick uneven movement of a chest.

Second breath. She fixed the location against a split root.

The lens cooled in her hand. The colours crowded back together.

“Damián.”

He woke at the sound of her voice and was beside her before she repeated it.

They found a young man caught between the root and the wall, one ankle swollen above a torn shoe. A branch had trapped his coat. He had crawled toward the smell of their fire shortly before dawn. By then his throat was raw from calling into the storm.

“Teo,” Damián said.

The man closed his eyes.

“Don't be pleased yet.”

They cut the coat free. Tomás brought a dry cloth and the last of their bread. Teo ate before he explained anything.

He had left the orchard two nights earlier, carrying a message for the inhabited station. He had been trying to avoid the fresh cart road when the bank slipped.

“The bag?” Selene asked.

“Mine. I sent it down with a trader a week before I tried walking out. She couldn't take a letter. Said paper was being searched. I thought you'd recognise it.”

“Why were the names gone?”

“They take the pages with names. Sometimes they scrape them. Depends how much of the book they want to keep.”

“Who does?”

Teo looked from Damián to Selene.

“The Palace.”

Damián leaned forward. “Are the others alive?”

“When I left. Berta's being sent below. Simón's already there.”

“And Leda?”

Teo wiped bread from his mouth with the back of his hand.

“Leda doesn't want to come home with you.”

The rain thinned. Beyond the wall, the first ordinary light of morning found the wet roots.

“Then let her tell me,” Damián said.

Teo considered him for a long time.

“You can come,” he said at last. “But when she tells you, you listen.”
