Leda had already hidden the seed trays when Selene returned.

“The lower work party moves at dawn,” she said. “Ors is opening another wet seam. They need people small enough to get through the access cut.”

“Berta?”

“And Simón. Twelve others.”

Selene set the broken piece of railing on the table. She had carried it all the way up without deciding to. Now the splintered limestone left white dust beside the bread.

Damián read the transfer notice twice.

“We need the watch here before morning.”

“Then you should have brought it,” Leda said.

Tomás unrolled the route paper. Ada's second party would be at the inhabited station by now, if the longer road had held. From there they could reach the orchard by a walking corridor and the old cart path in less than a day. They did not know the final turn.

Selene looked at the bell frame above the terraces.

She had thought it marked work hours. A narrow speaking tube ran from its base into the rock.

“Does that reach the station?”

“The relay does,” Inés said. “The bell gets an operator to listen.”

“Then we tell them.”

Leda laid a hand across the map.

“Ors controls the repair crews. He hears the public relay.”

“Not the watch channel,” Damián said.

She looked at him.

“You inspected that too?”

He went still.

The room seemed to narrow around the question.

“The watch channel had a separate board,” he said. “I saw it.”

“When?”

“Before this crew left.”

Selene could hear water moving beneath the floor. She could see Berta saying yes. Simón letting the chest slip, then being afraid of what it would cost.

“We could send a runner,” Tomás said. “It's slower. We can't guarantee dawn.”

“They'll be below by then.”

“Yes,” he said. “They might.”

He did not soften it. She was grateful and angry with him at the same time.

Inés pulled the map back toward herself.

“You cannot give out this path without asking the people it leads to.”

They asked. There were arguments in three places at once. Some wanted the watch immediately. Some wanted everyone to leave by the northern footpath, though Teo could barely stand and the oldest residents could not manage the steps before dawn. Dora offered to go as runner. Marín wanted to carry Berta out through the Palace's upper stair and fight anyone who stopped him.

Selene remembered how the water had repeated a single blow.

“We'd bring the fighting into the registry,” she said.

“She's already being hit there,” Marín answered.

No plan came clean.

At sunset Nara climbed from the lower stair with a folded message. Simón had been moved to the loading hall. The transfer would begin during the night. They were advancing it because visitors had asked questions.

Selene took the message from her.

For the next minute she could hear everyone too clearly: Damián, Inés, Leda, the runner's quick breathing, a child somewhere asking for a cup. Their words arrived without room between them. She wanted a stillness she could use. Instead she counted the rough stones under her fingers and made herself read the message again.

She went to the bell.

Inés followed her.

“Selene.”

“I know you're refusing.”

“Then you know what you're doing.”

Selene took the cord.

“I know what happens if nobody comes.”

She rang.

The sound crossed the orchard. In the paddock, Brío threw up his head. Tomás went to him and spoke low while Selene fitted the watch channel's brass plate into the relay.

A voice answered after the third call.

She gave her name, Ada's assignment mark, the number of people facing transfer, and the route from the inhabited station. At the last turn she hesitated.

“Repeat the final approach,” the voice said.

She did.

When she came down, Inés was already emptying the seed cabinet into canvas rolls.

“How long?” Selene asked.

“Until we know who heard you?”

Selene nodded.

“We'll know when they arrive.”

The first lamps appeared on the cart road after midnight.

There were too many for Ada's party, and they were moving too quickly. Harness bells sounded beneath the trees. Men with brown circles on their sleeves came through the dead trunk two at a time.

Carden Ors walked at the centre of them, carrying no weapon Selene could see.

He wore a weatherproof coat, well cut and clean at the cuffs. A registrar went beside him with a folding desk. The ordinary preparation of it frightened Selene more than a drawn sword would have done.

“The watch will be delayed,” he announced. “There is damage on the eastern road.”

Damián stepped down from the terrace.

“What damage?”

Ors looked at his bandaged hand.

“Captain Arocha. I thought I recognised the assignment mark.”

Behind him, two men began unloading chains for the carts.

Selene looked toward the relay. High in the bell frame, the brass plate she had trusted caught a little light.

Ors followed her glance.

“All the channels go through the same repair shed now,” he said. “Much easier to maintain.”
