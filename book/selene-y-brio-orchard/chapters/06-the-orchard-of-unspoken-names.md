They entered the orchard through the roots of a dead tree.

Teo led them to a hollow large enough for a horse. On the other side, a path bent behind a rock face and opened above a valley full of gold.

Selene stopped walking.

High overhead hung pairs of translucent membranes, each broad as a sail. Sunlight struck them and turned downward, gathering beneath the canopy in long amber rooms. Between the trunks rose blue flowers with throats the colour of banked coals. Water descended through terraced gardens. Laundry moved above a stone bridge. Someone was arguing about onions.

After days of stone and rain and the terrible effort of not arriving, the ordinary argument nearly undid her.

Brío lowered his head toward a bank of grass. She let him stand. Across a shallow pond, three capybaras watched the newcomers with less interest than they deserved.

“Those came through a walking corridor with a seed caravan,” Teo said. “Inés has opinions about the caravan.”

“Because they escaped?”

“Because nobody thought to ask what they eat.”

The woman who met them at the first terrace had soil up to her wrists and grey in her dark hair. She looked at Teo's ankle, then at the horses, then at the three strangers.

“You've brought four problems,” she told him.

“I've brought three people.”

“I was counting your foot.”

Inés Roa took them to a covered workroom. Before asking what Selene could see, she asked whether she could hold a basin without tipping it. Selene could. The basin proved more useful than her gift while Inés washed Teo's ankle.

Leda arrived carrying a wooden tray of seedlings.

Damián stood.

She set the tray down carefully before looking at him.

“You came yourself.”

“Of course.”

“That wasn't always the expensive part.”

He remained standing. Selene pretended to be occupied with the basin, then stopped pretending and gave them space.

Outside, Inés showed her where to put the horses. Brío's scrape was clean, but he still disliked pressure near it. He could stay in the lower paddock. Inés sent a runner to the village animal keeper and told Selene where the ordinary feed was stored.

“Don't let them eat the blue flowers.”

“Poison?”

“Expensive.”

The plants had names, and histories less convenient than their beauty. The amber sails belonged to Sombraluz trees, grown from several Veldorian seed families. Their paired fruit membranes sent light beneath the canopy, at a cost to their own growth. The straight, uniform plantation beyond the old wall had grown faster. It kept its light overhead. Nothing useful grew beneath it now.

“We took the wrong cuttings for three seasons,” Inés said. “You can see the mistake from here. Lovely trees. Terrible orchard.”

She touched the base of a blue flower.

“Floración Azul. Keep a steady note for an ordinary breath.”

Selene hummed. The flower's throat gathered a blue-white ring. When her note wavered, the ring broke.

“It listens,” she said.

“And stops growing while it does. Don't make it listen all afternoon.”

Selene tried one short phrase of an old song her mother liked. Nothing happened. Then she held a single note and the ring returned.

“It prefers me without words.”

“Many things do,” Inés said, and smiled for the first time.

They ate at a long table beneath the sails. Dora brought bowls; Marín brought a loaf and a complaint about the oven; Teo sat with his ankle propped on a stool. Four of the six blank spaces had faces now, and all four were arguing about how much salt the soup needed.

The other two places stayed empty.

Leda waited until everyone had food.

“We came because the road crew was being made to certify passages we hadn't seen,” she said. “Ors paid the contracts. He kept our wages against the cost of equipment. If we left, he called it theft.”

Damián started to speak.

She held his gaze.

He stopped.

“A woman offered us shelter here. Work we could choose. Names Ors couldn't use to follow us. We thought we could stay until the claims were settled.”

“Vesrane?” Inés asked.

Leda nodded.

The name changed the table. Marín broke his bread into smaller pieces. Dora looked toward the path leading beneath the orchard.

“Berta wanted to leave,” Leda said. “They said her original name couldn't be returned without exposing the person whose name she'd borrowed. Simón asked for his too. He's been working in the flooded registry since.”

“Who makes him?” Selene asked.

“Vesrane's officers hold the papers. Ors's men hold the outside roads. Each says the other is the reason.”

Ors's carts used the Palace's public loading ramp from the valley road. They had never found the hidden upper approach to the orchard. His repair depot lay only three hours away by the old cart road; the path through the dead tree was the part he lacked.

The orchard wasn't enclosed. Selene could see three paths from the table. It had taken her too long to understand why that hadn't answered the question.

“What do you want?” she asked Leda.

“For Berta to leave if she wants. For Simón to see his husband. For you not to send my mother a route to this table.”

Damián looked down.

“She thinks you're dead.”

“Then tell her I'm alive.”

“She'll ask where.”

“You don't have to give everyone everything they ask for.”

Later, Selene sat beside the lower pond with her notebook closed. The destellos were crowded here: people, horses, trees, small hungry things inside the reeds. Her attention kept snapping toward every movement. The more she tried to settle it, the more she noticed.

Inés sat on the low wall beside her.

“Which part are you trying to do?”

“All of it, apparently.”

“Would you like a task?”

She gave Selene three empty seed bowls.

“Hear the water under the nearest bridge. When you notice you've gone somewhere else, move a bowl.”

“Only three?”

“I need the rest.”

Selene moved all three almost immediately. Inés turned them back over without comment.

They sat until the sound beneath the bridge became distinct from the water farther down the terraces. Selene still saw the crowded light. It no longer required her to follow each part of it.

At dusk, a registrar came up the lower path carrying a porcelain token on a black thread.

“For the visitor from Avilara,” she said. “Vesrane will receive you in the morning.”

Selene did not take it yet.

“Under whose name?”

The registrar turned the token over.

On its pale back, in a precise hand, was written a name Selene had never heard.
