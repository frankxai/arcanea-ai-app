The next morning Brío would not let her put on the halter.

He did not bolt. He moved his head away, once, then again. When Selene reached over his poll, the skin beneath her fingers twitched.

She put the halter down.

His shoulder wound had closed. The warmth she had first noticed there had not gone, and now a tender ridge lay beneath the forelock. It was small enough that she might have missed it if he had been willing to work.

The animal keeper came before breakfast. His name was Salvat. He had the patient, unsentimental manner of a person who had spent years being stepped on by things he was helping.

He examined Brío in the paddock, watched him walk, checked the wound, then examined it again under clearer light.

“No riding,” he said. “No pressure across this part of the head. If he wants to walk, let him choose the pace.”

“What is it?”

“Something I haven't identified.”

She disliked the answer enough to trust it.

He took a few loose hairs, a scraping from the old pack frame, and a sample of the green dust that remained inside its split wood. Then he asked Inés about the botanical terraces.

“Living mineral,” she said. “Some of the old beds were grafted with Laeylinn Jade. I have the planting records. I don't know which seams survived.”

“Bring the records. Not the mineral.”

She nodded.

Salvat did not offer a grand name for the change. He moved Brío into a shaded pen beside the pasture, where they could watch whether he ate, slept, and moved comfortably. Tizón stayed in the neighbouring pen until Damián needed him. When Brío fretted at the separation, they opened the connecting gate.

Selene had been meant to scout the cart road before joining the registry party. Now she stood holding an unused saddle while people carried tools past her toward the stairs.

“I could go on foot,” she said.

Inés looked up from the samples.

“You could. Who stays with him?”

“Someone else.”

“Who?”

She had no name ready.

Selene put the saddle away. Leda took the scouting round and gave its packing work to Marín. Selene handed over her route notes, including the places she had not checked. The plan changed hands and continued.

The delay felt shameful at first. Beyond the wall, people were risking the northern trail. Simón was still below. She could hear tools striking mortar where Tomás had begun measuring the old arch, pretending to inspect a drain. And here was Brío, wanting familiar company and refusing to understand how badly she needed him to be easy.

She sat on the ground outside his pen.

After a while he came to stand near her.

“All right,” she said.

He breathed warm air over the top of her head.

Nothing in the world resolved.

By noon she had cleaned his water, cut a new cloth to keep the flies from the tender shoulder, and helped Salvat record the temperature of the skin at the same places as before. She had also noticed a way through the stable yard that the guards could not see from the main terrace.

Inés used it to move the last seed rolls.

Damián came at dusk, dusty to the elbows. He had helped Tomás clear the outside face of the blocked arch. Two courses of stone remained, held by a narrow iron brace. Ors's guard had accepted their explanation about drainage because the guard did not want to stand in water either.

“We're leaving the final stones until the people below are ready,” he said.

He set a bowl beside her.

“You forgot lunch.”

“So did you.”

“Mine is behind my back, where I hoped you wouldn't notice how much bread I took.”

She moved over on the wall.

For a little while they ate without discussing anyone's failures. The orchard's amber sails darkened above them. In the uniform grove beyond the wall, the last light stayed trapped high in the branches; under the mixed trees, people were still putting tools away by its redirected glow.

“When this is done,” he said, “I'm going back to every crossing on that certificate.”

“With Leda?”

“If she wants the work. With someone who can contradict me if she doesn't.”

“You'll need a shorter coat.”

He looked at the hem, now stiff with mortar.

“I was hoping that criticism had expired.”

She laughed, and the sound startled her. It had room in it for the things that were still wrong.

Later a small group reached the rest camp above the northern trail. Dora had found Ada's party at the inhabited station. Ors had ordered a supposed safety closure on the cart road, holding them there. The warning and the footpath gave them a way around it. They were bringing supplies and witnesses down in stages; the healer would stay at the camp. The local watch would follow once the detained road officers could get their people free of the false closure.

It would take another day.

Below the orchard, Vesrane delayed the transfer again, this time refusing Ors's carts entry to her loading hall while records were under review. Nara had made Berta's struck arm impossible to leave out of the account. The protection was narrow and temporary. They used it.

On the following evening Salvat said Brío could stay comfortably with him while Selene went below. He had brought the horse's feed himself and knew where to scratch without hurting him. Brío had accepted both facts.

Selene knelt beside her bags and packed no saddle equipment.

Under the lifted mane, against the old black hair, she found a tiny pale tuft that had not been there before. Salvat examined it without pulling.

“We'll mark the date,” he said.

She wrote it in the notebook beside the first measurements.

Nara had arranged the entry for the next morning. Selene spent that last evening with Brío and slept in the keeper's spare room. On the fifteenth day of the journey, she put on dry boots and went to meet the people waiting for her at the stair.
