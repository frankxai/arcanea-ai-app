The arrow went through Damián's map.

He had unfolded it against a survey post. A moment later the paper was fastened to the wood, the white feather trembling beside his hand.

“That was a boundary warning,” a woman said above them. “Tell me your names before you decide what the next one is.”

Selene could see the bow first, then the woman holding it. A short cloak lay against one brown shoulder. Pale strips of bark had been worked into the cloth, almost invisible against the branch. Nothing about the arrow suggested she had missed.

“Damián Arocha. Eastern routes.”

“Your routes end two posts behind you.”

“The Meridian Survey has—”

“A lovely seal. Yes.”

He closed his mouth.

Selene gave her name. Tomás gave his. Brío put his ears back at something under the aqueduct, where water threaded a green seam between the stones.

“Serevra?” Selene asked.

The woman lowered the bow a little.

“You've heard something useful, then.”

They dismounted behind the post. Farther ahead, an abandoned survey cut ran straight through the forest. Across it wound a much older aqueduct, changing direction with the ravine. Beyond the trees stood a white watch platform. Selene had seen it an hour earlier and assumed they were nearly there. Now it seemed farther away.

Serevra descended by a rope whose knots were darkened by years of hands. There was another rope beyond her, and a narrow shelf where someone could disappear while an intruder was still looking at the first.

She examined their letter without touching its seal.

“Six couriers,” she said. “Five came through my camp.”

Damián moved toward her. She raised a finger. He stopped.

“One was waiting beyond the crossing,” she said. “They left together. None was tied.”

“When?”

“Nine days ago. One argued with the woman who'd come to meet them. The little one with a cut under her chin.”

“Berta,” Damián said.

“She wanted to turn back. The others said the return road was watched.”

Selene took out her notebook. The word rescue, underlined on the first page, seemed suddenly too large.

“Who was watching it?”

“Men paid by Carden Ors. They collect passage debts. Sometimes the debts exist.”

“And sometimes?”

Serevra looked at the map pinned to the post.

“Paper is accommodating.”

The survey's claim to the crossing was older than hers. The crossing itself was not. She and two others had rebuilt it after the slope failed, hauling out survivors while survey officers debated whose stores they were permitted to use. Now she charged for passage and maintained two shelters. Damián could dispute the first fact. He could see the second.

The price she asked was a morning's work replacing a support rope. They had rope; she had none fit for the load. Damián looked at the westering light and began calculating another route.

“Don't,” Tomás said. “I can see you moving the mountains closer.”

They slept in the marked shelter. At dawn they worked.

Serevra showed them where to put their feet, which knots had shifted in the rain, which apparently sound plank had a dark softness underneath. Brío stayed on the bank with Tizón and the packhorse. When Selene led him toward the finished crossing, he stopped so hard that the rope slid warm across her palm.

She looked down for a loose board.

From under the next pier came a scrape like a blade drawn along a plate.

Serevra dropped flat.

“Back. Quietly.”

Damián had already stepped onto the stone. He froze. Beneath him, a hook rose over the lip of the pier. Moss fell from the roots. Another hook followed. Between them appeared a low armoured head, olive and wet, beneath a tall thorax jointed like a folded siege ladder.

Selene's mind tried to give the thing a face. It had none she could use.

“Orryx,” Serevra said. “Don't stamp.”

One of its four walking legs found the underside of the pier. Then another. The two blade limbs opened above the head. Damián's boot scraped stone.

The creature snapped upward.

He flung himself back. A hooked limb struck the space his calf had occupied, shearing the map case from his belt. Paper burst over the ravine. Brío wheeled, dragging Selene two steps before she let the lead run and got clear of his shoulder.

The horses scattered onto the bank. Tomás caught the packhorse behind a tree. Tizón stood trembling, his reins caught on a low fork.

Orryx pulled itself higher. Its rear legs scrabbled without purchase.

“Now,” Serevra said.

Damián crawled away while the legs searched. Selene wanted to reach for him. She was on the wrong side of a root, too far away, and reaching would only put another moving body above the creature.

She stayed still until he reached the bank.

Across the ravine, on a shelf below the aqueduct, lay pale discarded armour. Beside it, in a bowl of packed leaves, several dull oval shapes held the first light of morning.

“A clutch,” Serevra said. “They weren't there last week.”

“We can't cross?” Tomás asked.

“Not on that pier.”

Orryx struck the hanging map case, pinned it, and drew it beneath the stone.

Serevra studied the old water channel. A shutter had jammed it above the bend. Open, it would send water against a hollow slab on the far pier, away from the nursery. That drumming might draw Orryx toward the empty stone and cover the smaller vibrations of feet downstream. Someone would have to reach the shutter from the outer shelf.

Damián looked at Selene.

“I can fit,” she said. “But you'll have to tell me where it puts its legs.”

He took off his coat and wound it around the root at her waist. It was a tether of last resort, short and awkward. She checked it herself.

The shelf was colder than she expected. She moved sideways with the stone against her chest, her boots feeling for each depression. Below, Orryx worried the map case. A leaf slid from the clutch and turned slowly in the air.

“Rear foot coming up,” Damián said.

She stopped. The jointed walking foot scraped beneath her boot. She could hear her breath hitting the stone and coming back.

“It's settling. Wait.”

She waited.

Then the leg folded away.

The shutter had a wooden handle swollen with water. She pulled. Nothing. Pulled again and felt a sharp grain enter her palm.

“Don't fight it,” Serevra called softly. “Lift first.”

Selene lifted, then pulled. The handle moved half an inch.

Water struck the remote slab with a deep, irregular drumming. Orryx flattened against the stone, then turned toward the sound. The nursery stayed dry. Selene stumbled as the coat tightened around her waist, then found her footing and came back one step at a time.

The downstream root bridge had a timber deck wide enough for one horse, built for the shelter's supply animals. Serevra and Damián checked its lashings, pressed each board, and crossed with the packs before bringing the horses. They led them separately. Nobody rode. Serevra watched the nursery until the last hoof touched the far bank.

At the white platform, Damián gave her the rest of their good rope.

“We'll mark the crossing closed for the season,” he said.

“Write why.”

He did.

Before they left, she drew a turn in Selene's notebook.

“The woman who met your couriers had a green repaired bag. Go through the old botanical terraces. If you find fresh cart ruts, leave them.”

“You could come,” Damián said.

Serevra glanced back at the two shelters, the rope they had replaced, the clutch below the pier.

“So could everyone.”

She retrieved the arrow from his ruined map and walked back into the trees.
