The first person to call Brío a unicorn was a child carrying turnips.

“He is,” the child insisted.

Salvat looked over the paddock fence.

“He is also standing on the feed scoop.”

The child considered the two facts, found them compatible, and went on her way.

Brío's coat had changed through two uneven molts. Dark patches had lingered along his flank and knees while the new hair grew pale. His mane had come through silver-white at the roots, then slowly along its length. The small white star that had once distinguished him was difficult to find now unless Selene knew exactly where to look.

She did.

The growth beneath his forelock had hardened into a short horn, gently turned, its base protected by the shape of the hair. It did not shine when he was brave. It did not cure the old ache in his shoulder. Salvat checked it each month, alongside his teeth and feet, and kept records of the days when pressure or bright light bothered him.

The old botanical samples had contained Laeylinn Jade. That much the Academy's mineral workers could establish. Whether it had awakened something inherited, produced a new harmonic change, or merely accompanied another cause remained under study. Nobody had repeated the exposure to find out.

Brío had spent the worst weeks in a quiet pasture. Selene had learned to sleep through most of the night again once he could. The new bridle left the horn's base clear, and the saddle had been refitted after he was comfortable carrying a rider. Their rides were short, on known ground. There would be no more courier loads and no more dangerous crossings asked of him because he had once managed them.

He was very good at the new work of finding grass.

From the stable yard came a disagreement about a measuring line.

“It's crooked,” Leda said.

“The wall is crooked,” Damián answered.

“Then write that.”

Selene smiled and went to see.

They were checking the new ramp before anyone opened the upper stalls. Damián had finished the route reinspection three weeks earlier, under another captain's signature. He had lost authority to certify crossings alone. The pay deductions for his share of the repair would continue through the next wet season. Leda had accepted two surveying contracts with him and refused a third because she wanted to spend that month elsewhere.

His hand had healed enough for work. In cold weather he still had to loosen it before closing a buckle.

The coat was shorter.

“I see you've made one serious improvement,” Selene said.

“It was becoming difficult to ignore the number of people stepping on it.”

“Wise to consult the public.”

Leda passed her the measuring line.

“Hold this before you encourage him.”

There had been hearings. There were still hearings.

Ors's control of the relay depot and the disputed roads had been suspended. The record of coerced labour had survived in enough hands that his accounts could no longer settle the matter by themselves. Several workers had been paid from seized contract funds; others were still arguing over amounts, missing hours, injuries, and who had known what.

Vesrane had given evidence against him and been required to answer for her own orders. The Palace's return hearings now used registrars from different districts, with a traveler able to bring an independent witness. Its dry exits stayed open. Vesrane still knew the central thread better than anyone alive. She no longer held its keys alone.

Berta had taken work repairing saddlery near her sister. Her letters were short and entertaining, especially when someone sent a strap made for appearance rather than a horse. Simón was living with his husband and learning to set type with the hand that had healed imperfectly. Dora carried correspondence on a route whose operators she knew by name. Marín had become intolerable about receipts, according to everyone who owed him one.

Teo and Inés had moved the seed collection to two smaller gardens. The exposed orchard was still tended by residents who chose to remain, but its first season after the occupation had been poor. Whole beds had gone unwatered during the evacuation. Some plants would take years to replace.

Inés had written Selene once, asking for a set of labels legible in rain. Selene made them. She sent the design for approval before printing the full set.

The reply contained three corrections and no forgiveness she had not asked for.

They were corresponding again.

At the Academy, Selene still lost concentration. Her Foundation work had become steadier, though not spectacular. She still could not see her own light in a mirror, and the lens had not discovered a hidden setting. On difficult days she used the shutters, sat somewhere quieter, and asked a teacher a question she would once have tried to make sound cleverer.

She also worked. A small studio in Caracas paid her for a set of book interiors. She went back for the review, stayed with her mother, argued about a cupboard, and spent an entire afternoon with friends who wanted to hear about the man before they wanted to hear about the Palace.

“There is a horse as well,” she said.

“There is always a horse,” Mercedes answered.

Now Damián finished checking the ramp and came to meet her by the open paddock.

Tizón stood beside Brío, black coat shining in the morning. The two horses had spent weeks perfecting a method of opening a loosely tied feed sack. Salvat had changed the knot. They were reconsidering their approach.

“I leave on Thursday,” Damián said.

“North road?”

“For six weeks. Perhaps eight, if the second crossing needs rebuilding.”

“Then say eight when you write.”

“I will.”

She rested her arms on the fence.

“I've taken the studio work through the end of the month. And Ada wants me in the next Foundation field class.”

“That sounds like you won't be coming to watch me argue with stone.”

“I'm going to be busy arguing with paper.”

He stood beside her, near enough that she could feel the warmth of his arm without either of them having to explain the distance.

“I would like to see you when I come back,” he said. “Without an emergency arranging it.”

She turned toward him.

“Yes.”

He smiled, then looked briefly down at his hands.

“I had prepared more of that.”

“You can save it.”

“For when?”

She took the front of his shorter coat lightly between her fingers.

“Later.”

He leaned closer, slowly enough for her to meet him. The kiss was warm and slightly awkward at first because she was smiling. Then she stopped thinking about whether she was smiling.

Behind them, a feed scoop struck the ground.

Brío lifted his head with an expression Selene had known long before he grew the horn.

“We are being supervised,” Damián said.

“Poorly.”

They took the horses out after Salvat had checked the morning's tenderness. The path crossed a broad meadow beneath the Academy terraces, then curved along a low ridge where the wind kept the grass moving. Selene rode with a loose rein. Brío walked beside Tizón, paused to examine something edible, and continued when he was satisfied it was beyond reach.

Above the far ridge, two winged silhouettes turned in a rising current. Selene could not tell how far away they were, or what carried them. For a moment she imagined Brío above the valley, a white body held on great wings, the road dropping away beneath them.

Then he stumbled over a tuft of grass because he was looking at Tizón's mouth.

She steadied herself, laughed, and let the imagined wings go for now.

At the bend, Damián waited. Beyond him lay the meadow, a line of flowering trees, and a morning they had not promised to anyone else.

Selene brought Brío alongside. She asked for a little more pace, felt his willing answer under her, and rode out into the wind.
