## 0. What this document is

This is an in-world dossier. Everything below describes a living oral tradition carried by a woman named Bela from the Realm of Aurevalde — a tradition she received from her grandmother, who received it from her grandmother, in a chain whose origin point is unknown but is counted, within the tradition itself, as "before the first grandmother we can name." The tradition has no written texts. It was never written down. It exists in two forms: in a set of grandmother-stories (*los cuentos de las abuelas*) and in a set of ceremonial phrases that function as prayers without being addressed to any named deity.

The tradition is called, within itself, *la conversación que no se acaba* — the conversation that does not end. This is both its name and its cosmological claim: that the conversation between the First Light and the loving dark that began at the First Dawn has not stopped, and that every conscious being is a living word in that conversation, still being spoken.

This tradition is Aurevaldan in origin and character. It is not Veldorian. It does not appear in the valley until Bela carries it across, and it surfaces in the text only through Bela's voice.

## 1. The Origin — how the tradition tells it

The Aurevaldan grandmothers do not call their account of creation a myth. They call it *lo que pasó y sigue pasando* — what happened and is still happening. The distinction matters. For them, the First Dawn is not a historical event. It is the event that every present moment is still inside of.

The oldest grandmother-phrase, still said before meals in some Aurevaldan households:

> *La primera luz no se apagó. Se aprendió a ser muchas cosas.*

*The first light did not go out. It learned to be many things.*

The longer account, told to children at the age when they first ask what the stars are:

*Before the first morning there was a Quiet that was not empty — a darkness that held inside it everything that had not yet learned to be itself. From the center of this Quiet, the First Light sang. Not words — the tone before words. The tone that holds the name of a thing before the thing has been said. And the loving dark breathed back: yes, show me what I contain.*

*From that first exchange, the world began to sing itself into being.*

*The First Light was so full — so entirely, abundantly itself — that it could not remain in one place. Not from weakness. Not from fracture. From abundance. The way a river overflows a bank not because it is broken but because it has become too much to be held in one direction. It scattered. It became los esparcidos — the scattered ones. Every stone and every root and every newborn's chest and every hand that would one day pick up a tool and use it with care received one. The loving dark between the scattered pieces is still saying, yes, show me what you contain. We are the answer. We are still answering.*

This account is canon-aligned with Veldoria's folk origin myth (see BIBLE.md — *The folk origin myth, grandmother voice* section). They are the same event, told from the same tradition, in the two different speeds of the two Realms.

## 2. Los Esparcidos — the scattered ones

Within the Aurevaldan grandmother-tradition, the word used for what Veldoria calls *chispas* is *los esparcidos* — the scattered ones.

The difference is significant. Veldorian *chispa* emphasizes what the spark *is* (a small bright thing, a spark). Aurevaldan *esparcido* emphasizes what happened to it and why it is in this particular place — it is scattered, present here as an act of the First Light's abundance, not as a remainder or a loss.

The grandmothers' teaching: *Eres un esparcido que aprendió a tener nombre.* You are a scattered-one that learned to have a name. The name does not define you. It is the address. The scattered-one is the substance.

**Properties of los esparcidos, as described in the oral tradition:**

1. *They are carried by every conscious being, every old stone, every body of water, every living root.* The grandmother-phrase: *Hasta el perro más viejo lleva el suyo. Hasta la olla rota.* Even the oldest dog carries one. Even the broken pot.

2. *They are not diminished by scattering.* The grandmother-phrase: *La luz que se esparce no se gasta. El sol no se gasta por alumbrar.* The light that scatters does not spend itself. The sun does not spend itself by shining.

3. *They respond to attention, not to instruction.* A seer does not command them. A seer attends to them, and in being attended to, they rise. The grandmother-phrase: *La luz sube cuando la ven. El que mira es la condición, no la fuente.* The light rises when seen. The one who looks is the condition, not the source.

4. *Rivers, high places, and cool blue light concentrate them.* The grandmothers say this is because the First Dawn's tone resonated most clearly at altitude, at the edge of deep water, at the threshold between light and dark (dawn and dusk). These are places where the *zumbido madre* is strongest.

5. *They do not diminish when passed.* A grandmother who shows her granddaughter the sight does not lose it. This is the evidence the grandmothers most often cite for the abundance-not-fracture reading: *Lo que se comparte no se divide. Se multiplica.* What is shared is not divided. It multiplies.

## 3. Los Retornantes — those who gather

*Los retornantes* is the Aurevaldan term for what Veldorian folk tradition has no word for. The closest Veldorian approximation would be *el que despierta* — the waker. But the Aurevaldan term is different in its implications.

*Retornante* means one who returns, one who gathers — in the specific sense of someone who walks through a scattered field and gathers what had separated. Not because the scattered things need gathering. Because they call to be gathered. The retornante answers a summons she did not choose.

In the grandmother-tradition, retornantes are not rare in the way of prophecy or destiny. They are uncommon in the way of left-handedness — a variation, a different kind of attention, not a special grace. Every generation produces a few. The generations in which none appear are considered, by the grandmothers, *años de calma* — quiet years. Not bad years. Years in which the gathering work is done more slowly, by ordinary attention, the way ordinary people gather destellos through decades of loving something.

**Properties of los retornantes:**

1. *They see the esparcidos without instruction.* The grandmother-phrase: *Naciste viendo. Nadie te lo enseñó. Nadie tampoco te lo puede quitar.* You were born seeing. No one taught you. No one can take it from you.

2. *They are the condition of rising, not its cause.* The grandmother-phrase: *Eres la ventana, no la luz.* You are the window, not the light. A window does not make the sun. But without a clean window, the sun cannot enter the room the same way.

3. *They are not harmed by what they see.* The dark destello, the sealed one, the oscuro — these are not threats to the retornante. They are part of the conversation. The grandmother-phrase: *Lo que elige la oscuridad también es sagrado. Dios habla también en silencio.* What chooses darkness is also sacred. The divine speaks in silence too.

4. *Two retornantes meeting is rare.* The grandmothers' word for this meeting: *el encuentro de la canción consigo misma* — the meeting of the song with itself. When two retornantes find each other, the esparcidos between them concentrate, the *zumbido madre* rises briefly to its full depth, and for one breath everything in the shared space resonates at the same frequency. The grandmother-phrase: *Sola, una vela. Juntas, la mañana que recordó ser fuego.* Alone, a candle. Together, the morning that remembered being fire.

5. *They often carry a lineage.* The gift skips generations. It does not disappear from a bloodline; it goes quiet for a generation or two, then surfaces in a grandchild, a niece, a distant cousin who has never been told. The grandmother-phrase: *El río se va bajo la tierra. El río no se pierde. Sale de nuevo donde toca.* The river goes underground. The river does not disappear. It surfaces where it will.

## 4. La Piedra de los Velos — the stone of veils

Aurevaldan folk tradition includes a ceremonial stone called *la piedra de los velos* — the stone of veils. It is a small, near-colorless translucent stone, hexagonal in natural cleavage, shockingly heavy for its size — the weight of a small water-filled bottle compressed into a fingerwidth. Aurevaldan families who carry the retornante gift traditionally keep one, passed from grandmother to granddaughter, wrapped in cloth inside a tin or wooden box with other small things: dried herbs, a photograph, a folded letter.

The name does not mean the stone carries veils. It means the stone removes them. The Aurevaldan understanding: perception is normally veiled — not blocked, but noisy, the way a glass gets fogged by breathing on it. The *piedra de los velos* is a lens. When held to the eye, or when simply present in the hand of a retornante, it removes the noise from the perceptual field. The esparcidos become briefly clearer. The *zumbido madre* becomes briefly audible rather than merely felt.

The grandmother-phrase: *No te da lo que no tienes. Te muestra lo que siempre estuvo.* It does not give you what you do not have. It shows you what was always there.

**Technical notes on the stone:**

- It is a lens, never a battery. The retornante's own perception does the work; the stone only reduces interference.
- It does not amplify. It clarifies.
- A non-retornante holding it perceives a brief, wordless deepening of quiet — often described as *like the moment between the last word and the start of silence.* Not a vision. A quality.
- The stone does not function if it is sought for power. It functions when it is held *lightly* — carried the way a grandmother carries her remedy box, not the way a person carries a weapon.
- Multiple stones exist. They are not all the same stone. Each family that keeps the tradition has its own.

Bela carries one. It belonged to her grandmother, who received it from her own grandmother. It is approximately 6mm across, hexagonal, almost colorless, with a faint interior luminescence visible only in strong lateral light. It lives in a small tin box with dried herbs and a black-and-white photograph that Bela has not permitted herself to look at in forty years.

## 5. El Zumbido Madre — the mother-hum

The Aurevaldan *zumbido madre* is the same phenomenon Veldoria calls *el zumbido* — but the Aurevaldan tradition has thought more carefully about what it is, and the name is richer.

*Zumbido* in Veldorian folk usage means simply *the hum* — the constant low tone of the valley that people stop hearing when they grow old enough to be polite. The Aurevaldan grandmother-tradition calls it *el zumbido madre* — the mother-hum, the source-hum, the hum that was first. The name carries the theological claim: this hum is not a property of the valley, or of Aurevalde, or of any particular place. It is the tone of the original conversation continuing. Every place has it. Most places have been smoothed over too long by human habitation for anyone to notice it easily. High places have more of it because fewer people have lived there and the smoothing has not happened. Rivers have more because the water's acoustic properties do not smooth. Deep caves have more for the same reason as rivers.

The grandmother-phrase: *El zumbido madre es la conversación que Dios empezó y ninguno de nosotros terminó.* The mother-hum is the conversation God began and none of us has finished.

The Aurevaldan test for a true retornante: *¿Lo oyes incluso cuando no hay nada que lo haga ruido?* Can you hear it even when nothing is making it sound? A retornante can. It is one of the earliest signs. The child notices the hum before she notices the lights.

## 6. La Primera Mañana — the First Morning, still ongoing

The deepest Aurevaldan theological claim, and the one that aligns most completely with Veldorian canon:

*The First Dawn never ended.*

The grandmothers do not say *there was a First Dawn.* They say *there is a First Dawn.* The tense is present. The action is continuous. The First Light's scattering into los esparcidos is not a historical event that happened once. It is the nature of the First Light — to scatter, to become many, to inhabit every stone and root and newborn chest, to continue.

The grandmother-phrase: *La primera mañana no se fue. Aprendió a ser todas las mañanas.* The First Morning did not leave. It learned to be all mornings.

The practical consequence: in Aurevaldan ceremony, the moment of greatest attention to los esparcidos is not a recreating of the First Dawn — it is a *tuning in* to the First Dawn that is still happening. Dawn ceremonies, dusk ceremonies, and high-place ceremonies are not rituals of remembrance but rituals of attention to a living presence.

This is the key guardrail: the Aurevaldan tradition does *not* frame the scattering as loss, fracture, or wound. There is no Aurevaldan equivalent of a shattered vessel. The scattering is the form abundance takes. The grandmothers would say: *Lo que se esparció no se perdió. Lo que se esparció llegó a más lugares.* What scattered was not lost. What scattered reached more places.

## 7. Los Nombrados / Los Sin Nombrar

One of the tradition's organizational distinctions: *los nombrados* and *los sin nombrar* — the named ones and the unnamed ones.

*Los nombrados* are beings whose esparcidos have been seen and recognized by a retornante. They are not changed by being named. The naming is the retornante's act, not the being's. But there is a folk belief in Aurevalde that beings whose esparcidos have been named carry themselves slightly differently afterward — not taller, not special, but *as if they have remembered something they always knew.* The baker who has been seen by a retornante sometimes kneads with more ease. The grandmother whose light was recognized sometimes holds her own hands differently.

*Los sin nombrar* are not lesser. They are simply not yet seen. The grandmother-phrase: *El fruto no nombrado no es menos fruto. Solo está esperando la boca que lo reconozca.* The unnamed fruit is no lesser fruit. It only waits for the mouth that will recognize it.

This distinction is important because it prevents the retornante from falling into hierarchies. The seen are not elevated over the unseen. The retornante is not elevated over the ordinary. The grandmother-phrase: *La que ve no es más que la que no ve. La que ve debe ver sin volverse grande.* The one who sees is not more than the one who does not see. The one who sees must see without becoming large.

## 8. The Lineage in Bela's Family

Bela's grandmother's name was Arcelia. She was the last of Bela's direct line to have lived in the Aurevaldan highland village where the tradition was strongest. Arcelia's grandmother had also carried the sight; there is a photograph of Arcelia at twenty years old that reveals, to anyone who knows what to look for, the same quality of attention in the eyes that Mira has. The photograph shows Arcelia standing in a doorway of a red-stone house with her right hand slightly raised, not as if waving but as if touching the air to check its temperature. Bela recognizes this gesture now as the posture of a retornante pausing to attend to something invisible. At twenty, Bela did not know what the gesture meant. At seventy-eight, looking at the photograph, she knows.

Arcelia's name is written on the back of the photograph in a handwriting that is not Bela's and not her mother's — the handwriting of someone who wrote the name as documentation, not as affection. *Arcelia. 1946.* That is all.

Bela has not let herself look at the back of the photograph in forty years. She keeps it face-down in the tin box with the *piedra de los velos* and the dried herbs.

The unexamined thought she keeps not quite thinking: *that the woman in the photograph looks like the girl across the street.*

## 9. The Aurevaldan Ceremonial Vocabulary

Phrases from the grandmother-tradition relevant to the novel's substrate — given here in their original Aurevaldan register (which is, per BIBLE.md, a close cousin of Veldorian Veldarín):

**Phrases of greeting between retornantes:**
- *¿Ves bien esta tarde?* — *Do you see clearly this afternoon?* (Surface reading: a greeting. Actual register: are your perceptions unobstructed?)
- *Se ven bien las estrellas.* — *The stars are visible tonight.* (Code for: the esparcidos are active; this is a good evening for attention.)

**Phrases of ceremony:**
- *La conversación que no se acaba.* — The conversation that does not end. Said at dawn, at dusk, at the beginning of any gathering where the tradition is being held.
- *Naciste viendo.* — You were born seeing. Said to a young retornante on first recognition.
- *La primera mañana.* — The First Morning. Said at the moment of greatest attention — when the esparcidos are thickest, the *zumbido madre* is deepest. Not shouted. Said quietly, as acknowledgment, the way a person confirms a presence they already knew was there.

**Phrases of comfort:**
- *Nadie te lo puede quitar.* — No one can take it from you. Said to a retornante who has been dismissed, disbelieved, or called *loca.*
- *El río sale de nuevo donde toca.* — The river surfaces where it will. Said to a retornante who fears the gift will skip her children.
- *Lo que ves no te vuelve rara. Te vuelve útil.* — What you see does not make you strange. It makes you useful.

## 11. Bela's Specific Transmission to Mira (Chapter 11 substrate)

The fragment Bela tells Mira in Chapter 11 is not the full tradition. It is the grandmother-story she received herself at age seven from Arcelia, sitting in a red-stone kitchen in Aurevalde while the sky outside was the color of embers. She has never told it to anyone in sixty years in Veldoria. She tells it to Mira because Mira is the first person since Arcelia who would understand it without being taught what it means.

Bela's version is slightly compressed from the full tradition — the way a story compresses across sixty years of private holding — but the central images are intact:

The First Light, too full to remain in one place. The scattering through abundance. The loving dark between the pieces still saying *yes, show me what I contain.* The retornante as the answer still being made. *Naciste viendo. Nadie te lo enseñó. Nadie tampoco te lo puede quitar.*

Bela does not present this as cosmology or as doctrine. She presents it the way her own grandmother presented it: as a true story about what actually happened, told in a kitchen, told to a child who was already seeing without knowing the name for it. The story does not teach Mira to see. The story tells Mira that what she is seeing has a name, and that the name is old, and that she is not the first.

This is the entire gift.
