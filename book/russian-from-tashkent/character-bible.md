# Character Bible — Russian from Tashkent

---

## 1. Identity Layers — The In-Between

**Ethnic and linguistic reading** (flag for author confirm): The most probable autobiographical configuration is ethnic Russian or heavily Russified mixed-lineage — perhaps a grandfather who arrived as a Soviet engineer or military officer in the 1950s–60s, family rooted in Tashkent by the second generation. NOT a recent migrant from Russia, NOT ethnically Uzbek, but a Tashkentian Russian-speaker whose family tree never felt the need to leave — until suddenly, everyone else did. Less likely but possible: Tatar, Korean (the deported Koryo-saram community has a strong Tashkent presence), or Volga German. The Korean reading would layer additional displacement history under the Soviet layer. Mark entire lineage `[CONFIRM]`.

The distinction that matters: this is *Tashkentets* identity — citizen of the city first, Soviet second, Russian-speaking third, ethnic category last. After 1991 that ordering inverts. The new state wants ethnic category first.

**Language layer.** Russian is the first language — the tongue of lullabies, jokes, arguments, mathematics. Uzbek is the soil-language: absorbed through the street, the market, certain schoolteachers, the names of foods and neighborhoods and swear words that have no Russian equivalent. After independence, Uzbek becomes official and then compulsory in school, but for a Russian-speaker in the mid-1990s it still feels adjacent, not central — until it starts to feel like a wall. English arrives last, as instrument, not inheritance. Embassy-English: formal, transactional, aspirational. Three languages, three registers, three different versions of the self.

**Class layer.** Soviet engineer-intelligentsia or mid-level technical stratum — the most common professional bracket for Russian-speaker families in UZ. Father likely worked in industrial infrastructure (transformer plant, water authority, factory administration). This class had apartments, not houses; subscriptions to *Ogonyok* and *Nauka i Zhizn*; shelves of Soviet-era encyclopedias. The ruble's collapse in 1991 gutted this stratum faster than any other. Engineers' salaries evaporated. The cushion was small: a garden plot (*dacha*) on the city edge, relatives who could barter, informal work that the Soviet biography had not prepared anyone for.

**Belonging-anxiety.** The phrase that runs under the whole book: *Vy otkyuda?* — "Where are you from?" In Tashkent after 1991: "You are Russian, so you will leave." In St. Petersburg after 2000: "You are from Tashkent, so you are not really Russian." In Moscow: "You are from Central Asia, so you are — pause — *otkyuda?*" The protagonist carries no home address that fully accepts him. This is not tragedy in the immigrant-memoir key. It is the specific condition of the post-Soviet Russian-speaker in Central Asia: a man shaped by a country that no longer exists, living in a country that no longer claims him, briefly passing through a country that never wanted him, working eventually at the embassy of a country that does not know he is there.

## 2. Voice Swatches by Age — Interior Monologue

**Age 6 — 1991. The world tilts.**

The flags are different. Mama said so: different flags now, different name for the country, but the kettle still whistles at the same time every morning and the bread from the *lavash* stall on the corner still smells the same, so how different can it be. Papa came home last Thursday and sat at the kitchen table without taking off his coat for a long time. He and Mama talked after I was put to bed but I could hear the word *dengi* — money — and the word *zavod* — factory — repeated the way words get repeated when people are frightened. I don't know what to be frightened of yet. The school still smells like chalk and cabbage. My friend Dimka still lives upstairs.

**Age 10 — 1995. The departures.**

The Savchenko family left in June and the Oreshkins in August and now their apartment across the hall has a new family, Uzbek, the father works at the customs office, they are polite but we do not eat together. In school they have added Uzbek language hours and taken away some Russian literature hours and the teacher, Natalya Ivanovna, does not say this is wrong but she teaches the Uzbek hours with a face like she is reading a list of train departures. A dollar buys eight thousand *sum* now. Papa brought home dollars twice, from somewhere, and Mama held them and looked at them like they were evidence of something. Evidence of what I am not sure. I am starting to understand that the families who left understood something that our family did not, or understood it earlier, and I am starting to wonder if we were brave or just slow.

**Age 14 — 1999. The bombings. The silence after.**

February sixteenth. I heard it — everyone heard it — a sound that was not thunder, a compression in the air that arrived before the news did. At school the next day the teachers said almost nothing, which told you more than if they had said something. In Karimov's Uzbekistan the silence around a thing is the shape of the thing. What I understood without being told: someone tried to kill the president; the someone was Islamists from the Fergana Valley; now there will be arrests; the arrests will be wide; the silence will get wider. My Russian-speaker friends and I talked about it the way you talk about weather in a country where you cannot change the weather. We had learned, by fourteen, that the correct response to state violence is a kind of lateral conversation — you discuss what you had for lunch, you discuss football, you discuss everything adjacent to the thing you are actually discussing.

**Age 17 — 2002. St. Petersburg. The cold. The foreignness of being "from there."**

The Neva was frozen when I arrived and the cold was not like Tashkent cold, which comes at night and leaves by noon. Petersburg cold lives in the walls. My cousin Sasha had the couch, I had a sleeping bag on the floor, and in the morning I could see my breath inside the apartment. The city was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they don't care whether you're there or not. Nobody said *chyorny* — the word for black that is also the word for unwelcome — to my face. They didn't need to: they looked at the address on my documents and the warmth left their eyes. *Tashkent.* A pause. *Aha.* I had left Uzbekistan because I was not fully Uzbek. I was in Russia and I was not fully Russian. The gap between these two facts was not irony. It was where I actually lived.

**Age 22 — 2007. Chirchiq. Night shift. Copper dust and the calculation.**

The plant smells like heated metal and something electrical that has no name. Night shift runs ten to six and by three in the morning your mind goes to a place that is almost meditation, watching the conveyor, checking gauges, noting the numbers in the log. I am twenty-two. The men around me are thirty-five, forty-five, and they are already finished — not dead, just finished — and they know it and they have made peace with it in the way that requires you to stop looking at the future. I am still looking. The embassy job posting came through a friend of a friend; they need Russian-English interpreters for administrative work; you have to apply in person, bring documents, dress correctly. I am calculating. Not the way an ambitious person calculates — not with ambition. With arithmetic. What must be true for me to not still be here at thirty-five.

**Age 25 — 2010. The Embassy. The badge. The mirror.**

The badge has my photograph and my name and the seal and the building is air-conditioned in a way that Tashkent buildings are not, a specific American cold, and I am wearing the shirt I bought at the Chorsu market two weeks ago because it was the most formal shirt available and I thought about this for three days. The Consul who hired me is from Wisconsin and she pronounces Tashkent correctly, which surprised me. In the mirror of the men's room on the first day I looked at myself for a long time, not from vanity — I am not a vain person — but because I was trying to understand what the man in the mirror had required to get here. The Soviet-born father's salary that disappeared. The neighbors' apartments that emptied. The Petersburg cold. The plant's copper dust still under the nails of the hand holding the badge. I looked like someone who had arrived. I did not yet know where.

## 3. Family System

**Father — Nikolai Alexandrovich [CONFIRM name]**
Engineer, likely at a Soviet industrial facility — electrical infrastructure, water systems, or a plant in the Tashkent region. After 1991 his salary became ceremonial and then stopped. He found work through informal networks — repair contracts, small construction jobs, a stint at a joint-venture firm that did something administrative he was overqualified for. He stayed not because he was passive but because he could not imagine being Russian *in* Russia; his Russia was here, in these streets, these mulberry trees, these apricot-colored walls. This loyalty is the thing his son will spend years trying to name. Pride and grief together, never separated. Psychology: quiet, technically-minded, poor at expressing feeling, expresses it instead through doing — fixing things, building shelves, showing up. Arc: diminished materially; not diminished in his son's eyes, though the son spends a decade believing otherwise.

**Mother — Tatyana Viktorovna [CONFIRM name]**
Likely a teacher, librarian, or medical worker — the Soviet service professions that were feminized and then defunded together. ## 4. Inner Contradictions — The Engine

**1. Tashkent is home in the body; not home in the document.**
He knows the city the way you know a body — its smells, its timings, which streets flood in spring, where to buy the best *non* bread. He has no claim on it the new state recognizes. The contradiction is not political (he does not care about politics in an ideological way) — it is somatic. The place that formed him does not name him as its own.

**2. Russian is the language of his thought; Russia is the place that didn't want him.**
His interior life runs in Russian. His dreams are in Russian. His best jokes are in Russian. But Russia — the country, the city, the faces on the Metro — treated him as a provincial at best and a *churka* (ethnic slur, Central Asian) at worst. The language is his and the homeland of the language is not. This is not a metaphor. It is a daily fact he stopped explaining to people because no one who hadn't lived it could follow.

**3. He chose to come back from Russia. He did not choose to stay in Uzbekistan.**
There is a difference. Coming back was a decision made under specific conditions — cold, racism, no documents, the cost of starting over in a city that regarded him with suspicion. But "I came back" can be narrated as loyalty or as defeat, depending on the day, the decade, the company. The book must hold both without collapsing into either.

**4. Working-class hands, white-collar aspiration, and the embarrassment of having both.**
The plant work is physical, grimy, and skilled. He is not ashamed of it — he did it, he was competent at it, the men he worked alongside were not lesser men. But the embassy job required him to perform a cleanliness, a formality, an English-language self that implicitly disavowed the plant. He does not disavow the plant. He is also relieved to have left it. The tension between these two truths is one of the book's recurring textures.

**5. Pride in family who stayed; envy of family who left — and the arithmetic underneath both.**
The families who left in 1991–1995 are mostly doing better by any material measure. The families who stayed built lives that are real and specific and theirs. He loves his parents' choice. He also knows that if he had a child he would not make the same choice. This is not betrayal. It is what time does to inherited decisions.

**6. He benefited from Karimov's stability while understanding exactly what that stability cost.**
Karimov's Uzbekistan was authoritarian, corrupt, and paranoid. It was also, for a Russian-speaker with modest ambitions, navigable — because the infrastructure still ran, the streets were still safe at night, the factories still had rosters even when they paid nothing. The alternative — Tajikistan's civil war, Kyrgyzstan's unrest — was visible just over the borders. The gratitude is real. The revulsion at what produced it is also real. This is not cognitive dissonance. It is the moral experience of living in a non-democracy with insufficient power to change it.

**7. The embassy badge as arrival and as departure — from what, to what, he is still figuring out.**
The US Embassy job is the book's climactic beat, but it is not resolution. It is a new form of in-between: now he serves the country that is the destination of everyone who wanted to leave, from the inside of a building that processes that wanting. He is neither applicant nor American. He is the man who speaks both languages and belongs to neither room.

## 5. Voice Direction for the Prose

**Tense and person.** Past tense, first-person retrospective adult voice. Recommend maintaining this throughout — the retrospective distance is doing essential work. The adult voice looking back at the six-year-old, the fourteen-year-old, has irony and tenderness available to it that the present tense would close off. The narrator *knows* how things turned out; that knowledge inflects every observation. If the author wants to create present-tense sections for specific scenes (the embassy morning, the bombings), use them as punctuation within the retrospective frame, not as an alternate structure.

**Register.** Literary-realist, dry and observational. The wit is not in jokes — it is in precision. The sentence that describes the customs officer's face at the border, or the exact phrase the school principal used when announcing the new language policy, or the temperature of the plant at 3 a.m. — these are where the voice lives. Not confessional: the narrator does not explain his feelings, he shows you what he was looking at when he had them. Not MFA-precious: no lyric spiraling, no sentence that calls attention to itself as a sentence.

**The Hemon / Bezmozgis / Vapnyar / Krasikov line.** These are the right comparators — not for imitation but for permission. Hemon's compressed displacement, the way Sarajevo and Chicago exist simultaneously in the same paragraph. Bezmozgis's Natasha stories — short, airtight, the immigrant experience rendered without sentimentality but with full warmth. Vapnyar's oblique comedy of Soviet-Jewish dislocation. Krasikov's longer-breath stories that track the bureaucratic and emotional costs of the immigration question without resolving them. The shared quality: these writers trust the specificity. They do not explain the Soviet Union to you. They show you the particular object — the particular queue, the particular official, the particular smell — and let the history arrive through it.

**What to avoid.** Trauma-tourism: the narrative does not lean into suffering for effect. Immigrant-narrative cliché: no grandmothers with dill-smelling hands, no food as substitute for interiority, no "in the old country" framing that turns Tashkent into backdrop. Exoticizing Central Asia: Tashkent is not exotic, it is a real city with specific architecture and traffic and bad coffee and good bread, described from the inside by someone who grew up there. Noble-suffering tone: the protagonist is not a symbol of resilience. He is a specific person who made specific decisions under specific constraints. The book is about those decisions, not about what they prove.

## 6. Author-Confirm Flags

The following assumptions are built into this bible from the most probable autobiographical reading of the given facts. Every item here requires the author's truth-check before the bible is treated as authoritative.

1. **Ethnic lineage.** I assumed ethnic Russian or heavily Russified mixed background (possibly including Tatar or Koryo-saram). What is the actual ethnic composition? This changes the identity-layer analysis substantially.

2. **Sibling configuration.** I sketched an older sister who left, plus a possible younger brother. How many siblings are there? Who stayed, who left, where did they go, when?

3. **Father's specific profession and its post-1991 trajectory.** "Industrial engineer" is a reasonable guess. What was the actual job title and employer? What happened to it between 1991 and 2002?

4. **Mother's work.** Teacher, librarian, or medical worker — which? What did she do to keep the household running through the economic collapse?

5. **The Russia years: St. Petersburg first, then Moscow, or the reverse?** The brief says "St. Petersburg, then Moscow." Confirm chronology and duration. What was the specific reason for coming back to Tashkent rather than staying?

6. **The Chirchiq Transformer Plant.** Is this the actual employer? What was the specific job (line worker, technician, administrative)? This affects the class-texture of the plant sections.

7. **The US Embassy job.** What was the actual role — interpreter, locally-employed staff, administrative? The nature of the work shapes what the badge means.

8. **The neighborhood network.** Were there specific Russian-speaker or Jewish families whose departures were formative? Any composite characters already in mind, or should the friends and neighbors be invented?

9. **The 1999 Tashkent bombings.** How directly proximate was the protagonist to the event? Heard from home, saw aftermath, or more immediate?

10. **Voice preference.** Do the six voice swatches above feel accurate in register, or do any ring false? The interior monologue samples are the most author-specific element in this bible — they need the author's ear to validate.
