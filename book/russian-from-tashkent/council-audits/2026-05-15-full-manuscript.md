### David Bezmozgis — Voice & Restraint

**Strengths**

- The ants-and-crack opening in Chapter 1 is exactly right: a small, specific geometry that doesn't announce itself as symbol but earns its reappearance twenty-two chapters later in the compound gate closing. This is the economy the genre requires and rarely achieves.
- Chapter 2's departure scenes — Galina Ivanovna bringing a cake that is actually a farewell, the doormat smell of the empty apartment afterward — demonstrate the author's best instinct: showing social facts through objects, never through editorial. The vodka pour in the kitchen, heard from the other room, is the finest single image in the pre-pubescent section.
- The voice correctly withholds the protagonist's name. Not as a gesture but as a controlled witholding that accumulates meaning: "the name belongs to the boy and the boy still belongs to that afternoon." That is a sentence that could have been written by Bezmozgis himself, and I mean that as the highest compliment available from this seat.
- Chapter 5's one-word chapter break — "Bomby" — delivered by Vadim at a water fountain between two sandwiches at the correct Tashkent volume: flat, lateral, weather. This is the book at its disciplined best.
- The mother-at-the-Embassy-window scene in Chapter 12 — "Is it good work?" — is quietly extraordinary. The question she doesn't ask (is it safe) over the question she does ask (is it good work) is a lifetime of parenting philosophy expressed without comment.

**Concerns**

- The italicized adult-retrospect passages in Chapter 5 exceed their license. Specifically the section beginning "What I know now that I did not know then?" across four paragraphs. The information value is real — Jaslyk Prison, the arrest counts, the contested attribution — but the delivery mode is expository in a way that the rest of the book is not. The narrator is explaining to the reader rather than remembering. This is the book's most persistent structural tic, and Chapter 5 is where it is most visible because the stakes are highest.
- Chapter 9's return chapter is the weakest in the book. It is a chapter where the narrator does not know what he is looking at, and the prose accurately reflects that disorientation — but "accurately reflects disorientation" is not the same as controlled disorientation. The marshrutka scene in Chirchiq and the plov Sunday are both good; the surrounding material is somewhat adrift.
- The chapter-end notes appended to every chapter — "Chapter notes for the author" — are visible scaffolding that should have been stripped before the book reached this stage of audit. They are fine as working notes; they signal that the chapters have not yet been cut free from their construction process.
- Several [CONFIRM] flags appear in the running text of finished chapters: "[CONFIRM-YEAR: 1988 or 1989]" and others. These are appropriate staging devices at draft stage, but their density suggests biographical uncertainty that may be infecting prose confidence. The prose rhythm occasionally hesitates in ways that correlate with unconfirmed details, as if the writer is not sure enough of the fact to commit the sentence.

**Recommendations**

1. **Cut**: The four italicized retrospect paragraphs in Chapter 5 starting "What I know now that I did not know then?" — Reduce these to no more than one paragraph. The informational content can be distributed across the scene-level narration, or held until Chapter 6's adult voice section. The chapter earns its weight through what the fourteen-year-old sees; the adult explanation is a leak. *Rationale:* Bezmozgis's central craft axiom is that the sentence should show the observation; the paragraph shows the understanding. The current passage is explaining at the paragraph-level what should be available to the reader from the observational sequence.

2. **Strip**: The chapter-end notes throughout the manuscript. Everything from "END CHAPTER X" onward must be removed before the manuscript ships in any form. Their presence reads as the book not yet being a book. *Rationale:* A finished manuscript has no visible scaffolding. Economy extends to what is not on the page.

3. **Revise**: Chapter 9's middle third (the Chirchiq visit and the conversations with the father's colleagues). The chapter has two excellent anchors — the mother at the airport and the hotel application — and unfocused connective tissue between them. The father-son-adjacency scene at the kitchen table after the friend from the institute visits is doing work but is the longest it can be without earning more. *Rationale:* Precision of selection is the genre's primary tool. Every scene not doing double work should be cut to half its length or cut entirely.

**Confidence:** 0.88

### Sana Krasikov — Political Weight & Long Sentences

**Strengths**

- Chapter 10's reading of the hotel lobby as a secondary newspaper — "the American military contractors arrived the weeks when the diplomatic relationship with the United States was at its warmest" — is the kind of politically literate long-sentence observation that works precisely because it does not announce its intelligence. The narrator learned to read geopolitics from occupancy; this is demonstrated, not stated. The Andijan handling in Chapter 10 (a British journalist, a European development worker, a Russian delegation) is sophisticated in exactly the way sensitivity-direction.md asks for: institutional residue, not crisis reporting.
- Chapter 12's treatment of the refusals is the most morally complete section in the book. "The legal category for 'I have stayed as long as I can stay' is not on the non-immigrant visa form" — this sentence contains the entire political economy of the FSN position. The subsequent paragraph on the two truths that do not reconcile is this book's deepest sustained thought.
- The father's sentence in Chapter 5 — "I think that what happened today is what happens when a government needs something to happen" — and the adult narrator's refusal to adjudicate it across twenty-five years ("I cannot tell you whether it was wisdom or paranoia") is the correct handling of the attribution question. The book holds the indeterminacy of that sentence as a central fact, which is more historically honest and literarily sophisticated than any attribution would be.
- The kitchen table decision in Chapter 6 — "there is no plan; there has always been here" — is the strongest single exchange in the Russia-family section. The sentence structure itself enacts the father's logic: "There is no plan" (acknowledging the vacuum) followed by "There has always been here" (filling the vacuum with something solid). The prose earns the father's interiority without entering it.

**Concerns**

- The political content in Chapters 7 and 8 (the Russia years) is thin relative to the historical weight those years carried for Central Asian migrants in Moscow and Petersburg during 2000–2002. The propiska system and the document checks are rendered accurately, but the broader post-Soviet political atmosphere — Putin's first year, the normalization of the security apparatus, the Chechen wars as background texture — is largely absent. The world-building around the protagonist's political formation is less developed in Russia than in Uzbekistan, and it shows in the chapter's comparative flatness.
- The Andijan handling in Chapter 10 is correct but compressed. A two-beer silence from a development organization worker is the right image, but the chapter's full treatment of the post-Andijan period in the hotel is handled in a few paragraphs. The occupancy-as-political-instrument metaphor is good; it could sustain one more specific scene (a specific Uzbek government guest in the week after Andijan, a specific change in what could or could not be said at the front desk) before being formulated.
- Chapter 11's long reflective passages on industrial expertise and institutional collapse risk over-explaining what the Gennady scenes have already demonstrated. "When a specific part fails on a 1968 winding machine, you do not order a replacement. You modify another part." This is right. "This is what expertise looks like when the world that required the expertise has collapsed" — this is the narrator explaining what the reader has already understood. The paragraph following it continues the explanation for another four sentences. Cut it by half.

**Recommendations**

1. **Expand**: Chapter 7 or Chapter 8, one additional scene grounding the Moscow/Petersburg political atmosphere circa 2000–2002. The Putin reference is glancing ("the new president had been in office for a year"); one specific moment — a news item watched in Sasha's room, an overheard conversation at the restaurant kitchen — that gives the protagonist's political formation in Russia more than the racism and the document-check scaffold. *Rationale:* Krasikov's central axiom is that the post-Soviet person's political formation in Russia is as important as what happened to them there. The Russia chapters currently render what happened to the protagonist's body and wallet; his understanding of what was happening to the country is underwritten.

2. **Cut**: Chapter 11, the three paragraphs beginning "The machines worked. They worked in the way that things work when the people operating them have made their personal reputations responsible for the operation." The observation about improvised Soviet-legacy maintenance is good; the paragraph after — on expertise and institutional collapse — is excess narration of what the book has already shown. *Rationale:* In Krasikov's work, the long thoughtful sentence earns its length through the precision of the moral observation it carries. When the observation is already visible in the scene, the long sentence becomes paraphrase.

3. **Add**: A single scene in Chapter 12 — one specific moment post-Andijan in the Embassy compound from the Uzbek staff perspective — that renders the "Uzbek colleagues' silence" mentioned in sensitivity-direction.md §4. Currently the chapter gestures at this ("the Uzbek staff watched the American staff with an equivalent attentiveness") without scene-level specificity. One exchange between Dilnoza and the narrator, pre-2008, that carries the weight of what they both understood and could not say, would be the book's most original contribution to FSN fiction. *Rationale:* The FSN double-exposure (American employer, Uzbek government observer) is the book's genuinely new territory. It currently receives thematic description where it deserves a scene.

**Confidence:** 0.84

### Aleksandar Hemon — Suspended Self / Fragmented Temporality

**Strengths**

- The structural decision to ship strict chronological rather than frame-with-hinge is legitimate, and I will not call it wrong — I will say it is a defensible choice that costs something specific, discussed in the synthesis section. What is right about the chronological decision: it produces a narrator who accumulates understanding in visible increments. We see the political intelligence form; we see the language intelligence form; we see the body that becomes the Embassy body. The accumulation is the argument.
- The temporal compression is handled well in the chapter breaks. The decade between Chapters 1 and 2 (1989 to 1991) is a two-year jump that the book inhabits correctly by placing Chapter 1 inside the summer before the fall rather than arriving at the fall itself. Chapter 5's bomb-sound on the trolleybus — arriving before the information arrives — is a genuine formal achievement: the sensory event precedes its name, which is how history actually enters the body.
- The phrase "adjacent to belonging" in Chapter 7 is the book's most exact formulation of its central condition. "You're not one of them," said as a compliment that positions the narrator in the only available permit. The word "adjacent" is doing the structural work of the whole book in one syllable.
- The Yelchin-in-the-kommunalka device is right: a man from Baku, ten years ahead, as a forward projection of the protagonist's possible trajectory. The scene is brief and calibrated. "Moscow takes you." "Don't let it take you entirely." This is Hemon's mode precisely — the future visible through the structural echo.

**Concerns**

- The Russia chapters (7 and 8) are underwritten relative to their position in the book's argument. If the book's thesis is that the protagonist occupies a space the map has not drawn — not Uzbek, not Russian, not American — then Russia is the geographic and psychic test of that thesis, and it is handled in approximately 10,000 words when it could carry 15,000. The Petersburg chapter does its job (the propiska, the cold, the aha-pause) but skips the interiority of the suspended self. We know what the protagonist did (carried drywall, tutored Kostya, saved money); we do not fully know what it felt like to be Russian-speaking in the country of Russian, and the country did not want you. The closest the book gets is "I had left Uzbekistan because I was not fully Uzbek. I was in Russia and I was not fully Russian. The gap between these two facts was not irony. It was where I actually lived." That is in the character bible voice swatch, not the chapter. The chapters should be that precise.
- The Andijan years in Chapter 10 have a temporal gap: we move from 2003 to 2005 (Andijan) to 2005 (Embassy posting notice) in a single chapter. The three-year hotel period is compressed further in the post-Andijan material. Given that Andijan was the political event that restructured the protagonist's entire professional context, the aftermath deserves more than the lobby-occupancy metaphor. The time inside Andijan's aftermath — the months after, what changed in day-to-day hotel work — is glossed.
- The book opens in Chapter 1 at the ant-crack in the asphalt and closes in Chapter 12 at the compound gate on Moyqorghon Street. This is a clean circle. But the circle closes entirely on the same image type (a threshold, a threshold crossed, a small specific observation of an ordinary world). The book's argument — that the map has not drawn where the protagonist is from — deserves a closing image that is less resolved, more suspended. The "apricot trees were turning" ending is beautiful but it is peaceful, and the book's intelligence is not peaceful.

**Recommendations**

1. **Extend**: Chapter 7 by one additional scene that renders the suspension of being Russian-speaking in Russia — not the document-check scenes, which are already there, but a scene where the narrator's Russian fails him, or succeeds at the wrong moment, or produces the aha-pause in someone who should know better. One scene of linguistic uncanniness — the language being his and the city not recognizing the claim — would give the Russia section its structural argument. *Rationale:* Hemon's axiom is that the immigrant's temporality must be form, not subject. Currently the Russia section is subject (this is what happened); the form (the suspended, doubled, uncannily-Russian-speaking self) is under-rendered.

2. **Consider**: The closing paragraph of Chapter 12 — "I was twenty-five. I was Russian-speaking, Uzbek-born, Embassy-badged, courtyard-raised, plant-seasoned, hotel-schooled. I was from here. I was also, and had always been, from somewhere the map had not yet drawn." This is the right statement; it is too neatly a statement. The book has earned the knowledge but the articulation risks being too final. Consider moving this observation one page earlier and ending instead on the image of the badge in the pocket, without the summary. *Rationale:* The final sentence of "The apricot trees were turning. I went to bed" is correct. The summary paragraph before it is the narrator naming what the book has already demonstrated. Trust the demonstration.

3. **Hold**: The strict chronological structure — this is correct. The frame narrative (embassy-opening, return to frame) recommended in genre-direction.md would have been elegant, but strict chronological is what was built and it works. Do not add a frame prologue or epilogue to this draft. The cost is that the first four chapters are preparatory in a way a frame-opening would not have been; the gain is that the structural accumulation of the protagonist's intelligence is visible to the reader in real time, which the frame would have flattened.

**Confidence:** 0.79

### Hisham Matar — Authoritarian State's Long Shadow

**Strengths**

- The Karimov regime is handled exactly correctly: never caricatured, never the subject of a scene he enters, present entirely in its effects on how ordinary people moved through their lives. The bearded neighbor who shaves after February 16 — "the absence of the beard was so sudden and complete that it looked less like a haircut and more like a decision made under specific external pressure" — is the kind of observation that *The Return* was built on: the forensic attention to what a single small fact reveals about the state's operation on bodies.
- Chapter 5's shaped silence — "a shaped silence, like water taking the form of a container" — is the strongest single formulation of authoritarian affect in the book. The comparison is physical, unpretentious, and absolutely precise. The teachers who said almost nothing are rendered without condemnation; their compliance is a survival fact. This is Matar's mode exactly.
- The SNB presence in Chapter 12 is handled with perfect discipline: it is not named in individual scenes, it is present as a structural condition everyone understands and nobody discusses. The Dilnoza character — "she knew which SNB officer was assigned to the outer perimeter not because she had any formal contact with him but because in Tashkent in 2007 you knew who the SNB was if you paid attention" — is right.
- The father's not-washing-hands scene in Chapter 5 (the only time in the book he sits without washing first) is the book's single most devastating deployment of a repeated physical detail. The body breaking its own ritual is the exact register in which the authoritiarian state's shadow enters domestic space.

**Concerns**

- The book's treatment of the post-1999 crackdown is accurate and restrained in Chapter 5, but the Sanjar absence scene — the most legally and politically exposed material in the book — is almost too restrained. The narrative goes to great lengths to disclaim knowledge of what Sanjar's four-day absence meant, then does the work of attributing it to "someone in his family was arrested, or they were frightened." The disclaimer is right; the attribution, even hypothetical, undermines it. If the book genuinely does not know, it should not guess. The Sanjar paragraph could end at: "I did not know what those four days were. I do not know if I know." Full stop.
- The absent Dmitry Alexandrovich scene in Chapter 5 — "She doesn't know where he is" — is powerful but the character's function as a device is visible. He enters, serves his structural purpose (a disappeared colleague), and exits. The problem is that the scene is powerful enough that the device is visible as a device. Either Dmitry Alexandrovich should be more present in earlier chapters (a single sentence in Chapter 4 would be enough), or the scene should be slightly more oblique — the wife not named, the details even more compressed, so the scene is about the fact of disappearance rather than the particular man.
- The 2004 Tashkent bombings in Chapter 10 are mentioned as "two sets of bombings in Tashkent, in March and July" and then immediately characterized as "smaller" and "less complicated" — which they were historically, but the compression risks reading as dismissive. A single additional sentence acknowledging the specific state of mind of someone who has already lived through February 1999 and is now experiencing a second set of bombings would give the Chapter 10 material more weight.

**Recommendations**

1. **Revise**: The Sanjar paragraph in Chapter 5. Remove the hypothetical attribution ("someone in his family was arrested, or if they were frightened"). The paragraph's power is in not knowing; the hypotheticals diminish the not-knowing. End the paragraph: "What I know is that I noticed the absence, and I noticed the not-asking, and I understood from both that there were things in the city that were happening to my classmates that were not happening to me in exactly the same way." That is the chapter's most important ethical sentence. Stop there. *Rationale:* Matar's axiom is that what cannot be recovered should be marked as absence, not estimated as possibility. The Sanjar scene is about exactly that impossibility.

2. **Add**: A single sentence in Chapter 4 that plants Dmitry Alexandrovich or a character in his structural position — a colleague of the father's whom the narrator has met at the factory, whose name has appeared in a kitchen conversation. The plant needs no more than one sentence to function. *Rationale:* The disappearance in Chapter 5 earns its weight only if the person was, however briefly, present. A planted name is sufficient. Currently the scene depends on an invented character appearing for the first time in the scene of his disappearance.

3. **Clarify**: The post-2004 bombings passage in Chapter 10. One sentence: something like "For someone who had stood on a trolleybus in February 1999, the city's capacity for this had been established; the bombings in March were less disorienting than they would have been before that morning, which is a kind of damage the 1999 chapter doesn't fully account." This connects the two sets of bombings across the narrative without giving the 2004 events disproportionate space. *Rationale:* The authoritarian state's shadow includes not just the events but what those events do to a person's relationship to the next events. The narrator who has already lived through one set of bombings has a different response to the second set, and the difference should be registered.

**Confidence:** 0.85

### Svetlana Alexievich — Voice Tone & Oral-History Weight

**Strengths**

- The polyphony in this book is not formal — it is not a chorus of voices — but it is real: the mother, the father, Galina Ivanovna, Gennady, Malika, Dilnoza each have a distinct register. The mother's voice is the most distinctive and the most consistent. "Sometimes things happen, and then they happen another time" (Chapter 1), "That's good work" (Chapter 10), "Is it good work?" (Chapter 12) — three sentences across twenty years of narrative, each carrying a full worldview without a word of interior exposition. This is the dignity of ordinary people being complicated that this seat requires.
- Gennady in Chapter 11 is the book's most successful secondary character. His "good" in response to the Embassy letter news is the correct length for its weight. A man who operates in the practical register, whose precision contains whatever warmth he has: this is a person, not a device.
- The Yelchin character (the Azeri neighbor in the kommunalka) is underused but correctly deployed — he gets one long speech about Moscow and then one line of farewell, and in those two appearances he expands the book's cast of post-Soviet displaced persons without overrunning the narrator's frame. The detail about his lamb-with-plums-and-walnuts dish — "tasted of a specificity I recognized as the specificity of belonging to a place" — is the book at its most compassionate toward its secondary characters.

**Concerns**

- The book is weighted heavily toward the narrator's interiority and the narrator's parents. The other Russian-speaking characters who people the Yunusabad-Chirchiq world — neighbors, classmates, Dimka, Kolya — are consistently composite and consistently underdeveloped. This is partly appropriate (the author is writing from his own experience, not documenting his community) but partly a missed opportunity. The single most interesting unwritten scene in this manuscript is a Galina Ivanovna scene: the neighbor bringing a cake that is actually a farewell. The cake, the icing, the tea — this scene exists in one paragraph and should probably exist in a page and a half.
- The Uzbek voices in the book — Sanjar, Aziz, Farida Yusupovna, the woman at the hotel business center with the headscarf — are all rendered from outside, which is correct per sensitivity-direction.md red line 1. But the rendering occasionally tips from disciplined restraint into functional absence. Sanjar in Chapter 5 is essentially a structural device (the absence, the not-asking); Farida Yusupovna across Chapters 3 and 4 is a pedagogical apparatus (the waiting, the brightness). These are accurate renderings from outside, but they risk making Uzbek characters structurally invisible rather than structurally other. The thirty-second scene with the woman at the business center in Chapter 10 is better — she exists for thirty seconds and those seconds have weight. That is the model.
- The mother's voice is the book's strongest secondary instrument and it disappears from Chapters 7–9 almost entirely. She is referenced (the letter about the father's health that brings the narrator home from Moscow) but not present in scene. Given that the book's emotional center is the family, the three-chapter absence of the family is notable.

**Recommendations**

1. **Extend**: The Galina Ivanovna departure scene in Chapter 2. The current paragraph is the correct shape but half the correct length. The woman deserves her tea-and-cake more fully: what she said while not saying she was leaving, what the mother's face was doing while listening, the specific object she gave or didn't give. This is the book's first departure scene and it establishes the template for all subsequent departures. It should be the fullest rendering. *Rationale:* The dignity of letting ordinary people be complicated requires time. One paragraph is not enough time for a departure that recurs structurally throughout the book.

2. **Restore**: The mother in at least one scene in the Russia chapters. A phone call from Tashkent to Petersburg that the narrator makes from a payphone or Sasha's landline; a moment where her voice crosses the gap. This needs only a page. The absence of her voice across three chapters is felt as a structural absence. *Rationale:* The book's polyphony requires that its most important voice not disappear for a third of the narrative. The mother's voice is the book's moral compass; its absence during the Russia years leaves those chapters without their orienting note.

3. **Develop**: The headscarf woman in Chapter 10 (six weeks before Andijan). The scene is currently three paragraphs of transaction followed by one paragraph of adult reflection. The reflection is right; the transaction could sustain one more observation — not interiority (red line), but behavior, something she does with the USB drive or the plastic folder, one detail that makes her specific rather than archetypal. *Rationale:* The book's most sensitive characters — the ones Alexievich would have spent hours recording, the ones who lived the crackdown from inside its target — appear in this book only as thirty-second transactions. That is the honest constraint of the narrator's position. But within that constraint, maximum specificity is the only courtesy available.

**Confidence:** 0.77

### William Maxwell — Biographer-of-a-Friend Ethics

**Strengths**

- The author's note in Chapter 1 — "The protagonist's name is withheld on the page until the author chooses it" — is exactly the right ethical posture. The name belongs to the subject; it cannot be given until the subject gives it. *So Long, See You Tomorrow* was built on a similar act of withholding-as-respect. The unnamed protagonist is not a literary affectation; it is the correct expression of who has authority over the name.
- The [CONFIRM] flags throughout are — from this seat — the most important structural feature of the manuscript, not the most worrying one. They are the visible trace of the author-subject relationship: these are the facts that belong to Ruslan and that cannot be filled in by the writer, only by the person who lived them. The flags are the book's ethical transparency. In the published draft, they should not be removed until Ruslan has confirmed or denied each one. The book should ship with flags unresolved in a "working draft for Ruslan's review" version before any wider publication.
- The mother's line in Chapter 12 — "Is it good work?" — is the kind of detail that can only come from a real person in a real relationship. The writer did not invent this. Whether it is literally recalled or reconstructed from the texture of a real person, it has the quality of biographical truth: it sounds like someone specific. This is the gold of memoir-fiction.

**Concerns**

- The treatment of the sister is ethically incomplete. She is present in Chapters 1–6 as a structural counterweight (the seven-year-old with the cloth rabbit, the sister asking about *bomby*), and then she disappears from the narrative. In Chapter 9 there is a [CONFIRM-SIBLING-STATUS] flag that acknowledges we don't know whether she is in Tashkent or elsewhere by the time of the return. This uncertainty is the book's most significant biographical gap. A sibling who appears for six chapters and then vanishes without accounting is not a narrative choice — it is an unresolved biographical fact that must be resolved before the book ships in any form.
- The father's interiority in Chapter 5 — "I think that what happened today is what happens when a government needs something to happen" — is the most politically exposed sentence in the book. The father is still alive. His specific words, especially if attributed to a named or nameable person, could create problems for any family members who remain in Uzbekistan. The sentence is too precise to be simply a composite observation. The author needs to have made an active decision about this sentence with the subject's knowledge. It cannot be left in a [CONFIRM] state.
- The Gennady character is acknowledged as composite in the chapter notes, but his level of specificity — the Kharkiv origin, the 1978 start date, the son in Chelyabinsk, the father who came with the Leningrad evacuation — gives him the density of a real person. If there is a real person behind this composite whose family might recognize him from the book, that recognition needs to be accounted for, not just aesthetically managed.

**Recommendations**

1. **Resolve**: The sister's status before the manuscript moves beyond working draft. Where is she by Chapter 9? Where is she by Chapter 12? Her presence in the early chapters is too specific (the cloth rabbit, the *bomby* scene) to leave her fate unwritten. This is the book's single highest-priority biographical resolution. *Rationale:* Maxwell's axiom is that the writer has an obligation to account for everyone they have brought onto the page. The sister has been brought fully onto the page. Her disappearance from the narrative is an active choice only if it is a confirmed choice.

2. **Decide**: The father's sentence in Chapter 5. Either: (a) confirm with the subject that this sentence may stand as written; (b) make it clearly hypothetical/composite ("I imagined him thinking something like this, though he did not say it") which changes its register but removes the attribution; or (c) move the observation to the narrator's adult inference rather than the father's direct speech. The current form is maximally powerful and maximally exposed. That trade-off is the author's to make, not the council's — but the author must make it consciously. *Rationale:* The biographer-of-a-friend's primary obligation is to not expose the friend to harm. A sentence that could be read as attributing specific political skepticism to a named (or nameable) person still living in Uzbekistan requires the subject's active consent.

3. **Add**: An author's note at the beginning of the published manuscript that explicitly acknowledges Ruslan as the biographical source, states the degree of fictionalization, and confirms that Ruslan reviewed the manuscript before publication. This note does not need to be long — a paragraph. *So Long, See You Tomorrow* has a similar note; it transforms the book's ethics from ambiguous to declared. *Rationale:* The [CONFIRM] flags are the working-draft version of this note. Their function — marking what the author does not have authority to write alone — needs to be fulfilled in the published text by Ruslan's consent being visible to the reader.

**Confidence:** 0.82

### Annie Ernaux — Sociological-Witness Register

**Strengths**

- The class arc is the sharpest thing in this book, and it is rendered without a single moment of pity or triumph. The father's Soviet engineering salary becoming ceremonial, the mother's tutoring income in the black notebook, the son carrying drywall in Petersburg for seventy rubles a day, the son at the Embassy earning a FSN salary that was "three times my current salary" — this is the complete arc, and it is told through numbers and objects, never through sentiment. This is Ernaux's mode precisely.
- "I was twenty years old. I was working for a wage that was, by local standards, excellent, and by any other standard, not." That sentence in Chapter 10 is the book's most Ernaux-like moment: the class position stated in its full contradictory specificity, without apology or irony, simply the fact. The "not" hanging at the end is the right punctuation.
- The father-as-engineer who loses the economic function of his expertise while retaining its form — the Gennady parallel — is the book's most original sociological observation. Expertise intact. Institutional conversion of expertise into salary, social position, and meaning: collapsed. This observation is not stated as such until Chapter 11; it is implied in Chapters 1 through 4 through the father's behaviors (the wage slip arithmetic, the fixed things in the apartment, the notebooks). The accumulation is earned.
- The Chorsu carrot scene in Chapter 3 — "Because that's how plov is supposed to be here" — with the word "here" doing its small but significant work. Class-location and geographic-belonging stated together in the word "here." This is the right kind of precision.

**Concerns**

- The class arc has a gap between Chapters 9 and 10 that the book does not fully address. The narrator returns from Moscow in 2002, works at an unnamed logistics job, and then applies to the hotel. The gap is acknowledged as a "killing time" period but the class experience of that period — what it felt like to be a Russian-speaker of twenty-two with two years of Russian labor experience applying for a hotel job in Tashkent in 2003, the specific social texture of that application — is not rendered. 1. **Expand**: The logistics job period in Chapter 9 by half a page. The specific social dynamics of that work — the colleagues, the work environment, the daily commute, the contrast with what the two years in Russia had been — give the class arc its necessary middle step. The hotel application does not land with full weight unless the thing it was applied away from has texture. *Rationale:* Ernaux's auto-sociobiography shows the class movement through every social surface it crosses. The narrator's movement from Russia to logistics to hotel to Embassy is the book's class argument; the logistics step is currently a structural placeholder rather than a scene.

2. **Add**: One half-page in Chapter 12 on the specific experience of the Embassy salary. What could the narrator now afford to do? What changed in the apartment? What did his parents' kitchen look like after six months of his Embassy income contributing to it? This is not conspicuous consumption narration; it is the class-transformation rendered in the small specific details Ernaux uses: a repair finally made, a coat bought, a bill paid without the arithmetic. *Rationale:* The class arc is the book's spine. Its final act — the FSN salary — is the climax of that arc and currently receives no scene-level treatment.

3. **Clarify**: The Irina Arkadyevna scene in Chapter 8. Add one sentence of interior response to paying the two-hundred-ruble price. Not indignation — the narrator does not do indignation — but the specific arithmetic of what it means to pay fifty rubles extra per month when the day-labor rate is seventy rubles. The number matters. The class position is precise; the precision requires the number's weight to be felt. *Rationale:* Ernaux's central axiom is that the class experience lives in specific transactions, not in generalized observations about class. The transaction is there; its weight is not felt because the narrator moves on too quickly.
