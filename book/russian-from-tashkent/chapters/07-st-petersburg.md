# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 7: St. Petersburg

*St. Petersburg, Russia — 2000–2001* `[CONFIRM-RUSSIA-ARRIVAL]` `[CONFIRM-SPB-HOUSING]` `[CONFIRM-SPB-WORK]`

---

The train from Tashkent to Moscow takes three and a half days. The train from Moscow to St. Petersburg takes the night. By the time I descended onto the platform at Moskovsky Vokzal, I had been in transit for four days and had eaten the food my mother packed until it was gone and then eaten black bread and processed cheese from the *provodnitsa* and drunk tea from a glass in a metal holder that reminded me of the one on our kitchen shelf. I had watched the Kazakh steppe give way to the Ural foothills and the Ural foothills give way to the European Russian plain, and the plain went on for so long that I stopped looking at it and played cards with a Volga German family who were going to Munich by a route I could not follow on any map.

Sasha met me at the platform. He was twenty-three to my fifteen and he had been in Petersburg for two years already, which made him an authority. He was wearing a leather jacket that was not adequate for the February cold and he was carrying a plastic bag with two bottles of Baltika beer because he thought I was older than I was, or perhaps because he thought the occasion required beer whether I was old enough or not.

"You made it," he said.

I said I had.

"You're taller," he said.

I did not remember him well enough to know whether this was true. He was the son of my mother's sister `[CONFIRM-FAMILY-CONNECTION]`, and I had last seen him in Tashkent when we were both much younger, and the main thing I remembered was that he had a bicycle I was not allowed to ride. Now he had a leather jacket and two bottles of beer and an apartment seventeen minutes by metro from where we stood, and that was enough.

We took the metro. I watched the stations the way I had watched everything in the last four days, which was with the particular alertness of a person who understands that every detail will be needed later, once the understanding arrives. The stations were deep. This surprised me. Tashkent had a metro and I thought I knew what a metro was, but Petersburg's metro goes down at an angle that takes a full minute on the escalator, and the stations at the bottom are vaulted and tiled and lit in the Soviet way, which is to say as if light itself were rationed but still applied with care. Akademicheskaya. Politechnicheskaya. The names slid past.

I did not know then that the city we were rising through as the escalator climbed at the next stop was one of the most beautiful in the world. I knew what I could see: concrete, tiles, the backs of coats.

---

Sasha's apartment was on Ligovsky Prospekt, third floor of a building that had been built a hundred years before anyone in my family was born. The entrance — Sasha said *paradnaya*, not *podezd*, and I filed this in my head as the first of what would become a long list of Petersburg-specific words — had a door code scratched into the plaster by a previous tenant, three digits I was given and would not forget. The stairwell smelled of damp concrete and of a cat that was not visible but had been recently present.

The apartment had three bedrooms, which is to say three families. Sasha occupied one room with his friend Genka. The second room belonged to an elderly woman named Nina Petrovna, who had lived there since the Khrushchev era and had a shelf of porcelain figurines she would not discuss and a radio she kept at exactly the same volume every hour of the day. The third room was being sublet that month by a family from Penza, a father and mother and a boy of about seven who stared at me and did not speak for three days.

The kitchen was shared. One gas stove with four burners, each controlled by a different household. The agreement about the refrigerator was old and had evolved through several previous tenants and could not be entirely explained. Nina Petrovna had two shelves, bottom and second from top. Sasha and Genka had one shelf each, roughly, by informal negotiation. The family from Penza had a shelf they had established on arrival by a process that Sasha described as "they just did it and nobody said anything." I had a corner of Sasha's shelf.

The bathroom had a kolonka — a geyser water heater on the wall that required lighting with a match, then holding the match until the pilot took, then adjusting the temperature by a dial that had been recalibrated at some point by someone who was not a plumber. Sasha showed me this procedure with the gravity of a man initiating a younger man into a meaningful rite.

"Don't let the pilot go out while you're in the shower," he said. "Cold water. Very suddenly."

I said I understood.

"And close the window," he said. "The draught from the Neva blows the pilot out."

This was February in St. Petersburg. The window was not, I would soon learn, a window one opened by choice.

---

I slept on a *razkladushka* — a folding camp bed — on Sasha's floor, between his bed and the wall. The first morning I could see my breath inside the apartment. Not because the heating was broken, but because the heating was Soviet district heating that ran at a schedule set by the city rather than the household, and by early February the schedule had begun its spring reduction even though the temperature outside was still -14°C.

My hands stopped bending normally within thirty seconds of stepping outside. This is not metaphor. The knuckles stopped folding smoothly when I tried to make a fist, as though the cold had inserted something between the joints. I was wearing the coat my mother had bought me at Chorsu in November, a good wool coat in a dark color, which was adequate for Tashkent winter and was not adequate for Ligovsky Prospekt in February.

Sasha lent me an extra layer. He had a quilted jacket of the type sold at the Avtovo market, dark green, with a hood that could be pulled down and a zipper that caught. I wore it over my Tashkent wool coat for three weeks until I learned where to buy things more cheaply and bought a version of my own. This is how you learn a city: not from the top but from the specific problem of staying warm.

---

The problems arrived in an order that was not the order I would have predicted from home.

The first problem was not language. My Russian was entirely adequate. More than adequate: it was the only language I had. When Sasha's friends sat in the kitchen and talked until midnight about football and work and the cost of things, I understood everything. When the woman at the *produkty* store on the ground floor of the next building asked what I wanted, I told her and she gave it to me and we had no difficulty. When Nina Petrovna knocked on Sasha's door to tell us the television in the common room was being used that evening for something important and we would need to use our own television or do without, I understood her speech perfectly, including its undertone, which was not hostility but the absolute specificity of a woman who had established her rights through four decades of shared apartments and knew each right precisely.

The language was mine. Russia was the country of my language. I had heard this my whole childhood as a fact so obvious it did not require stating.

The first problem was the address.

---

In Russia, to do almost anything legal, one requires a *propiska* — a residential registration. Without propiska: no bank account, no official employment, no visit to a state clinic, no SIM card purchased in your own name. This was the Soviet-era registration system that Russia, unlike some other republics, had kept. In 1993 the Constitutional Court had declared it unconstitutional; in practice it functioned exactly as before under a renamed system. The word for the new system was *registratsiya* but everyone still said propiska.

I did not have a propiska in Petersburg. Sasha was registered at the apartment on Ligovsky but his registration covered him, not a visiting cousin from Uzbekistan.

This would have been, in the Soviet era, a serious crime. In 2000, in the first months of the new president, it was an inconvenience with teeth. The teeth were: the police could stop me at any time and demand documents. If my documents did not show a Petersburg address, I could be fined, or held, or in certain moods of certain officers, more than that.

Sasha arranged something. He knew someone who knew a woman who had an apartment in the Primorsky district and registered people there for cash, a hundred rubles a month, a rubber stamp and a signature in the right book. The stamp did not entitle me to anything in Primorsky. It entitled me to show the stamp to a policeman and watch his eyes change register from the register of a man who has found something to the register of a man for whom the paperwork is in order.

The woman in Primorsky I met once, briefly, in the hallway of her building. She was fifty, practical, asked no questions. She had a table in the hallway and a shelf of books and the particular stillness of a woman running a low-grade administrative business from her home and doing it in a way that would survive scrutiny if scrutiny was moderate.

She looked at my documents. She looked at my face. She made a note. She handed me back my documents plus a small piece of paper with a registration stamp.

"Don't lose that," she said.

I did not lose that.

---

What I understood about Petersburg in those first weeks was that it was enormous in a way that had nothing to do with population. Tashkent is a city built laterally — low buildings, wide streets, the courtyard as the organizing unit of social life. Everything is negotiated in small circles: the courtyard, the mahalla, the flat, the bazaar with its known faces. Tashkent is a city you can hold in your head as a collection of named places.

Petersburg is a city that does not want to be held. The Nevsky Prospekt goes on for so long that you cannot see the far end. The Neva is wider than anything in Tashkent that moves. The bridges go up at night in summer to let the ships through, and when I heard this from Sasha I did not believe it, and then I saw it, and then I believed it, and then I stood on an embankment in the white night of late June watching a cargo vessel pass through the opened Dvortsovy Bridge, and I understood for the first time that I was in a city that was genuinely indifferent to me — not hostile, just indifferent in the way that a river is indifferent to what crosses it.

This was a different feeling from Tashkent, where the city was indifferent to me in the specific way of a city that had decided my category was past tense.

Here there was no category yet. That would come.

---

I found work through Sasha, who found it through Genka, who had a friend whose uncle ran a construction crew that was doing renovation work on apartments in the Petrogradsky district. This is how work is found in a city where you have no propiska, no history, and no one to call who can call someone.

The work was moving materials. Not skilled labor — the movement of plasterboard, tile, sand bags, lumber, from a van parked outside a building to the floor of the apartment being renovated. Up stairs, usually, because Petersburg's elevator situation was similar to Tashkent's except the buildings were six stories, not nine.

The crew was six men. Sasha was on the crew when he could get time from his regular job. Two men were from Petersburg, older, quieter, who moved materials all day without speaking much and ate lunch from containers they brought from home. Two men were from Kyrgyzstan — brothers, twenty-something, who spoke Russian with an accent and spoke to each other in a language I could not follow and who were, I understood later, also working without the correct documentation and were also paying someone somewhere for a rubber stamp. The sixth man was the foreman, whose name was Vadim Gennadievich, a man of about forty who was stocky and wore a mustache and was from Yaroslavl and had been in Petersburg for fifteen years and had, in that time, developed a theory about Central Asians.

His theory was nuanced by the standards of the genre. He did not dislike Central Asians — or so he said, several times, usually after demonstrating the opposite. He had worked with them before. He found them, on the whole, acceptable workers. He had certain concerns about their reliability, their tendency to disappear without notice when family obligations arose in their home regions, their inability to fully understand the Petersburg context.

He told me this on the second day, while we were carrying a stack of drywall up four flights.

I said I was from Uzbekistan.

"Yes, I know," he said.

"My Russian is fine," I said. It was not a defense. It was information.

"I can hear that," he said. He stopped on the landing, set his end of the drywall down. "Where in Uzbekistan?"

"Tashkent."

"The capital."

"Yes."

He thought about this. He picked up his end of the drywall. "My brother-in-law was stationed in Tashkent. Seventies. He says it's hot."

I said it was.

"He says they have beautiful tiles on the buildings."

I said they did.

We carried the drywall to the fourth floor. Vadim Gennadievich set his end down and looked at me for a moment with the look of a man who has more or less arrived at a judgment.

"You're not one of them," he said.

I did not ask what he meant. I knew what he meant. The sentence was meant as a compliment. It was also an account of how I was going to be permitted to be here.

I took the compliment. I worked the rest of the day.

---

The metropolitan character of this problem — I am getting ahead of myself; this is a thought that took years to arrive at its full size — is that in Tashkent I was not Uzbek enough. In Petersburg I was not Russian enough. The space between these two not-enough's was where I actually lived. The fact that I was standing in the city that had produced Pushkin and Dostoevsky and Akhmatova, speaking the language of Pushkin and Dostoevsky and Akhmatova without an accent, being told I was not one of them — this was not irony. Irony is a literary designation. What it was, was the actual geometry of my situation.

I will not say I realized this then. I was fifteen and I was carrying drywall and my knuckles hurt in the cold. What I knew was: the sentence "you're not one of them" had placed me somewhere. The somewhere was adjacent to belonging, which is not the same as belonging. Adjacent is a kind of temporary permit. It had a certain warmth to it, which I accepted, because I was cold.

---

The police document check happened three weeks after I arrived. Not as a dramatic event — I want to be clear about this — but as an ordinary inconvenience that had the potential to become more.

I was on the metro, Ligovsky Prospekt station, early evening, coming back from the renovation site. A man in uniform was working his way through the car. This was standard. The check was random in the legal sense that there was no announced pattern, but in the operational sense it was highly specific: he was stopping men who looked young and dark and foreign, passing the men who looked like Petersburg.

I looked like what I was. He stopped. He asked for documents.

I gave him my Uzbek passport. He looked at it. He looked at me. He looked at the registration stamp from the woman in Primorsky.

A pause. In that pause I felt the specific sensation of a man who has prepared for an examination and is waiting for the first question, which is the question that will determine whether the preparation was sufficient.

He gave it back. He moved on to the next man.

I am telling you this as a minor event because in the arc of what would happen over the next two years in Petersburg and then Moscow, this one was minor. I had the rubber stamp and the rubber stamp was enough. The Kyrgyz brothers on the renovation crew did not always have adequate stamps, and their version of this encounter went differently. I would be lying if I said I did not notice the difference. I would also be lying if I said I did not feel something complicated about the difference: relief, and the particular shame of relief that comes from knowing the relief is unjust by a standard you cannot fully apply to yourself.

---

What I learned about St. Petersburg in the first six months was the following:

The marshrutki were faster than the metro for short distances and the routes were not posted anywhere accessible; you learned them by asking drivers or other passengers or by being wrong. The metro itself had a culture of silences — no one looked at strangers, no one made conversation, and the specific Petersburg metro silence was different from the Tashkent metro silence, which had its own quality but was warmer, or seemed warmer in memory, though I was remembering it from a standpoint of cold.

The bread in Petersburg was different from the bread in Tashkent. This is a small observation but it was present every day. Tashkent bread — non, the round flatbread with its stamped center, carried warm from the tandoor — was a morning ritual, social, perfumed, specific. Petersburg bread was dark rye sold in plastic from a grocery, or white brick from the state-style bakery, solid and adequate and entirely without ceremony. I ate it. I did not resent it. But I knew, in the specific way the body knows things, that I was eating the bread of a different place.

The white nights came in June. I had been told about the white nights but the knowing-of-the-thing and the experience-of-the-thing are not connected in the way one expects. At midnight in June the sky over the Neva was not dark. Not twilight-dark, not dusk-dark — it was the color of early evening at nine PM in Tashkent, a persistent light that came from the north at a low angle and did not go away. People sat on embankments and drank beer and talked. I walked to the Fontanka one night alone and sat on the stone embankment wall for an hour. The water was gray and the sky was gray-blue and there were other people sitting on embankments but none of them were sitting near me. The city was beautiful in the specific way that cities are beautiful when you do not belong to them yet and can therefore see them fully.

Whether I ever belonged to Petersburg I cannot say with confidence. By the time I left I knew the metro stops by feel and the marshrutka routes by logic and the specific register of cold that meant ice on the road rather than ice on the walls. But knowing a city's body is different from being claimed by it.

---

The money was seventy rubles a day when there was work, which was four or five days a week depending on the renovation schedule and Vadim Gennadievich's relationship to sobriety, which was genuinely variable. Sasha paid for my half of his shelf in the refrigerator for the first two months and I paid him back in November when I had accumulated enough to pay back something. The cost of the Primorsky stamp was a hundred rubles a month, budgeted first. The cost of the quilted jacket from Avtovo was three hundred and twenty rubles, paid for over two weeks at the cost of three days' food.

I was not poor in any absolute sense. I was poor in the specific sense of a person who has converted everything into arithmetic. At fifteen this arithmetic is clarifying in a way it would not be at thirty. Every decision has a number attached. The number tells you whether the decision can be made. I learned to work through the arithmetic quickly, instinctively, before wanting something too much.

This is one of the things that will later, in a different building, in a different city, prove to be a skill. At fifteen it was just arithmetic.

---

Winter deepened and then, slowly, became less itself. The cold lightened in a way that was perceptible only as duration — not as warmth, but as the decreasing amount of time spent doing the preparatory work of cold, the zipping and layering and preparation of extremities. By March I could walk to the marshrutka stop without the knuckle problem. By April the Neva ice broke. I watched it from the embankment with Sasha on a Sunday afternoon — the crack that starts somewhere upstream and travels downriver, the plates shifting and grinding, the whole surface of the river rearranging itself with a sound like a building trying to settle.

"First ice break," Sasha said, as though this were something to celebrate.

It was, in a way. The city was changing states. From one version of itself to another.

I was also changing states, though less visibly.

---

What changed was smaller than I would have expected from the inside of it. A name. A word. A way of looking.

In May, I was in the produkty store buying bread. The woman behind the counter was the same woman who was always behind the counter, mid-forties, not unkind, efficient in the way of a woman who has done this job in this store for ten years and has arranged the world around it into a stable system. She asked what I wanted. I told her. She got it. She said a price.

The transaction was finished. A man behind me was already waiting. But she looked at me — not the look of being stopped, not the look of hostility — just the look of a woman who has seen someone enough times to have a question.

"Where are you from?" she asked.

"Tashkent," I said.

"Uzbekistan?"

"Yes."

She handed me my change. "My colleague's daughter is there. Teaching school."

I said I understood. I took my change. I left.

This is a minor exchange. I am telling it because of what was in it: I had been asked where I was from, and the answer had been *Tashkent*, and this answer had located me in a way that was, in this particular case, neutral. For a moment, to this particular woman, Tashkent was a place where a colleague's daughter was teaching, and I was from that place, and we had a common referent, and the exchange was concluded on a note of modest human connection.

More often the exchange concluded differently.

"Where are you from?"

"Tashkent."

A pause. "*Aha.*" And the warmth left the eyes. Not cruelty. Simply a reclassification: from *person talking to me* to *person from Central Asia talking to me*, which is a different category entirely and carries with it a different set of expectations about what this person wants, whether this person will pay, whether this person is to be trusted, and whether this person's Russian — however correct, however native — is the Russian of someone who belongs in this city or the Russian of someone who has borrowed it without full ownership.

The *aha* happened more often than the other version. This is not a complaint. It is a fact about the city in 2000, which had accommodated enormous numbers of migrants from the former Soviet republics and had developed, through the nineties, a system of visible and invisible classifications for them. I was in one of the classifications. Which one depended on which day and which person I was talking to and which particular version of Central Asian I appeared to be.

I was not *churka* — the word was used for me twice in fifteen months, once by a teenage boy on Nevsky who said it at my back with his friends, once by a man on the metro who looked at my documents and used the word to his partner quietly, as though the word were simply factual, the way you say *sodium chloride* when you mean salt. I am not going to pretend the word did not land. It is not a word that lands lightly. But I am also not going to make it the weight-bearing element of this section, because it was not the dominant experience. The dominant experience was the *aha*, and the *aha* was worse in its way, because it was not hatred and therefore could not be argued with. It was just a reclassification, made quietly, in the course of ordinary daily life.

---

I found a second job in September, tutoring mathematics to a boy of twelve whose mother had heard through the chain of acquaintance that I was good at mathematics. This was true. The boy was Kostya, a quiet, near-sighted child from an apartment in the Frunzensky district, whose mother worked in a private clinic and wanted her son to pass his year-nine exams at above average. The rate was fifty rubles an hour and the lessons were twice a week.

The thing I understood about the tutoring, as opposed to the construction work, was that it placed me in a different position. The renovation work positioned me as a body in a space, moving materials according to instructions. The tutoring positioned me as someone with something the mother wanted for her son. This is a different kind of leverage, even when the person you are dealing with is entirely polite. Kostya's mother was polite. She made tea during the lessons and sometimes offered it to me and sometimes did not, which itself had a grammar I paid attention to. On the evenings when she made tea and offered it, she also asked questions — about my studies, about Tashkent, once about whether I planned to stay in Petersburg.

I said I did not know.

"You could go to university," she said.

I said that was possible.

She said no more about it. It was a seed she dropped and left for me to water or not, and I left it because the number attached to university, when I ran it, was not a number I could currently make work.

What I did with the tutoring money instead was open a savings column in the small notebook I had started keeping, the one with the costs of the Primorsky stamp and the quilted jacket and the bread. The savings column grew slowly. It was for something I did not yet have a name for, which turned out, by the following summer, to be Moscow.

---

By winter of the second year I had a better coat. I had the regular konstruksiya job, the tutoring twice a week, and a new sheet in the registration woman's book. I had learned the metro well enough to navigate it without looking at signs and to know which end of which car to enter to be closest to the exit I needed. I had eaten pelmeni from a stolovaya on Zagorodniy Prospekt enough times that the woman who served pelmeni knew my order before I said it. I had seen the ice on the Neva break twice.

I was, by any external measure, adapting.

The conversation I had not had — that I was not having with Sasha, because Sasha had not had it and would not know how to — was whether adapting was the right category. Adapting implies a destination. You adapt to something. I was learning the city the way you learn a piece of machinery: which levers do what, where it catches. This is useful knowledge. It is not the same as the city being yours.

---

There was a man in the kommunalka in the last months before I left for Moscow. He came to rent Sasha and Genka's room after Genka returned to his family in Volgograd and Sasha moved to share with a girlfriend. The man's name was Yelchin `[CONFIRM — composite name]`, and he was Azeri, forty-something, had been in Petersburg for ten years working various things he described as *eto i to* — "this and that" — with the evasive brevity of a man who has learned to describe his livelihood in ways that discourage follow-up questions.

Yelchin spoke Russian the way everyone in the apartment spoke Russian: as a first language, or near enough. He had left Baku in the early nineties, before the war, or during the war, or just after — the chronology shifted when he told it, which is the chronology of a man who has learned that some timelines are better left approximate.

He cooked excellent food. Not Uzbek food, not Russian food — he cooked in the way of a man from the Caucasus who has lived ten years in Petersburg and incorporated into his cooking the produce available in a northern city. He made a lamb dish with plums and walnuts that he ate over two days and that I ate once when he offered, and it tasted of a specificity I recognized as the specificity of belonging to a place — the food of a person who knew exactly what they were cooking and where it came from, which kitchen, which grandmother's variation.

We talked in the kitchen sometimes. He was not a man who asked intrusive questions, which made it possible to talk to him.

One evening in late winter he asked me how long I planned to stay in Petersburg.

I said I wasn't sure.

He nodded. He ladled the lamb into a bowl. "Petersburg is a good city," he said. "But it doesn't hold you. Moscow holds you. Moscow takes you and holds you." He paused. "Not always good."

I asked what he meant.

He waved his hand in a way that declined to be more specific. "You'll see," he said.

This was his assessment of Moscow delivered via a ten-year Petersburg veteran from Baku. It was not, in retrospect, wrong.

---

I left for Moscow in the spring of 2001. The reasons, insofar as they were articulable: the renovation work had become irregular as the Petrogradsky apartments dried up and Vadim Gennadievich was between projects. The tutoring had ended when Kostya's mother found a university student who lived in their district and was cheaper and did not require a commute. Sasha had moved in with his girlfriend and the room dynamic at the kommunalka had shifted in ways that made the floor of the new arrangement feel less settled.

There was also this, which I did not say and barely admitted even to myself: I was curious about Moscow. Curious in the way I had been curious about Petersburg — this is the country of my language, this is where it began, these are the places I grew up knowing by name and by weight. I had not found them in Petersburg. I was going to Moscow to find them or to confirm, with more evidence, that the search was wrong in its premise.

I took the night train. Moskovsky Vokzal in the early morning. The Leningrad Station at the other end, which is what Muscovites call the terminus because trains to Leningrad — to Petersburg, but they said Leningrad — depart from it. The city outside the station's arched entrance was louder than the city I had left.

Yelchin, when I told him I was going, said: "Moscow takes you."

I said I remembered.

"Don't let it take you entirely," he said. He did not explain this either.
