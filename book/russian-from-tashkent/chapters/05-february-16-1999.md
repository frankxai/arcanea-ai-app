# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 5: February 16, 1999

*Yunusabad, Tashkent — February 1999* `[CONFIRM-LOCATION: protagonist at home on school morning, or en route, or already at school]`

---

I woke before the alarm that morning because my sister was crying in the next room. `[CONFIRM-SISTER-AGE: she would be approximately 7–8, placed here in the small bedroom off the hallway]` Not the crying of a nightmare — she had the nightmare cry down, I knew its rhythm — but the crying of something having already happened, something found or broken, and I lay in my bed for a moment listening to my mother's voice from the hallway, low and even, the particular timbre she used when she was talking someone back from something.

The radiator was knocking. The February light through the thin curtains was the no-color light of eight in the morning, the light that makes everything in a room look like furniture.

I got up.

My sister had knocked over the small ceramic bird my father had brought back from somewhere — Samarkand, I think, or Fergana, one of the work trips that produced objects I had never asked about `[CONFIRM-CERAMIC-BIRD: any such object in household? Author can substitute]`. The bird lay in three pieces on the floor of the hallway. My mother was already on her knees collecting them, her back to me.

"I didn't mean to," my sister said.

"I know," my mother said.

My sister saw me and pulled her face into the look of catastrophe that I would recognize for the rest of her life — the specific expression she reserved for damage that had already been done and could not be undone. At seven she had already learned that some things cannot be put back. I remember thinking this was too young to know that. I remember that I said nothing about the bird because there was nothing to say.

I ate bread and drank tea. My father had already left. `[CONFIRM-FATHER-SCHEDULE: whether father left before family on workdays]` The kitchen was the kitchen. The window showed the courtyard between our block and the next, and the courtyard was a February courtyard, bare trees and a woman from the ground floor walking a very old dog along the path by the wall.

It is possible to narrate what happened that morning in terms of what I knew. It is more honest to narrate it in terms of what I did not know, which is almost everything.

---

School was forty minutes by foot or twenty by trolleybus, and I was running late, and I took the trolleybus. This is the level of decision-making available to a fourteen-year-old in Tashkent on an ordinary Tuesday in February.

The trolleybus had its smell: cold rubber and people and the faint electrical burning from the overhead line that I had never found in any other vehicle since. I stood near the middle doors and watched the street through glass that had been breathed on so many times it had lost its transparency in rings. February Tashkent through that glass: the plane trees on the boulevard pruned back to their knuckles, the pale walls of apartment blocks, a bread kiosk not yet open, a man on a bicycle dressed more for March than for the actual temperature.

Nothing was wrong with the city.

I remember this specifically, because it is the last memory I have of the city as simply itself, not yet organized by what was coming.

At the third stop before school a woman got on with two plastic bags and pushed past me with the specific Tashkent force that is not rudeness but volume, and I moved to the back of the trolleybus, and I was looking out the rear window at the street we'd just left, and that is when I heard it.

---

A sound.

Not an explosion — not the word. A compression. A thud that was also a pressure change, something felt in the chest before the ears understood it. One second of it, and then the trolleybus went on.

The other passengers: some looked up. The woman with the plastic bags put her hand on the overhead rail. A man in a gray coat turned his head slightly toward the window and then turned it back. Nobody said anything. This is what I remember most clearly. Nobody said anything.

The trolleybus went on for another block and then slowed to the stop and people got off and people got on and the city outside the window looked exactly as it had looked before the sound.

---

I did not know then that the Cabinet of Ministers building had its windows blown out. I did not know that Karimov had arrived late that morning — a piece of scheduling so mundane that it is still reported as fact without any accompanying sense of what it means to have the president of a country survive by being late. I did not know that six car bombs had been placed and six had detonated and that sixteen people were dead. `[CONFIRM-CASUALTY-FIGURES: research-bible.md states 16 killed, 120+ wounded — verify against author's memory of the numbers reported at the time]` I did not know any of the details, because I was fourteen and I was on a trolleybus and the city looked the same.

What I felt, if I am honest, was almost nothing. A sound that was not thunder. The passengers who did not speak. The city that continued.

I got off at my stop and walked the remaining six minutes to school.

---

The school was a three-story building from the Brezhnev era with a bulletin board in the main hallway that had the same handwritten notice on it from the previous semester. The notice said something about a parents' meeting that had long since passed. I had walked past this notice so many times I had stopped seeing it.

My class was 8B. `[CONFIRM-GRADE: protagonist at 14 in February 1999 would be in 8th grade — roughly equivalent. Confirm.]`

The classroom was already arranged when I arrived — this was unusual. Natalya Sergeyevna was standing at the front of the room with her hands folded in front of her, not at her desk, and everyone was in their seats, which at fourteen was never voluntary. A few of the girls had turned in their chairs to talk. The boys near the window were not talking.

I sat down.

The lesson did not begin. Natalya Sergeyevna stood with her hands folded and eventually the class went quiet because quiet was the correct response to a teacher who had decided to say nothing. I looked at Vadim next to me — he was my age, lived two streets over, we had been in the same class since fifth grade — and Vadim looked back with the expression that means: I know something and I can't say it here.

After perhaps three minutes Natalya Sergeyevna said: "Open your textbooks."

That was all.

We opened our textbooks. The lesson went. Russian language, I think — we were doing subordinate clauses, the kind of grammar exercise that requires sustained attention to something that does not matter. I could not have told you afterward what the exercise contained. I was attending to the silence around the lesson, the specific shape of what was not being discussed.

At the break Vadim caught me at the water fountain and said: "Bomby." Bombs. And then he was pulled away by someone from the other class and that was the entire conversation, and I had more than half the school day left to sit in rooms where the lesson continued exactly as scheduled.

---

*The city outside the school windows did not look different.*

*This is the thing I cannot explain to someone who was not there. When I say the city looked the same I mean: the same winter trees, the same gray sky of a February noon, the same woman who sold hot corn from a brazier near the school gate every day from October through March. The same people walking. The same trolleybus on the same route. A version of Tashkent in which I had been informed, by a word spoken at a water fountain, that bombs had gone off somewhere in the center this morning, and the city looked exactly like the city.*

*I know now that this is how it works. I know that the physical city and the political event are different registers that run in parallel and only intersect at checkpoints, at the faces of the adults, at the lowered voices. At fourteen I did not know this yet. I only noticed that the city looked the same while meaning something different, and I found this disturbing in a way I could not name.*

---

On the trolleybus home — I checked first; the line was still running — there was a different mood than the morning. Not panic. Not grief. Something I would describe now as a charged stillness. People were not talking in the way people usually did not talk on public transport: they were not talking in the way that indicated they had things they were deliberately not saying, rather than nothing to say.

I remember a man standing near the doors reading a folded newspaper with a particular concentration that was not reading. His eyes were not moving.

I remember a woman in a brown coat who spoke quietly to the woman beside her for the entire ride, and the second woman nodded and did not speak, and when my stop came I had still not heard what the first woman was saying.

At home, the radio was on. My mother was in the kitchen. She looked up when I came in.

"You heard," she said.

Not a question.

"At school," I said.

She nodded. She turned back to what she was doing at the counter and then turned back to me. "Your father called. He's fine. He'll be late."

I put my bag down.

"Where?" I said.

"The center," my mother said. "The government buildings. On the radio they said Karimov was not—" She stopped and thought for a moment. "They said he was late to the meeting."

I understood she was telling me Karimov was alive. I also understood, from the way she told me, that the specific grammar of this — late to the meeting — was doing a lot of work.

"Who did it," I said.

My mother shook her head, not in the direction of not knowing, but in the direction of: this is not a question we will be answering out loud.

---

The radio was the state radio, which was to say: the radio was Karimov's radio, which was to say: the radio had already organized the morning into a narrative. There were dead and wounded. There were enemies of Uzbekistan. There was a president who had survived. The language of the broadcast moved with a smoothness that I was old enough to recognize as rehearsed, as if the sentences had been prepared in advance of the event, which I would understand later was in some sense true: a government that has lived for a decade inside the grammar of threat has the post-threat announcements ready.

I did not know the word for this. I was fourteen. What I knew was: the radio voice had the quality of a teacher who has been told what to say and is saying it.

My mother listened with her hands busy, a dish towel in one hand, standing near the window. I watched her listening. This is what I remember: not the radio, which I have forgotten, but my mother's face while she listened to the radio. A face that was attending to two things at once — the words and what was underneath the words, the story and the story behind the story. She had been trained in this kind of listening for all of the years I had been alive and for all of the years before that.

At some point she turned the radio down.

---

My father came home later than usual, not very much later, but enough that my mother had put the food back on the stove once to keep it warm. He hung his coat, washed his hands, came to the table.

He said: "I came through Amir Temur Square. There are soldiers."

My mother set his plate down.

"How many," she said.

"Enough," he said.

This is the grammar of that household in that week: not many sentences, each one doing the work of many sentences. I was at the table. I ate. My sister `[CONFIRM-SISTER]` ate beside me and asked about something at her school, something about a performance or a test, I no longer remember what, and my parents answered her with the automatic warmth of parents attending to one child while thinking about another thing entirely.

After dinner my father sat with the newspaper. The newspaper — *Narodnoe Slovo*, which we had by that point switched to from *Pravda Vostoka* `[CONFIRM-NEWSPAPER-FAMILY-READS]` — had the bombings on the front page with two photographs. I read over his shoulder. He let me. The photographs showed the Cabinet of Ministers building, which I had walked past many times on school trips, with its windows dark where the blast had done something to them. The caption described an attack on the sovereignty of Uzbekistan.

"Papa," I said.

He looked up from the page.

"Who did it," I said.

He was quiet for a moment in the way that was not not-knowing. Then he said: "The paper says the Islamists."

"Is that what you think?"

He folded the newspaper with the care he always gave to folding, which was more care than it required. He put it on the table. He looked at me — which was unusual; he was not a man who looked at you when he was telling you something, he looked at the middle distance, the window, the tabletop.

"I think," he said, slowly, in the way of a man who is choosing words with genuine caution, not for effect, "that what happened today is what happens when a government needs something to happen."

Then he picked up the newspaper and opened it again, and I understood that the conversation was over.

I was fourteen. I filed this away somewhere below articulation, in the place where things go when you are too young to process them but old enough to know they matter. My father had just told me something he had never told me before, and he had done it so quietly that if anyone had been listening they could have argued he had said nothing at all.

---

*What do I know now that I did not know then?*

*I know that by the end of February 1999, somewhere between several hundred and several thousand people had been arrested in Tashkent and in the Fergana Valley. The government called them Islamists, Wahhabis, members of Hizb ut-Tahrir. Some of them had been members of those organizations. Many of them had not been anything except Muslim in a country that had decided to arrest Muslims.*

*I know that Jaslyk Prison opened that year, in the Karakalpakstan desert — a place so remote that its name was almost a joke, except that the people sent there were not joking and the conditions were not a joke. The names of men who died there would come out later, years later, through the patient work of people who wrote reports that the government ignored.*

*I know that the attribution — the question of who actually planted those six car bombs — has never been definitively settled. The official answer is the Islamic Movement of Uzbekistan. The Western analysts who studied it closely have proposed other possibilities. Some of these possibilities implicate people who had reasons to want the crackdown that followed. I do not know which is true. My father's sentence has stayed with me for twenty-five years, and I cannot tell you whether it was wisdom or paranoia, whether he was seeing clearly or whether he was fitting events to a theory he had already formed. He is still alive and we have never finished that conversation.*

*What I know is the way the city felt.*

---

In the days that followed — and here I must give up the pretense of exact sequence, because the days collapsed into a texture rather than a chronology — the city felt like a city that had decided someone was to blame.

This is different from a city that knows who is to blame. A city that knows is louder about it. A city that has decided moves differently: not loudly, not with accusation, but with a recalibrated attention. People looked at men with beards in a way that they had not looked before February 16. Not everyone. Not even most people. But enough people that the looking was there, and the men being looked at felt the looking, and changed the way they moved.

I noticed this because at fourteen the social physics of looking and being looked at is the primary curriculum.

The bearded men in my neighborhood were mostly older men, men my grandfather's generation `[CONFIRM-GRANDFATHER: whether paternal or maternal grandfather is part of household or nearby, whether devout]`, who had prayed quietly for decades, who had grown their beards as a matter of personal practice that had never before been anyone else's business. Now it was. Now the question of whether a man's beard had a political meaning was live, in the city's air, and the men themselves could feel the question being asked without words, and some of them began to come to the street with faces freshly shaved.

I remember this specifically. I remember a man I had always known — I have not named him and will not name him — who lived on the ground floor of the adjacent building and who had, for as long as I could remember, had a white beard cut short in the manner of the devout. One week after February 16 his beard was gone. I noticed it because it was a face I knew, and the face was the same face with a different frame, and the absence of the beard was so sudden and complete that it looked less like a haircut and more like a decision made under specific external pressure.

He did not look at me when I passed him that morning.

I did not look at him.

We had learned, by then, that certain things were better acknowledged by neither side.

---

The checkpoint appeared at the intersection two blocks from our building on a Wednesday, I think, maybe Thursday. `[CONFIRM-CHECKPOINT-TIMING: how quickly did checkpoints appear in protagonist's neighborhood post-bombing]` A police barrier — two sawhorses spray-painted orange, a uniformed officer, a plainclothes man nearby who was not pretending to be anything except what he was.

I walked past it on the way to school and the officer looked at me.

Not at me specifically. At me as a person in motion, at my face, at my age, at whatever it is a police officer in 1999 Tashkent looked for when he was looking.

I looked back.

This lasted perhaps one second. Then I was past the checkpoint and walking to school.

I had done nothing wrong. I was a fourteen-year-old boy on his way to school. The feeling I had was not guilt. It was something adjacent to guilt — a feeling of having been assessed, having been found provisionally acceptable, having been let through on terms that could be revised. I would not be able to name this feeling for many years. The name for it, I later learned, is the feeling of living in a country where the state has decided it is looking.

---

At school, the teachers said almost nothing. This was the consistent fact of the two weeks after the bombing: the adults around me had organized themselves into a silence that was not absence but shape. A shaped silence, like water taking the form of a container.

The geometry teacher — I will not give him a name `[CONFIRM: any teacher the author remembers from this period who can serve as anchor?]` — was a man in his fifties who had a way of working through proofs that suggested he found in mathematics a kind of proof against everything else. He taught us that week as if the lesson were the most important thing happening in the world. He may have believed this. The discipline of it — the insistence that the proof proceed, that the diagram be correct, that x equal only x — was either an escape or a defense. I was not sure which. I was not sure he knew which.

What none of the teachers did: offer an account of what had happened. The event had occurred. The government had said what it was. The government's account was the only account available in a classroom. Whether the teachers believed that account was not information they were going to share with eighth graders.

I had a friend, Kolya `[CONFIRM: any real Russian-speaking friend from this period the author can name]`, whose father was a policeman and who had heard, in the way that policemen's children sometimes heard things, that the arrests were happening in his father's district faster than the jails could hold people. I heard this at lunch, between two boys eating sandwiches in the February courtyard of the school, in the same volume I might have heard something about football. This is the specific Tashkent register I am trying to describe: information that was catastrophic in any other register, delivered and received in the flat tone of weather because no other tone was safe.

"Where do they put them," I said.

Kolya ate his sandwich. "His father didn't say."

The answer to where they put them was Jaslyk. We did not know this yet.

---

My mother's sister lived in the Chilanzar district `[CONFIRM-FAMILY-GEOGRAPHY: whether protagonist's family had relatives in Chilanzar or elsewhere in Tashkent]`, half an hour by bus, and she called every other day that week. I heard my mother's side of these conversations from the hallway, where I was doing something — homework, probably, or the performance of homework — while actually listening.

The conversations were in code that was not a code, that was simply the way women talked to each other in post-Soviet Tashkent when they had information they needed to share but were not willing to state directly, because there was a telephone line with a wire that went somewhere before it arrived at the other person, and the somewhere was the somewhere that lines went.

"You heard about Rashid," my mother's sister would say — and she was saying she had heard about a specific person.

"Yes," my mother would say.

"Yes," her sister would say.

This was a conversation about an arrest. I deduced this over several calls, in the way of a person who has no information except the negative space of what isn't being said.

I did not know Rashid. I would not have recognized him if I had passed him on the street. He was a name I had heard mentioned twice in adult conversation, and now he was a silence in adult conversation, and the silence was shaped exactly like a man who had been taken somewhere and whose name could not be said directly on a telephone.

---

*The city had decided. But what had it decided?*

*It had decided that the bombs were the work of religious extremists, because the government had said this, and because the government's account was the available account, and because, alongside the government's account, there was no other account that could be spoken out loud. The alternative accounts — my father's careful sentence, the rumors that moved through kitchens and telephone lines — had no public form. They were only shapes in private.*

*The decision the city had made was not a conclusion. It was a performance. Everyone performing the conclusion together, because the performance was what was required, and what was required was what was safe.*

*I was fourteen. I was learning the grammar of this city, this country, this specific way of living together. I was not learning it consciously — consciousness would have required language for it, and I did not have the language yet. I was learning it the way you learn the grammar of a language you have been speaking since childhood: through use, through the social consequences of getting it wrong, through watching the adults around me and understanding from their behavior what the rules were, even before anyone had told me what the rules were.*

*The rule was: agree in public, think in private, and maintain a very clear awareness of which register you were in at all times.*

*I would use this skill for the next fifteen years. It would get me every job I ever held.*

---

There was a boy in my class — I will call him Sanjar `[CONFIRM-NAME]`. He was Uzbek, had been in our class since sixth grade, was quiet and precise at mathematics in a way that I envied. After February 16, Sanjar was absent for four days. When he came back he sat in his usual seat and did not explain the absence, and no one asked, and this itself was a thing I noticed: the not-asking.

---

On the last day of February my father came home earlier than usual. He set his coat on the rack and came directly to the kitchen, where my mother was, and he sat down without washing his hands, which was the only time I had ever seen him sit down without washing his hands.

"Dmitry Alexandrovich is gone," he said.

I did not know who Dmitry Alexandrovich was. From context I understood he was someone at my father's workplace `[CONFIRM: whether father was at factory or elsewhere, and whether any colleague was arrested or fled during crackdown]`.

My mother sat down.

"When," she said.

"This week sometime. His wife called the plant."

"Where."

My father looked at the window.

"She doesn't know," he said.

My mother's hands were on the table. She looked at them. Then she looked at my father. Something passed between them in the way things pass between people who have been married for years and do not need words for the most important things.

I was in the doorway. They did not tell me to leave. They did not tell me to stay. I stayed.

"Here we are fine," my father said.

It was the same sentence my mother had said to me a decade before, on the edge of my bed, in a summer that had two months left.

Here we are fine.

It was not a statement of fact. It was a decision.

---

The sister: she asked me one evening what *bomby* meant. She was seven. She had heard the word. `[CONFIRM-SISTER-AGE]`

"Explosions," I said.

"Like fireworks?"

"No," I said.

She waited.

"More like—" I started, and then I couldn't finish the sentence, not because I didn't know what bombs were, but because I didn't know how to translate them into something a seven-year-old could hold. "Different," I said. "Louder."

"Did you hear them?"

"A little," I said. "On the trolleybus."

She thought about this. She had a small cloth rabbit that she carried when she was thinking. She was carrying it now.

"Is everything okay?" she said.

I looked at her. Seven years old, in the apartment in Yunusabad, in February 1999, holding a cloth rabbit.

"Here we are fine," I said.

She believed me because she was seven and I was her brother and this was still the time when brothers were believed.

I believed me a little less.

---

By the end of February the school announced that lessons would include a new component on the "defense of Uzbek sovereignty" — a short addition to the civics curriculum, two periods per month. Natalya Sergeyevna delivered this announcement with the face she had used, a decade earlier, for the hours of mandatory Uzbek language instruction — the face of a person performing compliance and nothing more.

The lesson, when it came, was exactly what you would expect: dates, names of organizations, the official narrative in its official form. We copied it into our notebooks. We would be tested on it.

The word *Wahhabi* appeared in the lesson. `[CONFIRM: this specific term was used in school curricula in the post-bombing period — author's direct memory is the primary source]` I wrote it down without knowing precisely what it meant, because at fourteen you write down what the teacher says and sort it out later. What I understood of it: a category of person. A dangerous category. The opposite of whatever we were supposed to be.

I wrote it down and I did not ask what it meant, and I did not ask because I understood — from the teacher's tone, from the context, from the new grammar of the city I had been absorbing since February 16 — that the correct response to this word was not inquiry but notation.

It was a sound with a danger attached to it. Like the word *again* in my father's voice, years earlier, at the kitchen table.

Some words you do not ask about.

You hear them and file them and wait to understand them from what happens next.
