# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 10: Hotel Years

*The [CONFIRM-HOTEL-NAME] Hotel, Tashkent — 2003–2006 · Ages 18–21*

---

The lobby was cold in a way that Tashkent buildings were not otherwise cold. American cold, I thought later, when I understood where air conditioning systems were designed and who they were designed for. The marble floor reflected it back. The chandeliers that hung above the check-in desk were large enough to require their own maintenance schedule, which I would learn about in my second week when a technician came to clean them and I had to explain to a German guest, in a German sentence I had mostly assembled correctly, why the lobby was slightly darker for forty minutes on a Tuesday morning.

My German was constructed from three years of school grammar and a cassette course I had borrowed from the Yunusabad library and returned without finishing. What I mean is: it was not German. It was a performance of German in front of someone who was not scrutinizing the performance too carefully because he wanted his key card replaced and had already moved on to thinking about his meeting.

This is what the front desk was. You learned, quickly, that most people at a front desk do not want a conversation. They want a transaction with the friction removed. Your job was to be the frictionless surface on which their transaction moved.

---

I was hired as a junior front desk agent `[CONFIRM-HOTEL-ROLE]`. This meant the desk slot from 7 AM to 3 PM, or the slot from 3 PM to 11 PM, rotating every two weeks. The morning shift was preferred by the senior agents; I was on afternoons and evenings for most of the first year, which suited me because the evenings were when the interesting guests arrived.

By interesting I mean: the ones for whom navigating Tashkent required something more than a room key.

The hotel at which I worked `[CONFIRM-HOTEL-NAME]` was one of perhaps three establishments in Tashkent in 2003 where a foreign business traveler could expect reliably working internet, a restaurant that would not give him intestinal difficulty, and a morning that smelled of coffee rather than of whatever the adjacent corridor happened to smell of. This made it the hotel of the diplomatic community, the international NGO sector, the oil and gas consultants, and a class of guest I came to think of as the authorized foreigners — people who were in Uzbekistan with the knowledge and approval of the Uzbek government, which meant they had either been invited or had navigated the visa system in a way that implied institutional backing. Tourists were rare. Tashkent was not, in 2003, on the path of anyone who was simply traveling for pleasure.

The practical effect of this guest mix was that the lobby was a reliable map of current geopolitics. The weeks when the American military contractors arrived — large men in civilian clothing who moved in groups and tipped in cash — were the weeks when the diplomatic relationship with the United States was at its warmest. The weeks when the French cultural mission was in residence, or the German development agency, were different weeks, with different political weather. After May 2005 and the Andijan massacre and the K2 eviction, the Americans stopped coming as frequently and the Russians started coming more, which was a geopolitical fact I read first from the lobby occupancy before I read it anywhere else.

I learned this language without being taught it. It is one of those skills that service work produces without announcing itself as education.

---

There was a woman at the desk before me — Malika, who had been there since the hotel opened and who had the specific authority of someone who knows where all the towels are `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]`. She was Uzbek, from a Tashkent family, and she spoke Russian with the slight correction that educated Uzbeks used in the mid-2000s, the Russian of someone who had chosen to use it rather than inherited it — a subtly different instrument. She showed me the systems. The PMS terminal. The key card encoder. The blocking procedure for VIP rooms. The telephone routing for calls that came in asking for guests by name rather than by room number, which were handled with a specific formula: *May I ask who is calling?* followed by *I'll connect you to their room* or, depending on the guest's flag in the system, *I'm afraid I don't have that name in our records at this time*, which was the formula for guests who had specifically requested discretion.

"Who asks for discretion?" I asked her, during the first week.

"People who have reasons," she said.

This was the complete answer. I understood, over the following months, that the complete answer was complete: there was no taxonomy of reasons that the desk needed to know, only the formula to apply and the judgment about when to apply it.

The judgment was the job. Everything else was just the vocabulary.

---

The guest I will call Mr. V. arrived on a Tuesday in late 2003, which was either November or December `[CONFIRM-ANCHOR-SCENE-SEASON]`, in a taxi from the airport with two hard-shell cases that he insisted on carrying himself despite my offer of the porter. He was German — from the passport, from the very organized way he presented his documents before being asked — and he was there for the electrical equipment tender at the Ministry of Energy or some ministry adjacent to energy, I no longer remember which, because there were many such guests in those years and many such tenders. What I remember is that he had a problem.

The problem was that his laptop adapter had failed on the flight, or he thought it had, and he needed an adapter compatible with his German-plug laptop for the Uzbek socket, which is the Soviet C/F socket, round pins, two-pronged, 220V, and which is different from the German Schuko socket in a way that is both significant and simple, which is to say: not the same but easily bridged by a standard universal adapter, which the hotel sold in the gift shop for a price that was slightly more than the adapter was worth, which was itself slightly more than anyone should pay for a piece of plastic and copper wire, but which was available at eleven-thirty at night when the guest needed it.

I sent the bellman to the gift shop for the adapter. I called the engineer on duty to confirm the room voltage was stable. I upgraded the guest from a standard room to a superior room because we had availability and because the small gesture of an upgrade at eleven-thirty when a man's adapter had failed and he had a meeting in the morning was the kind of gesture that turned a guest into a return guest. I did this without consulting anyone because Malika, in one of the quiet teaching moments that she distributed without marking them as teaching, had told me in the first week: the upgrades that matter are the ones the guest doesn't expect and that you give before they have to ask.

Mr. V. looked at the new key card. He looked at me.

"This is a different room."

"Superior room, same rate. The view is better in the morning."

He said nothing for a moment. He was calculating something — not the money, he was not the type to care about whether an upgrade meant a different rate; he was calculating whether this was a trap of some kind, whether the hotel or I personally wanted something from him. This was a reasonable calculation in Tashkent. In Karimov's Uzbekistan, services rendered unexpectedly were not always without strings. I had watched enough guests make this calculation to recognize it.

"The view," he said.

"The Amir Temur monument is directly south. In the morning light."

He took the key card.

"Very good," he said. It came out slightly formal, slightly stiff, the way appreciation sounds when it is crossing a language and a cultural gap and trying not to spill.

He left a substantial tip with the front desk the next morning when he checked out, in an envelope addressed to no one, which Malika accepted on behalf of the team and distributed with the equanimity of someone who has been distributing envelopes for some time. I received my share. It was not large. It was not nothing.

This is not the story of a significant exchange. It is the story of a transaction done correctly, which in the context of a foreign guest in an authoritarian state in 2003 had a slightly different meaning than it would have had in Frankfurt or Amsterdam. For the guest, the transaction was a moment of unexpected competence in an environment where unexpected things were usually problems. For me, it was the twenty-third such transaction of the month, distinguished from the others by nothing except that I have remembered it.

I have remembered it because Mr. V. came back six months later, and when he arrived at the desk he recognized me, and what I saw in his face when he recognized me was something close to relief — the relief of a man who knows the system, who knows the name, who knows that the frictionless surface is still there. By then I understood that this recognition was a form of currency. He recognized me as competent, which in the hotel industry translates into something real: the repeat guest, the recommendation, the small professional gravity that pulls resources in your direction.

Malika, who had watched this from the end of the desk, said nothing until the guest had gone to his room. Then she said: "He remembered you."

"Yes."

"He'll ask for you next time."

"Probably."

She looked at me with an expression that was not quite approval but that was the next best thing, which was acknowledgment. "That's how it works," she said, and went back to the terminal.

---

What the hotel was teaching me, over three years, was a set of skills that were not on any job description. The job description said: greet guests, process check-in and check-out, handle complaints, coordinate with housekeeping and maintenance, accept payment, issue keys. What the job actually was: learn to read the geometry of power in a room before the room told you what it was. Learn which guest had been sent by the Ministry of Finance and which had come through the Chamber of Commerce and which was a journalist who had been given a visa on the understanding that the journalism would not be about anything that mattered. Learn when to perform ignorance and when to perform knowledge and which of these the person at the desk needed from you at the moment they were standing at the desk.

These are not sinister skills. They are the skills of any person who occupies the middle position in any hierarchy — the translator, the receptionist, the local employee of the international institution. You are the hinge. The door opens both ways and you are what the hinge is made of.

My English improved substantially. The hotel had a language program for local staff — two evenings a week, an American-accented Uzbek woman who had spent three years at an institute in Tashkent and one semester on a program in Indiana and who taught English the way you teach a tool rather than a language, which was what the desk required. Grammar was for the Uzbek-English speakers who were going to write reports. For the desk, what you needed was: the register of warmth at check-in, the register of efficiency at complaint resolution, the register of regret when something was unavailable, the register of apology that was not admission of fault. These are not the same sentence in English and they are not the same sentence in Russian and the art of the desk is knowing which you need before the guest has finished their sentence.

My Russian had always been this instrument. I now had it in English too. I was beginning to have it in Uzbek in certain rooms, though Uzbek at the front desk was still, in 2003 and 2004, unusual enough to produce visible surprise in Uzbek guests — the small pleasure they took in being spoken to in their own language by someone who had no obvious reason to speak it.

I was twenty years old. I was working for a wage that was, by local standards, excellent, and by any other standard, not. I had a narrow bed in my parents' apartment and a commute on the marshrutka and colleagues who were, most of them, my age or slightly older, and who shared the particular condition of people who are more capable than the economy around them has yet found a use for.

---

In 2004 there were two sets of bombings in Tashkent, in March and July. I will not describe them with the thoroughness of the 1999 chapter — I was older, the events were less shocking in the sense that by 2004 you understood the pattern, and besides, the 2004 attacks were smaller and their political use was faster and less complicated. What I will describe is the hotel lobby in the days after each set of attacks.

The lobby changed. Not the lobby's furniture or its marble or its chandelier. The lobby's composition — who was in it, how they moved through it, what they said to each other and to us. After the March bombings: the diplomatic community guests accelerated their check-outs. Several NGO missions reduced their Tashkent footprint. A UN agency that had maintained a permanent booking on four rooms reduced it to two. What was left: the businessmen from the Russian Federation, who were not especially troubled, and the Uzbek government adjacent guests, who were also not especially troubled, and who were in fact, in the weeks following each set of attacks, more numerous than usual, for the obvious reason that Karimov's security state had, once again, a stated emergency that justified and extended its operations.

I learned to read the occupancy as a political instrument. Not in a sophisticated way — I was twenty years old, I was checking in guests — but in the way that any perceptive person in a position of structural observation learns to read their environment. The hotel register was a secondary newspaper. It told you, a week after the events, what the events had meant to the world that moved through the hotel.

The desk, during those weeks, required more. Guests who were frightened needed a different register than guests who were neutral. Guests who were Uzbek and government-connected needed a different register than guests who were foreign and privately nervous. I had the Russian-speaker's advantage: the primary diplomatic language of the post-Soviet space was still Russian, which meant I could read and speak to the full range of the hotel's guests without the mediation of an interpreter. This made me, in practice, more useful than the formal job title indicated.

It also made me, in ways I was only beginning to understand, more visible.

---

The question of what comes next is not the same question at twenty as it is at twenty-five. At twenty, the question has a provisional quality — it is a question you can table, because you are twenty, because the world has not yet insisted on an answer. At twenty-one, twenty-two, the question becomes less provisional. You begin to notice that the desk is not a temporary position but a structure, with people above you who have been there longer and people below you who have arrived more recently, and that the structure has a ceiling, and that the ceiling is lower than you would prefer to believe.

There was a senior desk supervisor — I will call him Timur `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]` — who had been at the hotel for seven years by the time I arrived, and who knew the guest database the way a librarian knows the stacks. He was Uzbek, Tashkent-born, and had gone to the same language institute as the English teacher, and he was, by the calculation of the hotel, at the top of what a local employee could aspire to. Above him was the Rooms Division Manager, who was Uzbek but who held a degree from a Swiss hospitality school `[CONFIRM-DETAIL]`, and above her was the General Manager, who was German and was on an expatriate package that included housing and a car and a salary structure that existed on a different planet than the som-based reality of the local staff.

I do not say this as complaint. The structure existed. It was not invisible. What you did with the observation of it was the interesting question.

Timur had observed it for seven years and had reached a conclusion I did not agree with, which was that the structure was fixed and that the correct response to a fixed structure was to occupy your position in it with competence and without grief. He was good at his job in the way that people who have accepted their limits are sometimes good at their jobs: with a thoroughness and attention to detail that arises from the absence of distraction. He did not look at the ceiling. He attended to the desk.

I attended to the desk and also looked at the ceiling.

I do not mean this as a monument to ambition. I was not especially ambitious — the character bible of my own life at twenty-two records arithmetic, not ambition, and the arithmetic at twenty-two was the same arithmetic as at any age: what must be true for me to not still be in this position at thirty-five. The arithmetic included the hotel, which was better than most alternatives I could see, and it included Russia, which I had been to and which had been clear about its position, and it included something else that I had begun to notice in the lobby, which was the building across the avenue.

I want to be precise about what I mean by "began to notice." I do not mean that I suddenly discovered the existence of a United States Embassy in Tashkent. I had known the Embassy existed since I was a child — it was on Chilanzarskaya Street, in Chilanzar district, and then it moved in 2006 to the new compound in Yunusabad, which was the district where I had grown up, which was either ironic or simply geographic. What I mean by began to notice is this: I became aware, over the course of the hotel years, of the people who arrived from the Embassy. Not the diplomats — they stayed at the hotel less often, they had their own housing arrangements — but the events, the working groups, the program visits. The Embassy organized events at the hotel from time to time. Small conferences. Language program graduation receptions. A briefing for business contacts that I was asked to staff for two hours one evening, handing out name badges and directing people to the function room, which was not skilled work but was adjacent to the world I was becoming curious about.

The people from the Embassy who came to these events were: American diplomats in business casual who had the specific quality of people accustomed to being in foreign countries without having become part of them; and Uzbek staff who were something else, something harder to categorize — my age or a few years older, formally dressed in a way that was neither the hotel's uniform nor the embassy visitor's business casual but something in between, something constructed, something that said: I have thought carefully about how to appear in this room, and the thinking has been successful.

I watched those Uzbek staff with an attention I had not yet learned to disguise.

---

In the spring of 2005, before Andijan, before everything became different, there was an afternoon at the desk that I have thought about many times. It was April — a good month in Tashkent, the apricots in bloom, the city briefly beautiful in the way it is only beautiful in April, before the summer heat kills everything's ambiguity. A woman came to the desk alone, without luggage, and asked in Russian whether she could use the hotel business center to print a document. She was Uzbek, middle-aged, wearing a headscarf — not dramatically, not in the ostentatious way that was by then politically inadvisable, but a headscarf nonetheless, which in Tashkent in 2005 was a small and specific act. She had a USB drive.

I told her the business center rate and she said yes and I walked her there and showed her the printer settings and came back to the desk. Standard. Fifteen minutes later she came back to the desk to return the key card for the business center and pay the fee. I charged her. She counted out the som carefully.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome," I said, in Russian.

She looked at me for a moment. "Do you speak Uzbek?" she said, in Uzbek.

"A little," I said, in Uzbek.

She nodded, as if confirming something, and left.

I do not know what she was printing. I do not know her name or her circumstance. I know that she had a headscarf and a USB drive and was using a hotel business center to print a document in April 2005, which was six weeks before Andijan, in a city where that combination of details had a specific register. I know that I charged her the standard rate and that she thanked me and that we spoke two languages in thirty seconds.

I have thought about that afternoon many times because it is the kind of afternoon that has no significance in itself — a transaction, correctly handled — and that nevertheless accumulates significance in retrospect, as the context around it hardens into history. She was someone navigating the city that April. I was someone navigating the desk. We were, for thirty seconds, both in the same room.

---

Andijan happened in May.

At the hotel, we learned what had happened the way you learn things in a city where the official news is managed: in pieces, from different directions, each piece arriving with a disclaimer about its provenance. A British journalist who was in the lobby at the time had information from a colleague who had been in the Fergana Valley. A Uzbek government official who checked in two days later had a version that matched the state television version, which was the version about the terrorists and the forty-seven, which was the early official count `[CONFIRM-EARLY-OFFICIAL-COUNT]`. A development organization worker from a European country, who had been in Uzbekistan for four years and knew the shape of things, sat at the lobby bar on the evening of May fifteenth and drank two beers without speaking and then said to me, when I asked if there was anything else he needed: "I'll be leaving next week."

I said I hoped it wasn't something we'd done.

He looked at me. "No," he said. "It's something they did."

He did not specify who they were. He did not need to. This was the lobby in May 2005 — a room where the specification was available to anyone paying attention.

I was paying attention.

The weeks that followed: the K2 eviction was announced in July. The hotel's occupancy among American-adjacent guests fell sharply. The German development agency extended its booking. The Russian delegation's visits increased. I tracked this not with a spreadsheet but with the involuntary accounting of someone who spends eight hours a day in a room where geopolitics has a room rate.

My English, by this point, was functional. My Russian had always been first-language. My Uzbek, as I have said, was the Uzbek of the street and the market and thirty seconds of conversation with a woman at a business center. I was twenty years old. I was at the front desk of an international hotel in a city that was reorganizing itself, again, around a political event I could read only from its effects on a lobby.

In September of 2005 I saw the posting `[CONFIRM-EMBASSY-POSTING-YEAR: 2005 or later]`.

It was on a bulletin board at the English language institute where I had been taking evening classes for a year. The US Embassy was looking for locally employed staff in the consular section. The requirements were: Uzbek citizenship, English and Russian fluency, secondary education minimum, no criminal record, availability for a background investigation. The compensation was listed in a range that was, at the lower end, three times my current salary.

I read the posting three times. Then I took out the pen I carried for signing guest registration forms and I wrote down the address.

---

I did not apply immediately. This is worth noting because the delay says something that the application does not. I knew what applying for a position at the US Embassy meant in Tashkent in the autumn of 2005: it meant the SNB had the application within a week of its submission. It meant that my name was added to whatever file the SNB maintained on persons with institutional contacts to foreign governments — a file that, in the post-Andijan period, was being examined with renewed attention. It meant that colleagues at the hotel who knew I had applied would know, or suspect, or at minimum be available to be asked whether they knew.

I discussed it with no one at the hotel. I told my mother in a sentence — "there's a position at the Embassy, I'm thinking of applying" — and waited for her response.

She said: "What section?"

I said: "Consular."

She was quiet for a moment in the way she was quiet when she was doing arithmetic. "That's good work," she said, eventually. Which was the totality of her endorsement, because she was a woman who understood that what you said in a kitchen was not private in a way that was absolute and that therefore the complete expression of support was: *that's good work*, said once, and then returning to whatever she had been doing.

My father said, the next morning, that he had a friend who had worked at a foreign consulate in the nineties who had told him the hours were long. This was either advice or approval. With my father, the two were often the same thing.

In November I submitted the application `[CONFIRM-APPLICATION-YEAR]`.

The background investigation took several months. I kept coming to the desk every morning. I kept processing the check-ins and check-outs, the key card failures and the upgrade requests and the occasional situation that required the formula for discretion. I kept watching the lobby.

When the letter came I was standing at the desk at seven-fifteen in the morning, a Tuesday, the morning shift I had finally been given after two and a half years of alternating afternoons. Malika had given me the shift as a parting gift, I think — she did not say so, but the timing was exact. She knew about the application. She had not said a word about it in six months. When I showed her the letter she read it and gave it back and said: "They'll want a specific shirt."

"I know."

"Not that one."

I looked at my current shirt. It was the hotel-issue white button-down. "The hotel's shirt?"

"Your shirt. Something you bought."

I nodded.

"The Chorsu market," she said. "Second level, left corridor. There's a man who sells formal shirts that are actually formal."

She turned back to the terminal. I put the letter in my jacket pocket.

The lobby was the same lobby. The chandelier was the same chandelier, its geometry of light landing on the same marble in the same way it had for three years. I had polished a great deal of myself on this marble without anyone announcing it as education.
