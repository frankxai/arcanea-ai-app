# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 9: Return

*Tashkent — Chirchiq — Yunusabad — 2002–2003* `[CONFIRM-RETURN-YEAR: 2002 or 2003]`

---

The flight from Moscow came in at dawn, which is the only appropriate time to return to a city you are not sure wants you back.

I had a bag with two changes of clothes, a winter coat that had been adequate for Petersburg and was already wrong for the Tashkent September, and the specific posture of a young man who had learned, in two years of being the wrong kind of Russian, that the correct way to move through an airport is to occupy as little space as possible. The customs officer at Tashkent International looked at my passport for a long time. It was the same face I had been looked at with in Moscow — a different language behind the look, but the underlying calculation identical: what are you, and do I need to be concerned about it. I was twenty-two `[CONFIRM-RETURN-YEAR]`. I weighed less than I had when I left. The officer stamped the passport without speaking and slid it back across the counter.

Outside the terminal the smell hit first. Tashkent in September smells of damp cotton and diesel and the specific heated dust that accumulates in the plane trees along Amir Temur, and underneath that, on certain mornings, of melon from the trucks coming in from the Fergana Valley. I had not smelled it in two years and I smelled it and felt nothing in particular except that it was known, which was not the same as feeling at home.

My mother was there. She had taken the bus from Yunusabad at five in the morning to be there before the flight landed, which was the sort of thing she did that I only understood as extravagance later, when I was old enough to understand that it had cost her something to spend the early hours of a September morning on a bench in Tashkent International rather than in her kitchen. She looked smaller. This was the standard observation: when you leave for two years and come back, the adults are smaller. I do not know if it was the Petersburg cold or the absence or the fact that I had been, for most of those two years, slightly afraid, but when I saw her I was aware of a physical weight behind my sternum that I had been carrying without naming and that named itself, now, as relief.

She held my face in both hands for a moment. She did not say she had been worried.

"You're thin," she said.

"Petersburg."

"I know," she said, as if that explained something medical.

---

The city on the way in from the airport was Karimov's version of Tashkent — which is to say, a surface of white marble and new plazas laid over the Soviet bones without disturbing the bones in any significant way. The road from Tashkent International passed the new Amir Temur monument, the horseman in bronze who had replaced Lenin on the central square sometime in the early nineties, and I noticed that there was a new building going up near the square, white and slightly too large for its site, the kind of building that announces a government program. My mother noticed me noticing.

"The administration building," she said. "For the Ministry of something." She said the last part with the careful imprecision of a woman who had learned, over the years, that knowing which ministry did not add anything useful to your life.

The Yunusabad apartment was the same apartment. I had been in Petersburg and Moscow for two years and come back to the same apartment and there was something in this continuity that I had no adequate language for — not comfort exactly, not its opposite. The shelves were still the shelves. The kitchen table still sat four though there were only three of us now `[CONFIRM-SIBLING-STATUS: sister gone by this point?]`. My mother had put clean sheets on the narrow bed in the room I had shared with no one since childhood. The narrow bed was narrower than I remembered.

My father came in from the other room. He had aged more than my mother. He was wearing the house cardigan and his slippers and his face when he saw me had something that was not quite relief and not quite accusation, a mixture of the two that I would take weeks to understand and never fully resolve into language. He shook my hand.

"You're back," he said.

"I'm back."

He nodded as if I had confirmed something he had known but had needed to hear stated directly. He went to the kitchen. My mother was already making tea.

---

I spent the first week doing almost nothing. This was partly exhaustion and partly the specific paralysis that comes from having your reference point removed. In Petersburg and Moscow I had known what I was: the *tashkentets*, the Central Asian who spoke perfect Russian, the person whose documents said one place but whose accent said another. The category was uncomfortable but it was a category, which gave you a wall to push against. Back in Tashkent I had no such wall. I was from here. I spoke the language of here. I knew which bus went where and which market had the cheapest bread and which streets flooded in spring. None of this gave me a future. It gave me a location.

My father went to work every morning at seven. He was at the technical institute `[CONFIRM-FATHER-EMPLOYMENT-2002]`, doing something with electrical systems consulting that was no longer really consulting and no longer really paid what consulting should pay but that kept him in a building with colleagues and a desk and the structure that a Soviet-trained engineer needed the way other people needed sleep. He had been there for several years by then, since the factory work became what the factory work had become. He did not talk about the factory. When I asked my mother, once, she said he was fine, which meant the subject was closed.

My mother asked about Russia. I told her about the cold. I told her about the communal apartment on Ligovsky where the gas flame on the stove didn't go all the way out at night and the woman in the next room slept with her dog in the kitchen. I told her about the construction work in summer and the restaurant kitchen work in winter and the document checks on the metro and the cold in a way that made it seem like a story about weather rather than what it had actually been, which was two years of being in a city that was mine by language and not by anything else.

She listened and did not push. She had the particular skill of my mother's generation and class: the ability to understand what had been withheld without pressing for it. She poured more tea.

I did not tell her about the night on Nevsky when two men in tracksuits had asked me where I was from and the answer I gave — Tashkent — had produced a pause and then a specific look and then the instruction, polite enough, to walk another direction. I did not tell her about the foreman on the construction site who had said, in front of five other workers, that Central Asians knew nothing about real cold and that was why they built such ugly buildings in their countries. I did not tell her about any of the specific education Russia had offered me on the subject of what I was.

---

Chirchiq, when I visited, was smaller.

I went out on the marshrutka on a Tuesday in October, not because there was any particular reason to go but because Chirchiq was where I had been a child and I needed to check whether the childhood was still there or whether Russia had done something to my capacity to locate it. The road ran through the industrial belt northeast of Tashkent, past the chemical plant whose towers were visible from the highway, past the transformer plant whose name I had known since I was old enough to know what transformers were because my father had colleagues who worked there, past the flat cotton fields that were past their season, the cotton already picked, the plants brown and exhausted in the October light.

The city itself was slower than I remembered. This was not nostalgia working against perception — the city was objectively slower, because the population had thinned. In the apartment block where my grandmother's sister had lived `[CONFIRM-CHIRCHIQ-CONNECTION]`, the courtyard had fewer children than it had in the nineties. The dvor was not empty, but the Russian-speaking children who had been the majority when I was young were now a minority, or absent entirely, and the ones remaining were the ones whose families had no good reason to go and had stayed by a logic I recognized because it was the same logic my own family used: we are from here, we have always been from here, where exactly would we go.

I walked down the main street of Chirchiq and counted what had changed. A new mosque, completed, that had been under construction when I left — this was not unusual, mosques had gone up across Uzbekistan through the nineties under a kind of permitted religion policy that had since become considerably more complicated. A building that had been a gosmagazin when I was a child and was now a private food shop selling Turkish chocolate and Israeli orange juice at dollar prices. A photograph of Karimov on the side of the administration building, the familiar blue-suit pose.

What had not changed: the smell of the river. The chinar trees. The transformer plant's chimney visible from the main street, the same chimney, the same height. I stood in the main street of Chirchiq on a Tuesday morning in October and understood that I could live here. I understood it the way you understand something is possible without it being desirable, the way you understand you could eat a particular food every day for the rest of your life without anyone being able to argue it would kill you.

---

I found a job in November, through a cousin of a school friend who worked in logistics `[CONFIRM-FIRST-JOB-ON-RETURN]`. I will not claim I found it through distinction or application or anything more dignified than the particular telephone network of Tashkent, which in 2002 operated the way it had always operated: you knew someone who knew someone, and the knowing had value proportional to how many debts and favors the chain of knowing could activate. The work was administrative — data entry, document processing, telephone calls to suppliers in Chirchiq and Bukhara and occasionally Almaty. The office was in a rented room in a building near Chorsu, up three flights of stairs, with a window that looked at another window. I was paid in cash, in som, and the som was adequate if you converted it mentally into dollars and did not convert it for too long.

My father said nothing about the work except that it was good to be working. This was the most he could say about it without saying something that acknowledged what the work was, which was the work of a young man killing time in the ruins of a planned economy while the new economy went about its business of making the wrong people wealthy.

I went to work in the morning and came home in the evening and ate with my parents and watched the news and went to bed. The news was Uzbek television, which by 2002 was a highly polished instrument for saying nothing of substance in four languages. Karimov appeared. Ministers appeared. There were reports on cotton yield and industrial output and the cultural heritage of the Silk Road. Nobody on Uzbek television reported on what was happening to men in the Fergana Valley who attended mosques that weren't state-approved mosques. What was happening to those men was available by other means — by conversation, by the specific silences that fell when certain names came up, by the neighbors who had stopped going to Friday prayers and when you asked why changed the subject to something about their digestion.

---

My mother made plov on Sundays. This is not nostalgia — it is a calendrical fact. She made it in the cast-iron *kazan* that had been her mother's, on the gas burner at full pressure, and the smell of the heated cotton oil and the carrots going in and then the meat and then the rice filled the apartment from about ten in the morning, which was when she started, until noon, which was when we ate. My father set the table. I sat on the stool by the window that I had always sat on, which was too small for me now but which I sat on anyway by the force of whatever habit is made of when the habit is attached to a particular chair and a particular window and a particular light in the October afternoon.

We ate plov and drank tea from the *podstakannik* that had been her father's and did not talk about Russia or Chirchiq or the future or the colleagues from the factory who had left for Chelyabinsk or Novosibirsk or Tel Aviv. We talked about things that were in the apartment: the plov, the neighbor upstairs who had acquired a television with a large screen and watched it at high volume, whether my mother needed to replace the burner on the stove or whether it could be repaired.

This was not avoidance. Or rather, it was avoidance, but the way a dam is avoidance — a structure built for a reason, maintained with care, entirely necessary for the landscape it creates.

Outside the window the Yunusabad apartment blocks ran their long flat geometry against the October sky. The city was doing what the city did. I was home in the specific sense that the body knows — the smell of cotton oil and the particular light and the podstakannik — and not home in the sense that mattered now, which was the sense of: what does a twenty-year-old do with this?

I did not have an answer. I had a job I would not keep for long and a narrow bed that was too narrow and a city that was mine and was not mine and the slowly forming understanding that these two things — mine and not mine — were not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived.

I did not come to this understanding elegantly. It arrived the way most understandings arrive: gradually and then all at once, in a marshrutka on a Tuesday, watching Chirchiq's main street go past.

The transformer plant chimney above the roofline. The same chimney. I looked at it for a long time.

There was a man at my father's technical institute who had done postgraduate work in Leningrad before independence — he was sixty, maybe sixty-five, and he had been at the institute since before I was born, and he knew my father in the way people who have worked in the same building for twenty years know each other, which is to say: with the particular intimacy of shared professional survival without ever having said anything personal. He came for tea once, that winter, and told me that his son was in Germany. Stuttgart. Working in something with automobiles.

I said that was good.

"He calls every two weeks," the man said. He said this the way you say something that is both better and worse than the alternative.

"My sister's in `[CONFIRM-SIBLING-DESTINATION]`," I said.

He nodded. We drank tea.

After he left my father sat for a while at the kitchen table and did not read the newspaper, which was unusual. I did not ask what he was thinking. He would not have told me, not because he was withholding but because there are certain thoughts that do not improve from being stated. I knew what the thoughts were. He knew I knew. This was sufficient.

What my father and I had, in that kitchen, in that winter, was not understanding exactly — it was something more like adjacency. Two people who had come to the same conclusion by entirely different routes and who were sitting together in the kitchen where the conclusion had been reached, neither of them required to say the conclusion out loud.

The conclusion was approximately this: we are from here, and here is not finished with us yet, and we will see.

---

By spring I had begun to think about the hotels.

Not seriously at first. I had a friend — Aleksei, who had been a year ahead of me in school and who had the specific advantage of an uncle in the service industry `[CONFIRM-ALEKSEI-HOTEL-CONNECTION]` — who worked front desk at one of the international hotels on the city's main boulevard. He had told me, the previous autumn, that they were hiring. I had not followed up for six months, which I attributed at the time to inertia and attribute now to a subtler form of calculation: I needed to be back long enough to know I was staying before I was willing to build something here.

By March I was staying. I applied. I wore the shirt that was the most formal shirt I owned, which was a white button-down that had been bought in Moscow for a job interview I had not gotten. I went to the hotel. I sat in an office with a woman named Zulfiya `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]` who asked me questions in Russian and then in English and then, briefly, in Uzbek, which I answered in the approximate, street-learned Uzbek of someone who had grown up in a Russian-speaking household in a neighborhood where Uzbek was the language outside the apartment, which was enough to pass. She looked at my application form.

"Russia," she said. Not a question.

"Two years. St. Petersburg, Moscow."

She made a mark on the form. The mark could have meant anything.

She called me three weeks later. I started in April.
