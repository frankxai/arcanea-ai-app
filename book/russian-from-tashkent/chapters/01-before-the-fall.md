## Chapter 1 · Before the Fall

*Yunusabad, Tashkent — Summer 1989* `[CONFIRM-YEAR: 1988 or 1989]`

---

The crack in the courtyard asphalt was wide enough for two ants but not for three, and I had been watching for some time how each ant came to its small geometry alone. The grain of bread they were carrying was larger than the ant carrying it. The grain went into the crack. The ant did not.

I do not remember the year as a number. I remember it as the courtyard between blocks twelve and fourteen, the chinar tree whose lowest leaves had begun to turn the particular yellow of a Tashkent August, and the heat that was the temperature of my skin and the temperature of the air both, so that I could not say where I ended.

There was a sound from above me. My mother's voice, from a fifth-floor balcony, calling my name — which I will not write down here, because the name belongs to the boy and the boy still belongs to that afternoon.

I did not look up.

The ants had a problem and the problem was mine.

---

We lived in Yunusabad because my father worked at the factory whose name I would not learn until later `[CONFIRM-FATHER-WORKPLACE]`, and because Yunusabad was where engineers were given apartments in the second half of the seventies, and because by 1989 it was where the children of those engineers were children. The blocks were nine stories. Ours was nine stories. The blocks were placed in the careful Soviet rhythm that planners had learned by then — two facing two, with a courtyard between, and a chinar at each end of the courtyard, and an iron carpet-beater bolted into the concrete near the playground, and the playground itself a faded set of metal animals on metal springs, painted in colors that had once been red and blue and green and were now closer to the color of the asphalt itself.

This is the kind of sentence I could not have written then. I had to grow up first, leave, come back, and leave again before I could see the courtyard as a system. At four, the courtyard was simply the place where the world began.

What I could see at four: the ants. The dust on my shoes, which were soft summer shoes my mother had bought at the central department store before the shelves emptied `[CONFIRM-SHOES-DETAIL]`. The bottom edge of a man's trousers, brown, scuffed at the heel, walking past me toward the entrance of block fourteen. The smell of dust and of something my mother would later identify as oil from the trolleybus on Amir Temur — I could only smell it as the smell of the road past our courtyard, the smell that meant outside.

What I could hear: the radio in someone's window playing the Mayak station, which I knew was Mayak because at our house too the radio was on and at our house too it was Mayak, and Mayak was a particular voice from a particular box at a particular hour. A woman was selling kvass from the round yellow barrel by the entrance to the metro, two blocks away, but I knew the kvass barrel was there because my mother had bought a half-liter that morning in a glass jar and the jar was now on the kitchen counter with a piece of newspaper covering the top. A boy older than me — maybe seven, maybe eight `[CONFIRM-AGE]` — was kicking a tennis ball against the side of block fourteen, and the ball kept coming back to him at exactly the same angle, which seemed to me to be the most interesting thing a boy could possibly do.

This was the world. The ants were in the world. I was in the world. Somewhere far above me, on the fifth floor of block twelve, my mother was calling my name.

---

I did not have a sense, then, of being anything in particular. I knew I was a boy. I knew my mother was my mother. I knew my father was my father and that he was at the factory and that he came home in the evening and washed his hands for a long time at the kitchen sink. I knew the language we spoke at home, but I did not know it was a language. I did not know yet that there were two of them in the air around me — that the woman selling kvass was speaking the other one to her customers, and that the kids on the playground sometimes shouted in the other one when they did not want their mothers to understand. The two languages were just sound. The sound of home was one. The sound of outside was the other. I did not know that this would matter.

I did not know there was a third one, English, the one that would eventually pay me. That was very far away, in the future, in a building behind a wall on Chilanzar Street `[CONFIRM-EMBASSY-LOCATION: pre-2006 address]` that I would walk into one morning twenty-one years from this afternoon and have my photograph taken for a badge.

What I knew was the ants.

---

"*Sonok.*"

My mother had come down. She did not call from the balcony again. She walked the five flights down because at four years old I would not have heard her over my own attention, and because she had given up on calling, and because her bread was in the oven and she could not leave it long.

She was wearing the house dress with the small flowers `[CONFIRM-DRESS-PATTERN]` and the canvas slippers she always wore in summer for the courtyard, the ones with the back folded down, the way a woman wears slippers when she is going to be back inside in two minutes. Her hair was pinned up because of the oven heat. She was twenty-six years old `[CONFIRM-MOTHER-AGE]`. She would live longer than her own mother. She would still be in Tashkent when I was thirty.

I did not know any of this. I knew her hands. I let myself be picked up under the arms and lifted onto her hip, which was the official position for being too tired to walk but not too tired to be carried, even at four, even when I could have walked.

The crack in the asphalt was below me now. The ants were still working. I watched them over my mother's shoulder as she walked us to the entrance of block twelve. I watched them until the building swallowed them.

---

The stairwell of block twelve smelled the way stairwells of block twelve smelled in 1989: of concrete that had been cool all morning and was now warming, of someone's frying onions, of the metal of the mailbox row on the ground floor, of the cardboard the mailboxes were stuffed with — a newspaper here, a folded blue letter there. The elevator was working that day, which it did not always do. My mother did not take the elevator. She took the stairs because the stairs were faster when one knew the elevator's habits.

I rode on her hip up the five flights. I counted the landings the way I counted everything, which was to say, not in numbers, but in the small thresholds of attention each landing offered: a broken light bulb on the third, a doormat with the word *Welcome* in a language I could not read on the fourth, a smell of cabbage on the second that meant someone was making *shchi* — which my mother did not make, because my mother made other things, but which I knew was *shchi* because the smell had a name in our house even if the soup did not appear in our pots.

At the fifth floor she set me down. I switched my outdoor shoes for the indoor pair without being told `[CONFIRM-SHOES-RITUAL]`. The hallway of our apartment had a single bare bulb and three coat hooks and the smell of bread. My mother had timed the loaf to come out as we returned. This was the kind of small competence I would not understand as competence until I had lived elsewhere and seen women who could not time a loaf to a walk down five flights and back.

---

The kitchen was the smallest room in the apartment and the room in which everything happened. The window was open. The kvass jar was on the counter where she had left it. The bread was in the oven. The carrots she was browning for the rice she would cook later — she had begun cutting them before going to find me, and she resumed without saying anything `[CONFIRM-MEAL: plov, or rice with chicken, or something else specific]`.

I sat on the small stool by the window where I always sat. From the stool I could see one corner of the courtyard, the corner where the tennis-ball boy had been, but he was gone now, and the corner was just asphalt and the long shadow of the building.

"You were watching ants," my mother said.

I said I was.

"What were they doing?"

This is the kind of question I have asked children in the years since. It is the question of a mother who has decided that the way to make her son a thoughtful man is to take seriously what he watches, even when she has bread in the oven and carrots on the stove and a husband coming home with a face she will need to read.

I told her about the grain. I told her about the geometry of the crack. I told her, in the words of a four-year-old, that the ants had a problem.

"What kind of problem?" my mother said.

I said I did not know. I said the grain was too big.

My mother turned the carrots once. "Maybe they will figure it out," she said.

"How?"

"They have all afternoon."

She did not say this to be philosophical. She said it because she had carrots to brown and because the afternoon was long and because four-year-olds need to be given the gift of unfinished problems. I would learn later that my mother had been given the same gift by her own mother, and that her own mother had been given it in a place I would also one day go and look at, on the river `[CONFIRM-MATERNAL-LINEAGE: which side of family, where from]`.

---

My father came home before seven. I heard the key in the door. I heard him take off his shoes in the small hallway and put on his slippers. I heard him wash his hands at the kitchen sink for the long time he always washed them, because at his factory the hands were the first thing one did and the last `[CONFIRM-FATHER-FACTORY-RITUAL]`.

He came into the kitchen with his sleeves rolled and his face the face he wore when he had been thinking. He kissed the top of my head. He said hello to my mother. He sat down at the small table where there was already a glass of tea waiting for him in a *podstakannik*, the metal holder my mother had inherited from her father and treated like a relic and used every day.

He read the newspaper. *Pravda Vostoka* `[CONFIRM-NEWSPAPER]`. He read it for some minutes without saying anything. My mother turned off the gas under the carrots. The bread was already on the counter, cooling under a cloth.

"In Fergana again," my father said, not looking up.

"I heard on Mayak," my mother said.

I did not know what Fergana was. I did not know what again meant. I knew the tone of voice. I knew that when adults used this tone, the question I might ask would not be answered, and that the asking itself was the wrong shape, and so I did not ask.

I will tell you what I know now, which I did not know then. In June of that summer, in the Fergana Valley, two hundred kilometers from where we sat, men with sticks had gone into the homes of people whose families had been deported there from somewhere else forty years earlier. The men with sticks had wanted those families gone. Some of those families had gone — into trucks, into trains, into rooms in cities where they did not know anyone. Some had not gone, and what had happened to those was the thing that was *again*. My father had read the article and folded the paper. My mother had heard it on the radio and turned the radio down.

What I knew at the kitchen table on that evening in the summer of 1989 was that the carrots were ready, that the bread was on the cloth, and that my father did not want me to ask what *again* meant.

I did not ask.

---

We ate. We ate the way we always ate, which is the way I would remember eating my whole life: at the small table, with the window open, with my mother sitting last because she was always serving, with my father not speaking much because he had spoken all day at the factory, with the radio low in the other room playing something that was not music yet but would become music before the broadcast ended.

After dinner my mother washed the dishes. My father drank a second glass of tea and looked at the window. I went into the other room and lay on the rug and pushed my wooden bus from one end of the carpet pattern to the other and back. The wooden bus had once been blue. It was now mostly the color of wood again, because I had pushed it across the carpet so many times that the paint had worn off in stripes.

At eight forty-five my mother came in and turned on the television. *Spokoinoi nochi, malyshi* — *Good night, little ones* — would play at the same time it always played, and after that I would go to bed.

The puppet bear and the puppet hare came on the screen. Their voices were the voices that had been the voices of bedtime for as long as bedtime had existed. The little song played.

Then *Vremya* came on, and my father stopped looking at the window and started looking at the screen.

The voice of the news at nine o'clock had a particular timbre. I have not heard it in many years, and yet I could write it now as one writes a known phrase. The orange logo turned. The man read what had been written for him. There were images of places I did not know. There was the name of a man, an older man with a birthmark on his forehead, who I would later know was the only name in the country that mattered, but who at four I knew only as the man my father watched without smiling.

My mother sat down on the arm of the couch. My father did not move.

I lay on the rug with my bus. I watched the bus and not the television, because the bus was something I could move and the television was something the adults watched, and I had not yet learned to want what the adults wanted.

But I heard the names. I did not know I was hearing them. I would not remember them as names for many years. *Tashkent.* *Fergana.* *Moscow.* *Gorbachev.* *Karimov.* *Glasnost.* `[CONFIRM-NEWS-CONTENT]` The words were sounds the way the language outside the courtyard was sound. They came in the door of the apartment and they sat on the rug with me, and I gave them no more attention than I had given the ants in the asphalt.

This was the last summer in which I would not give them attention.

---

When the news was over my mother lifted me from the rug and carried me to the small bed in the corner of the room I shared with no one. `[CONFIRM-SLEEPING-ARRANGEMENT]` She helped me into the soft cotton pajamas she had ironed the day before. She sat on the edge of the bed. She brushed my hair back from my forehead the way she always did. She sang the song she had sung me to sleep with since I had been small enough to fit in one of her arms, the song that begins *bayu-bayushki-bayu*, the song that does not really tell a small child to sleep so much as it tells him that he is small and that someone is awake on his behalf.

I asked her — I remember this — what *again* meant.

She did not look surprised that I had heard. She did not pretend I had not heard. She did not explain Fergana. She did not tell me about the men with sticks or the trucks or the rooms in cities. What she said was this:

"Sometimes things happen, and then they happen another time."

I asked her if they would happen here.

She thought about it for what I now know was a very long time for a mother sitting on the edge of a bed at nine-thirty at night with bread to be wrapped and a husband to be talked to in low voices when the boy was finally asleep.

"Here we are fine," she said. "You sleep."

I slept.

---

Outside, the chinar tree in the courtyard between blocks twelve and fourteen was the same chinar tree it had been at four o'clock in the afternoon. The crack in the asphalt was the same crack. The ants — I do not know what happened to the ants. They have had thirty-six years, and they were ants, and they did not survive the thirty-six years. Someone else's children have watched their children's children carry someone else's bread through the same crack, or through a different crack on the same asphalt, or through asphalt that has been repaired and broken and repaired again. The courtyard is still there. The blocks are still there. I have been told the chinar was cut down in 2007 `[CONFIRM-CHINAR-FATE]`, by which time I was already at the building behind the wall on Chilanzar Street, which is to say I was already someone else.

But that night I was four. I was asleep. My mother was wrapping the bread. My father was reading the paper again, with the lamp lit low, doing in private the thinking he could not do in front of the boy.

The summer of 1989 had two months left in it. The wall in Berlin had three months left. The country we lived in had two years and seven days. None of us in the apartment on the fifth floor of block twelve in Yunusabad knew this. We were not supposed to know.

This is what *before* means. It is the word for the time when you do not know.

### Chapter notes for the author

- **The name.** I left the protagonist unnamed on the page. Mother calls him *sonok* and refers to "the boy." When you choose the name, the simplest insertion is on the second page, mother's voice on the balcony: replace "calling my name — which I will not write down here" with the name and a line about the name. Or leave it withheld — there is a literary tradition for it (see Bezmozgis's *Free World*, where the boy's name is delayed).
- **The sister.** Per your confirmation, mother and sister stayed in Tashkent. I did not write the sister into Chapter 1 — she can arrive in Chapter 2 (1991 collapse) as a newborn, or in Chapter 3 (mid-90s school years) as a small presence, depending on her actual age relative to you. **Tell me the year of her birth and I'll place her exactly.**
- **The father.** I rendered him present in 1989 — engineer, quiet, factory worker. If your father was not in the household in 1989, or had a different relationship to it, this is a structural revision and the kitchen scene re-shapes around mother alone. **Was your father present in your early childhood?**
- **The Fergana detail.** I used the June 1989 pogroms against Meskhetian Turks as the "thing on the news" because it is real, period-correct, foreshadows the 1999 hinge, and gives the adult narrator an honest place to do his work without lecturing. If your family had no such conversation, or a different one (the 1989 language law? the August 1989 Tajik protests? Gorbachev addressing the Congress?), I can swap.
- **The chinar tree in 2007.** I invented its cutting-down for the closing pivot. If the actual chinar still stands, or was cut at a different time, that line is the easiest fix.
- **What the chapter doesn't do.** It doesn't explain Yunusabad to a Western reader. It doesn't translate *podstakannik* or *bayu-bayushki-bayu*. It doesn't name your ethnicity. It doesn't render your mother's interiority. It doesn't sentimentalize. It does carry the adult narrator's voice as ghost. Tell me which of these is right and which is wrong for your truth.
