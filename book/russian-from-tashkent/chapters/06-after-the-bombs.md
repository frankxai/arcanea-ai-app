# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 6: After the Bombs

*Yunusabad, Tashkent — 1999–2000* `[CONFIRM-PERIOD: this chapter covers approximately March 1999 through late 2000, protagonist ages 14–15]`

---

Spring came the way it always came in Tashkent — too fast, the apricots going in two weeks from bare wood to full leaf, the air turning from gray to something that smelled of dust and new grass and the specific sweetness of the irrigation water that ran in the concrete channels along the broader streets. By March the checkpoints near our building had gone from temporary to permanent in appearance if not in designation: the sawhorse was gone but the policeman was still there, posted at the corner like a traffic sign that had been installed and then no one had gotten around to removing.

I walked past him every day. He looked at me every day. By the end of March we had a working arrangement: I nodded, he let me pass, neither of us acknowledged that this was an arrangement.

This is what the city had become by spring.

---

The departures had started before February 16. This is important to understand: the departure of Russian-speaking families from Tashkent had been going on since 1991, a slow continuous bleed that had not stopped and showed no sign of stopping. The bombings accelerated it, or more precisely: the bombings and the crackdown that followed told the people who had been considering leaving that the calculus had changed.

Within the block I lived in, I can count six families who left between March and October of 1999. `[CONFIRM-DEPARTURE-COUNT: author should verify against actual memory of who left in their specific building in 1999]` I do not mean families I heard about from my parents. I mean families I had known since childhood — the father of one had been in the same factory cohort as my father, the mother of another had taught me piano for two years before the piano lessons stopped for economic reasons — whose apartments went from occupied to empty over the course of a season.

The pattern of a departure: first the word spread through the floor or the building, usually from the mothers, usually in the form of a fact already decided rather than a thing being considered. Then a period of visible activity — boxes appearing on landings, the particular sound of furniture being moved across parquet floors. Then a day with a truck in the courtyard, or a day without a truck but with an apartment that smelled different when you passed the door. Then silence from behind a door that had previously had sounds behind it.

Then a new family, or no new family.

What the remaining families said about the departures: not much, in public. In private — between my parents, at the kitchen table, in the lowered voices that had been the register of political conversation since I was four — there was a specific exhaustion in how they talked about it. Not grief, not judgment. Exhaustion. Like people watching a wave move through a room and knowing it will reach them eventually and not yet having decided what to do when it does.

---

My mother's sister — the one in Chilanzar — called one evening in May with the fact, delivered in the careful language of their telephone calls, that her husband had been offered a position in Novosibirsk. `[CONFIRM: whether a specific relative actually left or seriously considered leaving in 1999–2000, and where they went or considered going]`

I was at the table doing homework. My mother was at the counter. The conversation lasted forty minutes, and I heard my mother's side of it.

What I heard: a series of responses that were not yes and not no but something in between — a kind of patient acknowledgment that kept the other person talking while committing to nothing. My mother was good at this. She had been good at it for as long as I had been old enough to notice.

When the call ended she stood for a moment with her hand still on the receiver.

Then she made tea.

My father came home an hour later and she told him. I was in the other room, doing the homework or not doing the homework. I heard the kitchen conversation through the wall the way I had heard kitchen conversations through walls my whole life: not clearly, but enough. The cadences of a serious discussion. My mother's voice as the one carrying information; my father's voice as the one receiving it and then, slowly, responding.

What I heard: the word *Novosibirsk* three times. The word *Sasha* — my mother's sister's husband's name. The word *dengi*, money, twice. The word *tam* — there — several times, which was the word my parents used for Russia when they did not want to say Russia.

And once, clearly, my father's voice: "We are not going."

And my mother, after a pause: "I know."

And then no more words about it that night, or the next night, or the nights I was present for after that.

---

I did not ask my parents about this decision for many years. Not because I was afraid of the answer, but because the decision had already been made and it was not mine to interrogate. This is not a complaint. I am describing the structure of that household: there were decisions my parents made together that were presented to me as facts, and the correct response to those facts was to live within them. I had been born within a fact I had not chosen — the city, the language, the particular historical moment — and I had been living within it for fourteen years, and one more fact was not going to change the structure of living.

What I felt, if I am honest: not relief, not disappointment. A kind of clarification. The answer to a question I had been asking at the periphery of my consciousness since the first departure on our floor was: we are not going. I knew what not going meant. I had been watching what not going looked like for eight years, since 1991, since the first families left and my parents had watched from the balcony and stayed.

Not going meant: this apartment, this city, this arrangement with the policeman at the corner. Not going meant: continuing to be the kind of people who stay.

I was fourteen. I did not know yet that continuing to be the kind of people who stay is itself a form of heroism and a form of defeat simultaneously, and that there is no way to tell from the inside which it is, and no particular advantage to knowing.

---

That summer the city was quieter than it had been. `[CONFIRM-SUMMER-1999: author's sense of the city's atmosphere June–August 1999]` Not peaceful-quiet — the quiet of a room from which something has been removed and not yet replaced. The familiar bakeries and kiosks and the woman selling corn on the cob by the school were all still there. The trolleybus still ran. The Metro still ran. Karimov still appeared on television with the face of a man for whom the bombings had been not a crisis but a clarification: something he had known was coming, something he had planned for, something that had arrived on schedule.

He gave speeches that summer which I did not watch but which my father watched in the other room, the television volume low, the face of a man assessing something. The speeches said: the enemy was defined, the enemy was being dealt with, Uzbekistan was safe and sovereign and moving forward. The speeches had the quality of all speeches in Uzbekistan that I had ever heard: the quality of having been written for a listener who already agreed, and delivered to an audience whose agreement was not optional.

At school in September — ninth grade, a different classroom, the same building — the component on Uzbek sovereignty was extended. It was no longer an addition to civics; it was its own slot on the schedule, two periods per week. The teacher assigned to it was not our usual class teacher but a man from the administration who had the look of someone who had been given a job in which the job was to transmit a specific set of sentences, and who was doing it.

We took notes. We would be tested.

---

One specific afternoon: October 1999. I was walking home from school and the route I usually took had a police check — not a checkpoint, exactly, but two policemen standing near the entrance to the metro who were checking documents, doing it casually, the way authority does things when it wants to be seen doing them without looking urgent. I had my school ID and my propiska card. `[CONFIRM: whether protagonists at this age carried registration documents; propiska/internal passport rules in Uzbekistan in 1999 for 14-year-olds]`

One of the policemen looked at my documents and handed them back.

The man behind me — perhaps thirty, wearing a coat, carrying a briefcase — had his documents looked at for longer. The policeman asked him something. The man answered. The policeman asked something else. This exchange lasted perhaps two minutes. I was past them by then, but I looked back once.

The man was still there, still answering questions, the briefcase at his feet.

I did not see what happened next. I continued home.

What I understood: I had been waved through. The man behind me had been detained for further questions. The difference between us was not — I was certain of this, with the certainty available to a person who has been watching the city's social physics for nine months — the difference between documents in order and documents not in order. The difference was something the policeman had registered in the three seconds he had looked at my face, and something else he had registered in the three seconds he had looked at the other man's face.

I was Russian-speaker, passed as secular, ethnicity legible as Slavic. `[CONFIRM-MIX-VISIBILITY: how the protagonist presented physically, whether his appearance was read as Russian/Slavic, whether he was ever stopped or questioned more closely]`

The man with the briefcase was something else.

I walked home. I put my bag down. I washed my hands the way my father washed his hands, which I had never done before — his ritual, not mine, but it was what I did that afternoon.

---

My mother had a friend, a woman named Galina `[CONFIRM: placeholder name — author should replace with any real composite from this period]`, who had been her friend since before I was born, who lived in Chilanzar and who was, by the autumn of 1999, working on her documents for Germany. She was half German — Volga German on her mother's side — and the German government had in the 1990s opened pathways for the *Russlanddeutsche*, the Russian Germans scattered across the Soviet Union by various deportations, to claim German citizenship.

Galina was not planning to leave. Then she was. The transition between these two states, my mother told me once, many years later, happened in three days. Not the departure itself — that took another year of paperwork — but the decision. Three days after February 16.

"She said she woke up on the third day and knew," my mother said.

I was an adult when she told me this. I asked my mother if she had felt anything like that.

She thought about it. This was her habit: the long pause before an honest answer, the refusal to give you the easy version.

"No," she said. "I woke up the third day and knew the opposite."

I did not ask her what she meant. I understood she meant that the bombings had, for her, not opened a door but clarified which door was hers. The door of staying. The door of: this is where we are and we will make it work. I had always known this about her, but I had not known she had chosen it consciously, in the specific days after February 16, in the way that constitutes real choosing.

---

By January 2000 Galina's family had an address in Hanover. `[CONFIRM-GALINA-DESTINATION: author can adjust to any German or other city where families they knew emigrated]` They left in a February — I cannot tell you which February, whether it was 1999 or 2000, I have lost the sequence — but I remember the going because it was the last time I saw Galina's son, whose name I will not write here because he was a friend in the way that neighborhood friends are friends, which is to say completely, and the ending of such friendships is not something I have ever processed to completion. `[CONFIRM: whether the author had a close friend from a family that emigrated to Germany in this period]`

He gave me a cassette tape before he left. I do not remember what was on it. I remember holding it and knowing it was the kind of object that was going to mean something later, when I had enough distance to understand what was being given, and not yet meaning anything except: he is leaving and this is what he had to give.

I played the tape many times after. Eventually the tape wore out.

---

The year 2000 arrived without ceremony of any particular kind that I remember. The millennium preparations in Russia and in the West had been on television — the New Year fireworks over various capitals, the Y2K alarm that turned into nothing, which the adults around me found darkly funny in a specifically Soviet way: a catastrophe that foreigners had spent billions preparing for and which did not happen, while the catastrophes that actually arrived in this part of the world had come without warning and without preparation money. My father made this joke once and then put it down, because there was no need to make it twice.

By January 2000 the crackdown that had followed the bombings had settled from acute to chronic. The checkpoints were part of the street furniture. The arrests continued but at a rate that no longer produced daily news — they were now simply part of the city's operations, the way the metro was part of the city's operations. People who disappeared disappeared in a quieter rhythm.

At school: the civics curriculum was fully adjusted. Russian literature hours had been reduced again — not dramatically, but another slice, another concession to the Uzbek language instruction that was now not optional but mandatory through all subjects. I noticed this less as a political fact and more as a daily calibration: more time in one language, less in another, the linguistic ground shifting in the way that ground shifts when tectonic things are happening somewhere below.

My Russian was native. My Uzbek was functional — street Uzbek, market Uzbek, the Uzbek of asking for something and being understood. What I did not have was school Uzbek, literary Uzbek, the Uzbek of a person for whom the language was not a second geography but the first. I was aware of this gap. I had been aware of it for years. The gap was not getting smaller.

---

There was a conversation. Spring 2000.

It happened at the kitchen table, which is where all conversations of significance in that apartment happened. My father, my mother, me. My sister was somewhere else in the apartment — she would have been eight or nine by then, and she had learned, with the instinct children develop in households where adult conversations sometimes have weight, when to be elsewhere. `[CONFIRM-SISTER-AGE-2000]`

My father had been working on documents. `[CONFIRM: whether father had begun any process of documentation for potential relocation — propiska update, Russian citizenship inquiry, anything of this kind in 1999–2000]` Not documents for leaving — or at least, that is not how I understood it. Documents related to something at work, I thought. Paperwork that required attention.

But my mother had that evening a piece of paper from someone — I did not see it clearly, it was passed across the table quickly — and after my sister had been put to bed there was a conversation that I was present for, sitting in the corner with the specific stillness of a teenager who has learned that stillness is the condition of being allowed to remain.

The conversation was about my uncle in Novosibirsk. `[CONFIRM: whether there was an actual relative in Russia who offered or suggested that the family could come]` He had written. He had said — and here is where I can only give you the sentence as I heard it, which is to say incompletely, through a door that was not quite open — that there was work, that the city was manageable, that if they were going to go they should go before it became harder to go.

My father heard this and said nothing for a while.

My mother said: "He means the registration."

My father said he knew what he meant.

"Volodya," my mother said — this was her tone for: I need you to think about this seriously, not to dismiss it — "the children need to know what the plan is."

I sat very still.

My father looked at the paper. He looked at the window. He looked at the table.

"There is no plan," he said. "There is here. There has always been here."

My mother said: "That's not an answer."

My father said: "That's the only answer I have."

They sat with this for a moment. I sat with it from the corner.

Then my mother said something very quietly that I did not hear clearly, and my father said something back, and after a while my mother made more tea, and the conversation ended not with a resolution but with a exhaustion — not the exhaustion of defeat but the exhaustion of people who have been carrying something heavy for a long time and have decided, for tonight, to set it down.

Nothing was decided at that table. Or perhaps everything was decided, in the way that not deciding is itself a decision: we stay, and we do not discuss going, and we hold what we have and see.

I understood, sitting in my corner with my stillness, that I was watching my parents make the choice that would shape the next ten years of my life. I understood that they were not making it easily. I understood that "there is no plan" and "there has always been here" were two sentences that contained a whole philosophy of staying, a commitment to this apartment and this city and this specific version of their lives that was not passivity but something harder than passivity — a refusal to admit that the ground had shifted enough to require a different calculation.

I did not know if they were right. I know now that they were right in the way that any consistent choice is right: it produced a life, it produced me, it produced the person who would eventually walk through a gate on Moyqorghon Street with a badge around his neck and speak English to a woman from Wisconsin and do the work that needed doing.

Whether it was the best choice — whether there was a better choice available to them at that table on that spring evening — is not a question I have the authority to answer. I was fifteen. I was in the corner. I was watching.

The summer of 2000 was the summer I started seriously learning English. `[CONFIRM-ENGLISH-TIMELINE: when protagonist began studying English seriously — at a private school, through an institute, self-taught, or through contact with the hotel world]`

This is not as disconnected from the kitchen table conversation as it might seem. When my parents said there is no plan, here has always been here, what they were also saying — what the logic of staying in Tashkent in 2000 required — was that the person who stayed needed to become more useful to a world that was changing around him. Russian-language skills were increasingly beside the point in formal Uzbekistan. Uzbek was the state language and I was not building the Uzbek I would need. The parallel track, the track that led not into the Uzbek state machinery but into the space adjacent to it — the hotels, the international organizations, eventually the embassy — required English.

I had studied English in school since fifth grade, the way everyone studied English: the textbooks that were at least a decade out of date, the teacher who knew the grammar but spoke with a Tashkent accent that was not going to help anyone talk to an actual English speaker, the vocabulary lists that contained words like *magneto* and *telegraph* and almost none of the words you would need at a hotel front desk.

That summer I found a private English teacher. `[CONFIRM: details of English instruction — private tutor, British Council, American Corner, private school]` She was a woman in her sixties who had spent twenty years teaching English at the pedagogical institute and who had, after retirement, converted her front room into a classroom that held four or five students at a time. She charged a rate that was manageable — this was the parallel economy of tutoring that had been growing since the state schools stopped paying their teachers enough to live on.

Her English was not American English. It was the English of a Soviet-trained teacher who had learned from British textbooks in the 1970s — precise, a little formal, with a vocabulary that was technically correct and entirely usable. What she had was the structure. What I had to acquire was everything else: the idiom, the rhythm, the specific sounds that my Russian-trained mouth resisted.

I went twice a week through the summer. By September I could construct a grammatically correct sentence in English. By October I could construct one that didn't sound like a textbook.

This is not the remarkable thing about that summer.

The remarkable thing is that I can locate the summer of 2000 — the summer after the kitchen table conversation, the summer after the checkpoint wave-through, the summer after Galina's son left with his cassette tape — as the summer I understood, for the first time, that the instrument of my survival was going to be language. Not loyalty to a state. Not skill at a factory. Not even the particular social intelligence of knowing which register you were in. Language. Multiple ones. The more of them I had, the more possible futures there were.

This was a fifteen-year-old's logic. It was also correct.

---

One afternoon that autumn I was in the courtyard after school. The courtyard was less full than it had been when I was a child — the families who had left had mostly been replaced by Uzbek families, some of whom had children, but the children were younger than me and the social ecology of the courtyard had shifted, the particular mix of languages and games and domino tables reorganized into something that was Uzbek in its orientation in a way the courtyard of my childhood had not been.

I was sitting on the wall near the chinar tree `[CONFIRM-CHINAR: Ch1 establishes the chinar tree; whether it was still there in 2000]` reading. There was an old man I did not recognize — a new resident, I think, from one of the recently filled apartments — sitting on the bench my father's friend had used to sit on for years. He was drinking tea from a thermos. He looked at me once, unhurried, the look of an old man assessing whether a young man is worth speaking to.

"You're studying," he said. In Russian.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded. He poured more tea. He looked at the tree.

"What subject," he said.

"English," I said.

He was quiet for a while. Then he said: "Good. That one will be useful."

He did not say useful for what. He did not say useful where. He said it in the tone of a man for whom the statement was self-evident, the way you might say *carry an umbrella* in a city with an unreliable sky.

I thought about it for a long time after. I still think about it. I do not know who he was. I do not know how he knew what he knew, or whether he knew anything specific or only something general about how the world was organized. I only know that he said it, and I went upstairs and did my homework, and the homework was English.

---

*By the end of 2000, the Russian-speaking population of Tashkent was a fraction of what it had been in 1989. The census would later show the numbers: the demographic that had been eight percent of the republic, then five percent, now heading toward two. In the apartment building where I had grown up, I could count on one hand the Russian-speaking families who remained.*

*We were one of them.*

*I do not know what this means. I have known it as a fact my whole adult life — that we stayed, that most others left, that my parents made a choice in the years 1991 through 2000 that resulted in us remaining in a place that had remade itself around our absence-in-progress. There are people who stayed and became more Uzbekistani as the state required, learned better Uzbek, adjusted, found the new equilibrium. There are people who stayed and withdrew — smaller lives, more private, Russian language maintained in the apartment as the last territory of self-definition. My parents were neither of these entirely.*

*What they were: people who believed, with a quiet stubbornness I did not fully understand until I was much older, that a life built in a place had a claim on that place, regardless of what the place decided to call itself. They had built their life here. The Soviet Union had ended. Independence had come. The bombings had come. The crackdown had come. Their friends had left. Their apartment was still their apartment. Their children were still their children.*

*They stayed.*

*And I, at fifteen, with the English teacher twice a week and the cassette tape that had worn out and the checkpoint policeman who knew my face, was starting to understand that I was not going to stay in quite the same way.*
