# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 3: The Long Adjustment

*Chirchiq / Yunusabad, Tashkent — 1993–1995* `[CONFIRM-SCHOOL: protagonist ages 8–10, School No. [X] — author to confirm school number or district]`

---

The money came undone in the spring of 1993, which is to say, it came undone more completely than it had been coming undone, because by then it had already been coming undone for a year and a half. My mother had explained this to me once in the kitchen, briefly, without drama, in the same voice she used to explain why we were having porridge again when we had had porridge the day before: because the box of flour had to last until Friday, because Friday was when things could be different.

What she explained about the money was this: it costs more now. More for bread, more for the oil, more for the soap. Not because the things themselves have changed but because the paper has changed. The paper is the same paper but it is less paper. You understand?

I said I understood. I understood in the way you understand when you are eight: which is to say, I understood that this was important and that I should stop asking for things.

I had a sister by now. She had arrived the previous year `[CONFIRM-SISTER-BIRTH-YEAR: placeholder 1992 or early 1993 — author to confirm; I'm placing her as a baby/toddler in 1993-95 for these chapters]`, small and loud in the particular way of small things that do not yet have anything to be quiet about. She slept in the corner of my parents' room in a crib my father had borrowed from a neighbor — the first of several neighbors to give us something they would not be needing anymore, since they were also leaving. My sister had not yet been explained anything and so had nothing to worry about. I considered this her main advantage over me.

---

In the second year of primary school — I was eight, the year was 1993 `[CONFIRM-SCHOOL-YEAR]` — we had two tracks in our class. I understood this only slowly, the way you understand things that are presented as simply the way things are: there was Russian-language instruction, which was most of our day, and there was the Uzbek language period, which was three times a week with a teacher who came specifically for it.

The Uzbek teacher's name was Farida Yusupovna `[CONFIRM-TEACHER: composite; keep name Uzbek, patronymic confirms professional register]`. She was younger than Irina Mikhailovna. She had a quality of determined brightness that I associated with people who have agreed to be somewhere. She spoke Russian to us when she needed us to understand something, but she did not let us answer in Russian. When we answered in Russian she waited, looking at us with that brightness, until we tried again in Uzbek.

This was not comfortable. Some children in my class did not try again. They said their sentence in Russian, slowly, as though repetition would work, and Farida Yusupovna waited them out. These standoffs lasted at most a minute. After a minute she would give you the Uzbek word you needed, which felt like mercy and felt like losing simultaneously.

I was not one of the children who refused to try. I tried. But trying and succeeding were not the same thing. The Uzbek sentences I produced were, I know now, grammatically approximate — the right vocabulary assembled in a Russian syntax, like furniture moved into the wrong apartment. Farida Yusupovna accepted this, for the time being. I noticed that she accepted it slightly less each week.

---

My friend in school at this time was a boy named Kolya `[CONFIRM-KOLYA: composite, Russian-speaking, same building or nearby; full name Nikolai, called Kolya; his family leaves eventually]` — properly Nikolai, but everyone called him Kolya, the way everyone was always called something shorter than their name. We had been at school together since the first year. His father worked at one of the smaller plants outside Chirchiq, in something to do with chemicals, and his mother had been a nurse and now was something else, something in one of the new small pharmacies that had opened when the state pharmacy became empty.

Kolya and I ate lunch together and walked to and from school together most days and spent a portion of every Saturday in the courtyard bouncing balls or collecting things or conducting the investigations that pre-adolescent boys conduct in the margins of their neighborhoods. We had a shared project that year: a list of every make of car that we saw on our street, compiled in a notebook Kolya had acquired somewhere. We had been keeping this list for months. It had become genuinely complex — the Soviet cars and the new Turkish ones and the occasional foreign car we couldn't identify and had to describe instead.

Kolya had a facility with Uzbek that I did not have. Not studied facility — he had the facility of someone who had grown up in closer proximity to it, because his building was older and the courtyard had always had more Uzbek-speaking families. His Uzbek was not good, exactly, but it was less flagrantly Russian than mine. This was the first time I had encountered the word *proximity* as a concept in the body, rather than a word in a dictionary: some people stood closer to the language and some stood farther, and the distance was not chosen.

---

In the autumn of 1993 there was a boy in my class who I will call Sanjar `[CONFIRM-SANJAR: composite Uzbek classmate — name is fine, keep general]`. Sanjar was Uzbek-speaking at home, though his Russian was functional — the Russian of a child who had learned it from school and television rather than from parents. He and I were not friends, exactly, but we were the kind of classmates who worked together on things without friction, which at eight is a sufficient basis for coexistence.

In October, during a Russian literature class, Irina Mikhailovna asked us to read aloud from a Pushkin poem. She went around the class and each student read a stanza. When she reached Sanjar, he read slowly — more slowly than the poem called for, feeling for the words — and Irina Mikhailovna waited. She did not hurry him. When he finished, she said, in her usual even voice, that he should practice this one at home.

After class Sanjar was quiet. Not upset, exactly — he was a contained boy, Sanjar, not given to visible reaction. Just quiet.

I did not say anything to him. I did not know what the correct thing to say was. I filed the moment the way you file things at eight: as something that had happened, whose meaning I was still working out.

Later I understood what I had been watching: a boy being asked to perform in a language that was not his first language, in a class in which I was the norm and he was the exception, and the teacher who did not see the irony of this because she had not yet been given a reason to. Farida Yusupovna, in our Uzbek class, was doing the same thing to me three times a week. Neither of us had asked to be in the position we were in. Both of us were in it.

---

The language question arrived more directly in the following year.

I was nine. The school had acquired, from somewhere, a policy — I would not have called it a policy at nine, but I now know it was a policy — that Uzbek was to be used in common areas. The hallways. The canteen. The courtyard between school buildings. Not in Russian-language classrooms — the instruction language stayed — but outside them.

This was not enforced with particular severity. No one was punished for speaking Russian in the hallway. But the teachers would respond to Russian-language questions in the hallway in Uzbek, and then in Russian, and there was a sense that the Uzbek response was the right one and the Russian one was a concession.

I tested this once, not deliberately. I dropped my textbook in the hallway near the office and it made a noise and a teacher I did not know well — a man, older, who taught something in the older classes `[CONFIRM-TEACHER-NAME: keep generic, the point is the institutional posture]` — glanced at me and said something in Uzbek. I said, reflexively, in Russian: "I dropped my book, sorry." He looked at me for a moment with an expression I could not read. Then he said, in Russian: "Pick it up." And walked on.

It was not hostile. It was, if anything, tired. The expression of a man who has adapted and is waiting for the people around him to adapt also.

I picked up the book. I thought about it for the rest of the day.

---

What the money was doing, that same year, was this: it was becoming a different kind of problem. The coupon-som had arrived the previous November — `[CONFIRM: November 1993, correct per research bible]` — and it looked like money but it behaved like a newspaper in the rain. My mother began a new habit: on the first day my father brought home his wage, she went to the market and bought flour. Then cooking oil. Then whatever else was on a list she kept folded in her coat pocket. She did this before she did anything else with the money, before she paid the utility bill, before she set anything aside. She did not explain this to me. I understood it by watching: the flour went into the canister, the canister was full, the fullness of the canister was a calculation that protected us from the paper dissolving.

My father's wage was doing something strange. It was the same number — the same numbers were written on the slip he brought home — but the number bought less each month. He brought the wage slip to the kitchen table and put it next to the price of flour from the market and did arithmetic on a notepad in the compact handwriting of a man who had spent years annotating technical drawings. `[CONFIRM-FATHER-NOTES: he is an engineer; handwriting detail consistent with character bible]` He did not show me these calculations. I saw them once when I was reaching past him on the table for a pencil.

What I saw: columns of figures, a series of subtractions, a number at the bottom that was underlined twice.

I did not ask what it meant. I already knew what it meant. It meant less.

---

The families leaving continued to leave. By 1994 the departure was enough of a pattern that it had a social grammar. You knew when a family was leaving because they would give things away: furniture, books, kitchen items, the extra blankets from the storage space. The giving-away happened a few weeks before, and the things you received were weighted with a specific significance — not the casual significance of a gift but the significance of something being redistributed before a closing. My mother received a set of plates from one family, a curtain rod from another, a bag of lentils from a third. She received them with gratitude and without comment. We used the plates. We put up the curtain rod. The lentils became soup.

I understood by now what was happening, in the way a nine-year-old understands something: not as policy, not as history, but as the specific faces of specific people going away. The Sorokina apartment had been empty for two years and now had a different family. `[CONFIRM: Galina Ivanovna departed Ch2, apt now occupied]` The family upstairs from Kolya had left the previous spring. Kolya himself had started saying, with a particular careful neutrality, that his parents were "looking at options." He said *options* the way a child says a word he has heard in a context he hasn't fully parsed.

In October of 1994 Kolya came to school and told me his family was going to Samara. He said it the way you state a fact. He said his father had a cousin there who had a construction company.

I said, how long will you be gone?

He looked at me. I saw that he understood what I was asking and that I had asked it wrong. Not *how long* — that was the wrong question. But I did not know the right question yet and I think he did not either.

"I don't know," he said. He was nine. He was not going to cry about it and neither was I, because we were nine and what nine-year-olds do is keep a list of cars and eat lunch together and ask stupid questions in the face of difficult facts.

We kept the car list. We gave it to each other on his last day, torn in half — half the pages for me, half for him — as though dividing it meant something, as though it meant the data would be preserved.

---

After Kolya left I had other friends, because children have other friends, but none of them were Kolya. The one who came closest, in the particular way of the proximity you form at ten when a vacancy opens, was a boy I will call Aziz `[CONFIRM-AZIZ: Uzbek-speaking classmate, new presence after Kolya leaves; composite but important for the school scenes]`. Aziz was in my class and lived four buildings over in a direction I didn't usually go. His father worked at something I didn't quite understand — something in the new administrative infrastructure of the city, something to do with permits `[CONFIRM-AZIZ-FATHER: keep vague, this is the bureaucratic class that does well post-independence]`. His family had not been giving things away.

Aziz and I played together after school and this was unremarkable except for one thing: we played in Russian. He spoke Russian well, better than functional, better than learned — he had older siblings who had been in Russian-medium schools before the language shifts, and the Russian had come down to him through them. But when adults were present — his mother, occasionally, or the older women in the courtyard near his building — he switched to Uzbek, and the switch was seamless, the way a person switches who is comfortable in both rooms. I could not switch back. I could enter the Uzbek with difficulty, the way you enter a cold room — reluctantly, with awareness of the transition. He slid in and out of it like a door that was not locked.

I did not say anything about this. Neither did he. But I noticed it.

---

The school language examination happened in the spring of my fifth year — I was ten `[CONFIRM-AGE: spring 1995]` — and it was the moment that later seemed, from the outside, like the moment something shifted, though from the inside it was less clean than that.

It was an Uzbek language examination. Written, with a time limit. We had to write a short text — ten sentences — on a given topic. The topic that spring was "My family." *Mening oilam.* Ten sentences about my family in Uzbek.

I wrote ten sentences. I know they were not very good. I used vocabulary I knew and arranged it in the way that felt most right to me, which was the way that was most Russian. I wrote, more or less: my family has four people. My father is an engineer. My mother works at home. I have a sister. We live in an apartment. My father reads newspapers. My mother cooks. We have a television. I like school. My family is good.

This is the text of a child who is describing rather than writing. But it was Uzbek — technically Uzbek, grammatically approximate — and when Farida Yusupovna marked it she gave me a 4 out of 5, which was the grade for someone who had tried and not fully arrived. She gave Sanjar, that same day, a 5. She gave Aziz a 5. She gave a girl named Malika a 5. `[CONFIRM-MALIKA: composite Uzbek girl, fine to keep]` She gave Kolya's empty seat nothing.

I took my 4 and put it in my bag. Four was not failing. Four was the grade of someone who was adjacent to the expectation but not inside it.

I thought about this on the walk home. I passed the building where Kolya had lived. There was a new family there now — I did not know them, had never spoken to them. Their bicycle was in the courtyard in a slightly different spot than Kolya's family's bicycle had been.

I thought about Kolya in Samara. I tried to picture Samara and could not. I pictured snow, which was probably wrong for spring, but it was all I had.

I thought about Aziz sliding through the door without effort and me standing in front of it with a 4.

The thought I did not have, which I would only have later — would only have from the vantage point of the person I became — was this: the examination was designed for Aziz. Not maliciously. No one decided, sitting in a school administration office, *this examination should disadvantage the Russian-speakers*. But the examination came from a country that was now an Uzbek country, and an Uzbek country's school would have an Uzbek examination, and the Uzbek examination would find Aziz in the room it was built for and find me in front of the door I could enter imperfectly.

That was not a thought I had at ten. At ten I had a 4.

---

There was a Saturday in the autumn of 1994 when my father took me to the Chorsu bazaar. `[CONFIRM-CHORSU: protagonist has been to Chorsu before but now old enough to notice differently]` He took me occasionally, and I had been often enough to have a sense of the place — the smell of it first, which reached you before you entered, the composite smell of meat and melon and spice and diesel from the Gazelles parked at the periphery. But this Saturday was different because I was old enough, apparently, to be given a specific task: I was to select the carrots.

My father had the money — a thick fold of the new coupon-soms, which I had recently learned to read as denominations rather than just paper colors. He had the list in his head, not written, which was something I noticed about him: he never wrote grocery lists. The list lived in him as clearly as the technical drawings he made at work.

We moved through the bazaar. He pointed at things and named prices and I watched him negotiate, which was a thing he did without apparent pleasure — not a market personality, not someone who enjoyed the back-and-forth — but efficiently, in the way of a man who had learned that efficiency was a form of dignity in a place that did not always offer dignity to people with his face and his name.

At the carrot section I stood for a long time. The carrots came in two types: orange ones and yellow ones. I knew from our kitchen that plov was made with yellow carrots, but I had only seen yellow carrots at the bazaar and not in the store, and I was not sure if the distinction was universal or local. I asked my father which kind we wanted.

He said yellow.

I asked why yellow.

He said: "Because that's how plov is supposed to be here."

I filed this. *Here* was doing something small but significant in that sentence. Here, as opposed to somewhere else. Here, where plov had yellow carrots and the examinations were in Uzbek and the flags were new.

---

My mother's notebook had grown by 1995. It had companions now — a second notebook, smaller, with a red cover, in which I believe she kept track of tutoring income. `[CONFIRM-MOTHER-TUTORING: per character bible, mother possibly ran tutoring sessions; placeholder]` She had begun, at some point I could not precisely date, teaching mathematics and Russian to a small number of children in our apartment, after school on certain afternoons. I knew about this because on those afternoons I was not supposed to be in the main room and I was occasionally required to take my sister out to the courtyard for a specific number of minutes. The children came and went without introduction.

The income from this — whatever it was — went into the red notebook in an ink that was different from her blue-ink letters. Black ink for the numbers. Blue for letters. I did not ask about this either, but I thought about it: the careful categorization of things in a world where the categories kept shifting.

My father was still at the factory. `[CONFIRM-FATHER-WORK-STATUS: research bible notes salary collapse; father described as finding informal work; leaving him at the factory through mid-90s with degraded salary is the correct narrative anchor]` He went in the mornings and came home in the evenings, which was the same as always. What was different: the small things that had been routine — a bottle of something on the shelf, a magazine subscription, the occasional package of biscuits from the store with the red wrapper — had become infrequent. Not absent. Just no longer reliable.

We were not the family who was going hungry. I want to be clear about this, because there is a version of this story where the scarcity is the point, and I have never been sure that version is honest. We were the family that adjusted. The flour in the canister was almost always there. The occasional scarcity was of the kind that produces knowledge about what matters and what doesn't, not the kind that produces fear.

But I was learning the grammar of reduction: the smaller cuts on the butter, the tea with less sugar, the decision about whether to buy new shoes or extend the life of the old ones by another season. The grammar was new. My parents had grown up in a world where the grammar was different — where the things were fewer but more certain — and they were learning the new one alongside me.

---

In the spring of 1995, Aziz's family moved to a different building. Not out of Chirchiq — his father's work was here — but a different building, in the part of the district where the buildings were slightly newer, slightly better maintained. His father had arranged it. I did not fully understand at nine the mechanism by which an apartment in a certain building was arranged versus another, but I understood the direction of travel: upward, which was a direction my family was not moving.

We remained friends. He came to our side of the district and I went to his. But the walk was longer and the casually-encountered play — the kind you fall into without planning — happened less.

He came to visit the afternoon after he moved, with a small formality that was new between us, and we sat in my room and talked. He asked, at one point, whether my family was staying. Not *moving* — staying. The question implied the alternative. He had the directness of a child whose parents had told him the thing, or enough of the thing, that he could ask it plainly.

I said I thought so.

He asked if I wanted to stay.

I looked at him. I was ten. I was aware that this was a question adults were asking each other in different forms and that I had inherited it. I did not have the answer my parents had, which was a complicated adult answer made of money and family and the specific weight of the familiar. I had only what I actually felt.

What I actually felt was: I want Kolya to be two floors up and I want to have a car list that doesn't have to be divided. I want the flour to be there without planning. I want the Uzbek examination to make sense the first time I read it, not the third.

What I said was: "I don't know."

Aziz nodded as though this was an acceptable answer. "My parents say it's better to stay," he said. "My father says the ones who left too fast will want to come back."

I thought about this. "Where would they come back to?" I said.

He didn't have an answer for that. We were both ten.

---

What I understood by 1995, without having words for it yet: the country had been remade while I was inside it, and the remade version had a logic I could follow but in which I was not entirely native. Not foreign either. Something without a clean name, which was its own kind of problem.

My language was right for the apartment and wrong for the examination. My face was right for neither the Russian claim that was leaving nor the Uzbek claim that was arriving. My name — which I will still not write down here — could belong to either, depending on how you said it, which I had also begun to notice. `[CONFIRM-NAME-AMBIGUITY: the protagonist's name may be ambiguous between Russian and Uzbek versions, author to confirm. This is a structural element.]`

My sister, from her corner of my parents' room, had no concerns yet. She was three. She was learning to say things in Russian because Russian was the language of the apartment. One day, perhaps sooner than I thought, she would also have Farida Yusupovna, and Farida Yusupovna would wait for her with that determined brightness, and she would try again.

I hoped, with the inarticulate hope of a ten-year-old, that it would be easier for her. I didn't think it would be. But I hoped it.

---

There was a last day at school before summer, the year I was ten, when Sanjar — quiet Sanjar, who had been careful and steady in my class for three years now — walked past me in the hallway and said something. He said it in Uzbek. A whole sentence, fast, aimed at me.

I understood most of it. Most of it was something ordinary — something about a summer plan, a place he was going, a relative's house. I understood *yoz* for summer and *boramiz* for we're going and a name of a place I didn't catch.

I answered in Uzbek. Not perfectly. Not in the Uzbek of someone for whom the door was unlocked. But in the Uzbek of someone who had been practicing the key long enough that it turned.

Sanjar looked at me for a moment with an expression I have thought about since: not surprised, exactly. Something more like acknowledgment. The acknowledgment you give someone who has done the work to arrive somewhere, even if arriving wasn't particularly graceful.

He said something I completely understood — it was short, it was: *yaxshi* — good — and walked on.

I stood in the hallway for a moment.

*Yaxshi.* Good.

It was not triumph. It was more lateral than triumph. It was the feeling of a key in a lock, and the lock is inside a door, and the door opens onto a country that is learning to call itself its own, and you are standing in the hallway of it with your 4-out-of-5 Uzbek and your half of a car list and your sister who doesn't know yet what you know, and the summer is coming and it is yours too, even if you have to negotiate your way into it every time.
