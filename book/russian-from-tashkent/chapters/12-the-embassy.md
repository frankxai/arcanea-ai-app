## Chapter 12: The Embassy

The badge was blue.

This seems like a small thing to begin with, but the badge was the first specific fact about the Embassy that was mine in a material sense — not a document, not a phone call, not an envelope on a factory floor, but a physical object with my photograph and my name and the seal of the United States of America and a clip that attached to a shirt pocket. I stood at the security checkpoint on my first morning and clipped it on and walked through into the compound and the guard on the outer perimeter, who was Uzbek, from a private security contractor, nodded at me with the specific nod of someone acknowledging a colleague. The nod meant: you are expected here. The nod was not nothing.

Inside the checkpoint, the compound was a different country. Not America — not in any way that would survive examination — but not Uzbekistan either. Something in between, which was technically the correct status: diplomatic compound, extraterritorial, subject to different rules about who came in and who came out and what happened inside. The buildings were American-functional: efficient, air-conditioned, fluorescent-lit in certain sections. The signs were in English with Uzbek and Russian subtitles. The coffee was American coffee, which is to say it was a different beverage from what I drank at home, lighter and more abundant, served in paper cups, available in a break room that had a small American flag on one wall and a list of Embassy emergency numbers on another.

I had my photograph taken for the badge before any of this, in a room where a staff services officer asked me to look at a mark on the wall and not smile. I looked at the mark and did not smile. The photograph that resulted is not a flattering photograph. I still have it.

---

My section was consular `[CONFIRM-EMBASSY-SECTION]`. The Consular Section of a US Embassy processes visa applications from citizens of the host country who wish to travel to the United States. It is, in the hierarchy of embassy work, neither the most glamorous section nor the least. It is the section that has the most contact with ordinary Uzbek citizens — not with government officials, not with business delegations, but with the full range of people who have decided, for whatever reason, that they need to be on the other side of the planet from where they were born.

An FSN in the consular section assists in the processing of those applications. The work is: document review, scheduling, pre-interview information collection, translation support during interviews where needed, data entry into the system, coordination with the American officer who conducts the interview and makes the visa decision. The FSN does not make the visa decision. The FSN is not permitted to advise the applicant about the decision or to communicate the decision before the American officer has communicated it. The FSN is the frictionless surface on which the transaction moves.

I had done this before. The vocabulary was different and the stakes were higher, but the structural position was the same position I had occupied at the hotel desk: the hinge. The door opening both ways, my job to be what the hinge was made of.

---

There was an officer in my first year who I will call David `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]`. He was from Ohio, mid-thirties, this was his second tour — he had been in Moldova before — and he brought to visa adjudication the particular combination of genuine engagement and procedural discipline that the job required, and that not all officers managed with equal consistency. He interviewed applicants, on average, for three to four minutes each. In three to four minutes he had to read a document file, conduct a brief conversation in English or with my translation assistance, assess credibility and intent, and make a decision that would significantly affect someone's life or not, depending entirely on which side of the decision the person landed on.

I watched him do this for three years. I will not describe his methodology in detail — not from confidentiality, but because the methodology was less interesting than what it revealed about the room, which was what I was there to observe.

The visa interview room was a window. A reinforced plexiglass window with a speaker, in the way of a bank or a post office counter. On one side: the American officer and the FSN. On the other side: the applicant. The distance across the window was perhaps forty centimeters. It was not a large space. The things that crossed that forty centimeters were: documents, questions, answers, a small amount of hope or the absence of hope, and the specific kind of human attention that both parties paid to the transaction, for entirely different reasons.

I had been on the other side of many windows. I had been on the hotel side and I had been on the customer side. The Embassy window was different from all of these because on the other side of the Embassy window there was someone who had prepared, in many cases, for months — who had gathered documents, paid fees, obtained photographs, answered forms, convinced their employer to write a letter, convinced their bank to produce statements, convinced themselves and sometimes their family that this was reasonable and possible. They had stood in a queue. They had been processed through the outer checkpoint by the Uzbek guard. They had sat in the waiting room and looked at the American information posters in English and Russian and had thought, probably, the thought that everyone in a waiting room thinks, which is: what will happen when I get to the window.

I was at the window.

---

The daily count of visa applications in 2007 Tashkent was not low `[CONFIRM-APPROXIMATE-VISA-VOLUME]`. The post-Andijan period had dampened the NGO and program sectors that generated some visa traffic, but it had not reduced the number of Uzbek citizens who wanted to travel to the United States for family visits, for business, for education programs, for the full range of ordinary reasons that human beings move across the surface of the planet. The US-Uzbek bilateral relationship was at a managed low; the visa line was full.

I will describe a specific day, which I will call a Tuesday in the autumn of 2008 `[CONFIRM-SCENE-YEAR]`, not because any particular Tuesday was defined by its day or its year but because specificity is the only way I know how to write this, and because general descriptions of Embassy work have been written by people who were not standing on my side of the window.

The morning queue began forming at seven. The Embassy opened the consular window at eight-thirty. In the ninety minutes before opening, the people in the queue stood in the concrete forecourt behind the outer perimeter and talked to each other or did not, depending on whether they were the kind of people who talked to strangers in queues. Tashkent in autumn was cool in the morning, the apricot trees in the neighborhood already losing their leaves, the light at seven AM the particular low autumn light that does not insist on itself.

By the time I was at my desk at eight, the file review had been done — I had been doing it for forty minutes, the same as every morning — and the first appointments were in the system. The first appointment was a woman from Namangan, mid-fifties, immigrant visa petition for family reunification. Her daughter had been in the United States for four years on a student visa and had married an American citizen; the daughter had filed the petition. The mother had taken a train from Namangan to Tashkent the previous day and was staying with relatives in Yunusabad. She was wearing a blue headscarf and a good coat that was slightly too warm for the day. She had her documents in a clear plastic folder that she held against her chest with both hands.

I greeted her in Russian. She looked at me with the expression that applicants produced when a local person spoke to them — the expression of recognition mixed with the particular anxiety of: this is still foreign, and this local person is on that side, and I am on this side, and the window is between us.

I asked her to please put her documents in the tray. I walked her through the standard information collection. Her Russian was good; she had been a schoolteacher. She was sixty-two. She had one daughter in California and one son in Tashkent. She wanted to meet her grandchildren. She had a plane ticket — provisional, awaiting visa approval — for December.

David came to the window for the interview. I translated two questions that the woman had not caught in English — her English was minimal — and David nodded and looked at the file and asked the standard questions about ties to Uzbekistan, about her son, about whether she intended to return after the visit. The son's employment. The property she owned. The questions were procedural and the answers were truthful, which I knew because by 2008 I had a reasonable calibration for what truth sounded like in an interview room.

The interview lasted six minutes. David said: we'll let you know by email in three to five business days. I told the woman this in Russian. She looked at the window and then at me.

"Will it be approved?" she said to me, quietly.

This is the question that the window is not supposed to answer. The FSN does not advise on outcomes. The FSN communicates procedural information.

"We'll let you know by email," I said.

She nodded. She put her documents back in the plastic folder. She said spasibo and turned and walked back through the waiting room.

I watched her go. David had moved to the next file.

Three days later, when the email notification went out, I saw her name in the approved queue. I had no way to reach her to tell her. The system notified her by email. She got to California in December. I know this only because six months later she arrived for an exit interview — a procedural requirement that is now abolished but was then standard — and she recognized me at the window and said: "You were here when I came the first time." And I said yes and she said her grandchildren's names, two of them, the boy's name and the girl's name, and she smiled in the way that people smile when they have the evidence in front of them that a thing they needed to happen has happened.

I processed her exit paperwork. The whole thing took four minutes.

---

There were the refusals. I will not spend as much time on them as on the approval because the refusals were more numerous and because my relationship to the refusals was complicated by the fact that I could not say everything I understood about them.

What I understood: the legal framework of US immigration law. The categories of ineligibility. The burden of proof, which rested on the applicant. The concept of immigrant intent, and why the consular officer's job was to assess it from a thin file and a four-minute conversation.

What I also understood: the person on the other side of the window had, in many cases, a reason for wanting to go that did not fit into any of the categories. They wanted to go because Tashkent in 2008 was Tashkent in 2008. They wanted to go because their daughter was in New Jersey and the daughter was not going to come back. They wanted to go because they were forty-five and they had watched the Russian-speaking half of their city leave over the previous fifteen years and they had stayed and staying had cost them something they were only now fully accounting.

The legal category for "I have stayed as long as I can stay" is not on the non-immigrant visa form.

I translated the refusals in the voice I had learned at the hotel — the register of regret that is not admission of fault — and the applicants heard what they heard and sometimes accepted it quietly and sometimes did not. The ones who did not accept it quietly — who argued, who asked why, who said they would try again — I processed with the same efficiency as the ones who did. The efficiency was not cruelty. It was the protection of a system that had to work at scale, and the window had to stay open, and the next person was waiting.

I have thought about this. I thought about it at the time and I thought about it on the marshrutka home and I thought about it in the years since. The thinking does not produce a resolution. What it produces is an accurate account of the position: I was the Uzbek national at the window of the American institution, in the room that processed whether Uzbek citizens could reach the United States, and I held this position with competence and with the understanding that competence in this context was not neutral. Nothing in that room was neutral. The window was not neutral. The forty centimeters were not neutral. I was not neutral.

What I can say is that I was fair. I was, within the constraints of the role, which were real constraints, fair. This is not a small claim and it is not a large claim. It is the claim I am willing to make.

I will now describe the Embassy in the post-Andijan years, which means I will describe an atmosphere rather than an event, because the events had happened before I arrived and what I found when I arrived was the residue of those events hardened into institutional practice.

The K2 eviction had been in July 2005. The Embassy move to the new compound in Yunusabad had been in February 2006. I arrived in early 2007. What this means is that I walked into an institution that had spent two years living with the consequences of a political rupture and had, by the time I arrived, developed the specific adaptive posture of an institution that has processed trauma and resumed operations: careful, attentive, not quite relaxed.

The American staff at the Embassy were — let me say this carefully — good at their jobs, and the complexity of their situation, which was the complexity of representing a government whose relationship with the host country was defined by a massacre and a base eviction, was visible in the quality of their attention. They watched the Uzbek staff with a thoughtfulness that was not suspicion and not entirely its opposite — a professional attentiveness to the signals that a local employee might send about the current political atmosphere, about what was being said outside the compound, about what the mood in the city was on a given week.

The Uzbek staff watched the American staff with an equivalent attentiveness. This was not surveillance in any formal sense. It was the natural attention of people who have learned that their professional position is contingent on a bilateral relationship they do not control and whose temperature changes without notice. You learned to read the American mood the way you learned to read the hotel lobby: not with anxiety, but with the constant low-level monitoring that is the real cognitive texture of working inside someone else's institution in your own country.

My Uzbek colleague in the consular section, whom I will call Dilnoza `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]`, had been at the Embassy for six years by the time I arrived — she had survived the Andijan chill, the position cuts, the compound move — and she had the specific authority of a person who has outlasted a period of institutional contraction. She knew the American consul by name and history. She knew which American officers were genuinely committed to the mission and which were doing their tour and counting months. She knew the SNB officer who was assigned to the outer perimeter not because she had any formal contact with him but because in Tashkent in 2007 you knew who the SNB was if you paid attention, which she did.

She told me nothing explicit. She showed me, over several months, how to move through the compound with the specific economy of motion that said: I am competent here, I belong here, I am not making a claim that requires examination. It was the same skill I had learned at the hotel. It was the same skill I had brought from childhood, from the courtyard, from the school corridors where being Russian-speaking had required a certain quality of presence. Every context had its own version of this skill and the versions accumulated.

---

There was a morning in October 2008 — I remember October because the apricot trees in our courtyard had begun to turn, which was the way I tracked the years in those days, by the change in trees — when an applicant came to the consular section whose file Dilnoza handed me without comment.

She did not say *look at this*. She did not say *be careful*. She placed it on my desk with the small motion of a person setting down a cup of tea, and she returned to her own desk, and she opened her own file, and she began her own work. I have thought about that motion many times. It was the motion of someone who had decided that the file required attention but had also decided that the decision to attend to it would not be spoken aloud, because speaking it aloud would have made the attention visible to anyone who happened to be in the room, and the room contained an American officer and a window through which the SNB liaison watched the gate.

The file was a visa application for a man in his forties whose listed employer was a translation services company that had, in the public record, done contract work for several international organizations during the Andijan period. The man had a wife and two children. He was applying for a visitor visa to attend an academic conference in Massachusetts.

In a different month, in a different year, the file would have been a routine refusal under the standard non-immigrant intent criteria. He had a young family and an established business; the consular officer would have denied the visa with the standard letter and the man would have applied again the following year, and the year after, and at some point he would have stopped applying. This was the normal grammar of the consular section.

But Dilnoza had placed the file on my desk.

What this meant — what I understood, without being told — was that there was something about this case that was not routine. Either the applicant had a history I needed to know before the interview, or the applicant was someone whose interview required a particular kind of attention, or the applicant was the subject of an interest that had been communicated to Dilnoza through a channel that the consular section did not formally have.

I did not ask which. I read the file.

The man came in for his interview at eleven o'clock. The American officer asked him three questions. He answered all three competently in adequate English. The officer denied the visa under 214(b). The man received his refusal letter without surprise. He thanked the officer and left.

After he left, the officer turned to me and said: "That seemed clear."

I said it had seemed clear.

He returned to his next file. I returned to mine. Dilnoza did not look up. She was processing her own queue. The room continued. Across the courtyard, through the security window, the SNB liaison who watched the gate continued to watch the gate. The October light moved across the consular section the way the October light moved across every room in the city: at the same angle, with the same patience.

What I understood, and what Dilnoza understood, and what the applicant understood, and what the American officer did not understand, was that the visa refusal had been the correct outcome by every formal metric, and that the applicant had received the precise outcome he had come for. He had applied. He had been denied. He had a denial letter. He had, in his hand, the document that proved he had attempted to leave and been refused. He could now show this document to whoever required it of him.

I do not know who required it. I do not know what arrangement the document was part of. I do not know whether the man wanted to attend the conference or not.

Dilnoza did not tell me, and I did not ask, because asking would have been the kind of question that made the answer dangerous to know.

In the late afternoon, when the officer had left for the day and Dilnoza and I were closing down our terminals, she said: "Coffee tomorrow."

I said yes.

We had coffee the next morning at the little kiosk near the gate that the FSNs used because the compound cafeteria was where the Americans were. We talked about her son, who was preparing for university entrance exams, and about her mother, who had been ill, and about the chinar in the courtyard outside her apartment building that the city had begun to talk about removing. We did not talk about the file.

I have thought, in the years since, about what it would have meant to speak about the file. To have named, in language, what we had both seen, what we had both done, what we had both not done. The naming would have required a vocabulary that was not available to us in any of the three languages we shared, because the vocabulary had not been built for this kind of thing — for the work that happened across the visa counter when the visa counter was also the only legal exit from a particular kind of attention.

What we shared instead of vocabulary was the coffee, and the chinar, and the universities, and the mothers. The texture of FSN work in the post-Andijan years was not heroism and not collaboration. Not silence as betrayal, not silence as resistance. It was the willingness to sit across a small table from someone who had also seen what you had seen, and to talk about the trees, and to leave the rest in the place where, by mutual decision, it would not be spoken.

---

I want to describe the 2008 period specifically, because 2008 was when certain things clarified.

In 2008 the US-Uzbek relationship was in the slow diplomatic rehabilitation that follows a rupture: both parties behaving as if the Andijan conversation had been had and could now be set aside, neither side quite believing this, both sides finding it convenient to proceed as if they did. The American counter-narcotics program had resumed. The USAID education programs had modest funding again. There were visiting Congressional delegations, which produced the particular energy of important Americans arriving in a place they had previously decided not to care about and discovering it was more complex than they had decided. I processed the visa paperwork for the delegations' Uzbek counterparts and watched the briefings through the glass partition of the consular waiting room and thought nothing consequential.

What I thought, instead, was the kind of thinking you do when you have been in a particular position long enough to see the patterns: the rhythm of bilateral relations, the institutional memory that persisted in the FSN corps when American officers rotated every two years, the specific way that an Embassy operates as a vessel for a policy that was made elsewhere and that the local staff live inside without having made.

I was twenty-three. I was learning, without being taught, the difference between a policy and a country. A country is a thing you can be born into, live inside, leave, return to, grieve. A policy is a thing that is decided about you. The Embassy was a building full of people who had been assigned to implement a policy and who were, almost all of them, trying to do this well, and who were doing it in a country that contained millions of people who had not been asked their preference.

I was one of those people. I was also, each morning, walking through the compound gate.

These two things were not a paradox I was required to resolve. They were the condition.

---

There was an American officer, in my third year, who asked me a question I have thought about since.

Her name, which I will not use, was from a state I had never visited, and she had come to Tashkent on a first tour after a posting in Europe, and she was the kind of person whose genuine curiosity about Central Asia was not performance but was also not yet calibrated to the specific texture of the place — she still saw things with fresh eyes, which is sometimes an asset and sometimes a liability. She was assigned to the political section `[CONFIRM-OFFICER-SECTION]`. We had met at a compound function — one of the embassy's periodic all-hands gatherings, lunch in the conference room, the kind of thing that was both collegial and carefully orchestrated.

She asked me how long I had been working here. I told her. She asked how I had come to the job — the standard question, asked with genuine interest. I gave her the standard short version: hotel work, a posting came up, I applied. She nodded. Then she said: "Do you think you'll stay in Tashkent?"

I said I didn't know.

She looked at me for a moment. "I ask because — " She stopped. She was trying to formulate something and was deciding whether to formulate it in front of me. "I ask because a lot of the FSNs I've met who have been here for a while have a kind of — I don't know the right word. Patience? That isn't it exactly."

"Endurance," I said.

"Maybe. But that sounds hard."

"It is sometimes," I said.

She thought about this. "Is this the kind of place that — " She stopped again. "Is this sustainable? Being here, working here, when the bilateral relationship is what it is?"

I said that the bilateral relationship had been what it was for several years and the work had continued.

She nodded slowly. "But for you specifically. Not institutionally."

I was twenty-four. I had been at the Embassy for two years. I had processed several thousand visa applications and translated several hundred interview conversations and gone home on the marshrutka every evening to the Yunusabad apartment and eaten dinner with my parents and watched the news and gone to bed. The work was good work in the specific sense that it was work I was competent at in a context that required the full range of what I was, which was: Russian-speaking, Uzbek-national, English-functional, locally-rooted, institutionally-legible. It was also the only work I knew of, in Tashkent in 2009, that paid a wage that allowed me to save anything while continuing to exist in Tashkent in 2009.

"It's sustainable," I said. "For now."

She seemed to hear something in *for now*. She said: "Are you thinking about leaving?"

I did not answer this. Not because the answer was no, but because the answer was a complete sentence that required more than the lunch break to say, and because we were at a compound function and the compound function was not the place for it, and because she was a first-tour officer who would rotate out in two years and I was a local employee whose career was a closed system and the specific asymmetry of this was information that the lunch break would not improve.

"Not yet," I said.

She nodded. She asked if I wanted more coffee. I said yes. We talked about something else.

---

In the later part of my Embassy years — 2009 and into 2010 — I became the FSN who the newer American officers asked about context. Not the political context, which was the political section's business, but the ordinary context: how does one find a good doctor in Tashkent, is it safe to take a taxi after midnight, what is happening in the news that isn't on the English-language wires. This is not a formal role. It is the role that accrues to a local employee who has been somewhere long enough and who is visibly interested in the answer rather than in the performance of the answer.

I was the person who explained, to an officer who asked about the plov at a diplomatic reception, the specific difference between Tashkent-style and Fergana-style and why the argument between them was a real argument and not local color. I was the person who, when a contractor from the States asked why there were so many portraits of Karimov on public buildings, said: because there are, and that was the complete answer, and the contractor heard it and understood that the complete answer was complete. I was the person who, when a colleague was preparing a program event for Uzbek students at an American Corner and asked me to review the materials for tone, read them and said: this word, *freedom*, is fine; this phrase, *independent choice*, reads as political in Uzbek in a way it doesn't in English, and she changed the phrase.

I was the translator in the full sense. Not only the Russian-to-English and Uzbek-to-English sense, which was the formal role. The other kind: the person who stood at the point where two systems touched and prevented the damage that systems do to each other when they touch without a hinge.

I want to describe what the Embassy looked like from the outside.

From outside the perimeter wall — the concrete barriers, the guard booth, the Vehicle Inspection Point — the Embassy was a building behind a wall. This is the architectural grammar of post-Nairobi American diplomatic compounds: fortress-legible, set back from the street, the perimeter itself a communication about the threat level the State Department assigns to the host country. The communication, in Tashkent, was: elevated. The compound communicated this to every person who walked past it on Moyqorghon Street, which included my neighbors, my parents, the marshrutka drivers, the women with string bags coming from the market, the schoolchildren whose school was two blocks further down the street. They passed the Embassy wall every day.

My mother had walked past it to visit me once, in my first year, bringing lunch in a container because she had calculated that Embassy cafeteria food was expensive and that I had not yet sorted out the canteen system. She passed the outer perimeter, showed her documents to the Uzbek guard, was cleared, and came to meet me at the inner checkpoint. She looked at the compound with the expression she used when assessing a situation she had not encountered before — the specific neutral attention that preceded her forming a view.

"It's very clean," she said.

I said the Americans were particular about cleaning.

"The windows," she said.

I said yes, the windows were cleaned every week.

She ate lunch with me in the break room. She looked at the American flag on the wall and the emergency numbers list. She looked at the paper coffee cups. She said the lunch was good. She asked about the work and I explained the consular section and she listened with the attention she had brought to my account of the ants in 1989, the same quality of listening: this is a problem, you are working on it, let's understand the problem correctly.

When she was leaving she stopped at the inner checkpoint and looked at me.

"Is it good work?" she said. Not *is it safe*, not *do they treat you well*, not the questions a mother is expected to ask. Is it good work.

"It is," I said.

She nodded. She walked back through the compound. The Uzbek guard nodded at her. She walked out onto Moyqorghon Street with the empty lunch container and went home on the marshrutka.

---

There were things about the Embassy work that I could not explain in the break room and that I will try to explain here, from the distance that makes explanation possible without making it tidy.

The Uzbek applicants who came to the consular window were people I recognized. Not personally — I did not know them — but in the way that recognition works inside a cultural context you have grown up in: you recognize the suit the man is wearing as the suit that was bought for this occasion; you recognize the folder that the woman holds against her chest; you recognize the specific mixture of preparation and anxiety that is the face of someone who has done everything correct and is now waiting for the result of everything correct. I recognized this because I had been, for most of my life, on the other side of institutional windows. I was on this side now, and the forty centimeters between me and the person standing at the window was a distance I had crossed and that they had not yet crossed, and the knowledge of that distance was present in every transaction.

I processed the applications with competence. This is not separate from what I have just said. The competence was how I held the knowledge of the distance — not by refusing the knowledge but by converting it into the best possible performance of the job. Each person at the window deserved the best possible performance of the job. I gave it. This is the claim.

There were days when the claim was expensive. I will not catalogue them. The Uzbek applicant who was refused because his ties to Uzbekistan were too thin to satisfy the standard and whose ties to Uzbekistan were too thin because Uzbekistan had spent fifteen years making thin ties to it the correct response. The applicant who cried, quietly, at the window, and to whom I could say only the procedural formula in the procedural tone. The applicant who looked at me when the refusal was communicated and said: *you know how it is*, in Uzbek, quietly, which was an appeal not to the consular section but to the person behind the badge who was from here, and I responded with the procedural formula because the formula was what I had, which is true, and because the formula was what was needed to keep the window functional for the next person, which is also true.

These two truths do not reconcile. I spent three years not reconciling them and eventually I stopped expecting the reconciliation.

I will describe the end of the chapter here, which is also the end of the book.

It is 2010. I am twenty-five. I have been at the Embassy for three years. The bilateral relationship is in what analysts call a managed normalization — which is to say, both governments are behaving, and the behavior is functional, and neither government has recently produced an event that disrupted the behavior. The consular section runs its quota. The American officers rotate; I know the rotation schedule well enough to brief incoming officers on the local context before they have asked for the briefing. The city outside the compound is Karimov's Tashkent: the marble facades, the Tamerlane monument, the portraits on the public buildings, the chaikhanas unchanged, the plov unchanged, the marshrutkas unchanged, the SNB unchanged. I commute each morning on the same line through the same streets I have commuted on since I was in school.

The afternoon I am thinking of — I want to describe a specific afternoon — is in September, which is the month I associate with returns, the September when I came back from Russia, the September that still smells of damp cotton and diesel and the heated dust from the plane trees.

I am at the consular window `[CONFIRM-CLOSING-SCENE-DATE]`. The day's interview queue is nearly finished. There is one appointment remaining: a young man, twenty-two or twenty-three, presenting an application for a cultural exchange program, one of the programs that runs through the State Department's educational exchange division. His English is very good — he has prepared for this interview with the thoroughness of someone who has been told that the preparation matters. His documents are in order. He has a place in the exchange program already confirmed, contingent on the visa. He wants to go to Chicago for eight months.

David, or whoever is conducting the interview `[CONFIRM-ALIAS]`, runs through the standard questions. The young man answers them correctly, which means: accurately, with the specific quality of a person who is telling the truth about their intentions, which are to go to Chicago, participate in the program, and come back. His ties to Uzbekistan are a mother and a university place and an apartment that his parents have their names on. These are sufficient.

The interview takes five minutes. The officer marks the file approved. I tell the young man, in Russian, that his visa has been approved and he will have his passport back within two business days.

He looks at me for a moment — not with the overwhelmed gratitude that some applicants produce, not with the relief of someone who was not sure the outcome would be good, but with the specific expression of someone who expected the outcome to be good and is confirming that expectation, and who is already, in the thirty seconds after the confirmation, thinking about Chicago. He is twenty-two. He has a cultural exchange program and a confirmed visa and an idea of what comes next.

"Thank you," he said.

"Good luck," I said, in Russian.

He walked out through the waiting room. I watched him go. I have seen thousands of people walk out through the waiting room and I always, briefly, watched them go.

At the end of the day I logged off the system. I collected my things from the desk — the pen, the notepad, the reading glasses I had started needing two years ago and that I still did not quite accept as mine `[CONFIRM-EYEGLASS-DETAIL]`. I clipped off the badge and put it in my jacket pocket. I walked out through the inner checkpoint and the compound and the outer perimeter. The Uzbek guard nodded at me. I nodded back.

Outside the wall, Moyqorghon Street was doing what Moyqorghon Street did at six in the evening: the marshrutkas, the women with string bags, the schoolchildren now coming home from the other direction. The apricot trees on the block were turning. The Yunusabad apartment blocks in the middle distance were the same apartment blocks I had grown up in, the same nine-story geometry, the same balconies stacked with the life that balconies are stacked with. Somewhere in those blocks my mother was in the kitchen and my father was at the kitchen table with a glass of tea.

I stood outside the compound gate for a moment — not for any particular reason, no drama, the marshrutka to Yunusabad was three minutes further down the street and I was not yet late — and I looked at the street. The street I had grown up adjacent to. The city I had left and returned to. The building behind me with the wall and the flag and the window and the forty centimeters and the three years of transactions.

What was I thinking?

I was thinking about whether my mother had made plov or something else. I was thinking about a document I had not finished that I would need to complete in the morning. I was thinking, briefly, about the young man with the Chicago visa, who was on a marshrutka somewhere, probably, also going home for dinner and also thinking about what came next.

I was also, underneath all of this — not with urgency, not with grief, but with the particular quality of attention that comes when you have been a certain place long enough to read its weather — aware that this was a version of things that would not hold indefinitely. Not because anything specific was wrong. Because everything specific was right, and things that are entirely right have their own kind of instability.

I did not know what came next. I had been told, implicitly, by the young man across the window and by the applicants before him and by the man in the hotel lobby who had gone to Stuttgart and by Gennady at the winding machine: the knowing of what comes next is not distributed evenly across the population of a city, and I had, by 2010, reached the point where not knowing was no longer sustainable as a resting state.

I picked up the marshrutka. I went home. My mother had made plov.

I ate with my parents at the small kitchen table where I had always eaten. My father read the newspaper. My mother poured tea from the podstakannik. The news was on the television in the other room — Uzbek television, the Karimov version, the version with the ministers and the cotton yield and nothing of consequence. Outside, the Yunusabad apartment blocks were lit in the evening, windows yellow across the entire grid, which was my childhood and was also a place I was going to leave.

I knew this and I ate plov and said nothing about it and went to bed.

The knowing was enough for one evening.

*There is a year between this September and what I will call the beginning of the next chapter, which is a chapter this book does not contain. What it contains is this: thirty years ending at a kitchen table in Yunusabad, a badge in a jacket pocket, a marshrutka route that could be walked in thirty minutes. The full inventory of a life that was complete in the sense that every life is complete at any given moment, and incomplete in the sense that this particular life did not close here. But here is where the book closes.*

*Because the question the book has been asking — where are you from, and what does that mean now, and is there a country whose name is yours — does not resolve in September 2010. It does not resolve. What happens instead is that you learn to carry it differently. The question becomes a part of the weight you walk with, and the weight becomes, with enough time, simply the weight of yourself.*

*I was twenty-five. I was Russian-speaking, Uzbek-born, Embassy-badged, courtyard-raised, plant-seasoned, hotel-schooled. I was from here. I was also, and had always been, from somewhere the map had not yet drawn.*

*The apricot trees were turning. I went to bed.*
