# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 11: Chirchiq Transformer Plant

*Chirchiq — 2006–2007 · Ages 21–22*

`[CONFIRM-CHIRCHIQ-PATH: Was there a gap between hotel work and Embassy application? The timeline here places a Chirchiq period 2006–2007, between hotel years and Embassy start. If the hotel-to-Embassy transition was more direct, this chapter may span an earlier period or a different duration. The Chirchiq chapter's emotional function — the older Russian-speaker as possible future — works at any age between 18 and 25.]`

---

There was a period — not long, and I have not always been sure why I am including it except that it has a logic the book requires — between the hotel and the Embassy when I worked at the Chirchiq Transformer Plant.

I want to be careful here about the explanation, because explanations of this kind of move tend to produce the wrong impression. The move was not downward mobility experienced as trauma. It was a sideways move in a direction that seemed, at the time, to have a reason. The Embassy application was in process. The background investigation took longer than the letter had implied. Meanwhile there was a position at the plant — through a friend of my father, through the specific telephone network of Tashkent that I had already described in the context of the hotel — and the position paid in a way that the interim work I was doing did not, and there was a specific calculation that went: this is temporary, this is different, this is the kind of thing you do in the space between things.

I have said the protagonist is not ambitious and this is true. But the absence of ambition is not the absence of calculation. I was calculating.

So: Chirchiq. From October 2006 `[CONFIRM-CHIRCHIQ-START-YEAR]` for approximately eight months, which is long enough to know a place but not long enough to become it.

---

The plant smelled like copper before you entered the gate. Not metaphorically — there was a duct from the transformer winding bays that exhaled copper oxide and cutting oil into the October air of Chirchiq at seven-thirty in the morning, and you walked through that exhalation on the way to the security check, and by the time you had clocked in and reached your section the smell was inside you. It did not leave at the end of the shift. You carried it home on your skin and in your clothes and the soap at the washbasins in the locker room addressed it without eliminating it.

The section I worked in was winding operations `[CONFIRM-CHIRCHIQ-ROLE: line work, winding, quality control, or administrative?]`. Power transformer windings are, in basic terms, copper wire wound in specific patterns onto a core, to specific dimensions, to create the electromagnetic relationship between the high-voltage and low-voltage sides of the transformer. The machine that winds the wire — the *obmotochny stanok*, the winding machine — is operated by a winding operator who reads the specification sheet and adjusts the machine's parameters accordingly. The copper wire is precise, the geometry is precise, the tension is precise. You do this wrong and the transformer fails at load and somewhere in the grid a substation goes dark and someone has to come and explain this to the client, which is usually another Uzbek state enterprise and the explanation is a conversation no one wants to have.

The work required attention. The attention was possible. What the work did not require was the kind of extended engagement with other human beings that the hotel had required, which was either restful or depressing, and I was not always sure which.

---

The shift I was assigned was the late shift: 4 PM to midnight `[CONFIRM-CHIRCHIQ-SHIFT]`. This was the shift the senior operators had declined, the shift that fell to the newer workers and to certain older workers who had been on it for so long that the late shift had become simply their hours, the hours of their lives, without the qualification of seniority or preference. I was among the former and I was also, on the late shift, among the latter — which is to say that the late shift at the plant contained both the newest and the oldest, with not much in between.

The man I will call Gennady worked the winding bay adjacent to mine `[CONFIRM-ALIAS-GENNADY]`. He was fifty-three, or fifty-six — I am not certain, and the difference between fifty-three and fifty-six at a Soviet-era industrial plant in Uzbekistan in 2006 was not large, because the distinction between those years was less important than the distinction between the category of men who were still working and the category of men who were no longer working, and Gennady was firmly in the former. He had been at the Chirchiq plant since 1978. He was from Kharkiv `[CONFIRM-GENNADY-ORIGIN: or elsewhere in Ukraine/Russia]`, originally — his family had come in the Soviet engineering migration of the sixties and stayed because by the time there was a reason to leave, leaving had become harder than staying. His wife had died. His son was in Chelyabinsk. He had an apartment in Chirchiq, on one of the streets near the factory that had been built for the factory workers in the sixties, and he lived in it alone and came to the plant five days a week and wound transformer coils and ate in the factory canteen at nine PM and went home at midnight and repeated the procedure.

He was not unhappy, as far as I could tell. He was finished, which is not the same thing.

---

I want to describe a night at the plant, because the night shift has a specific texture that the day shift does not, and the texture is the chapter.

By ten PM the winding bay has settled into its own logic. The day shift is gone. The supervision that the day shift carries — the foreman visible, the plant manager occasionally in evidence, the awareness of being observed — has thinned to the minimal level of the night: a single foreman doing rounds every two hours, a quality control woman who sat at a desk near the varnishing bay and had the specific quality of someone who has seen everything at this job and no longer needs to watch to know. The noise of the machines was constant. Not loud in the way that construction is loud — not assaulting — but present, a winding note that you heard differently at ten PM than at four PM, the difference being that at ten PM you were inside it rather than adjacent to it.

The copper wire moved through my hands. This is a physical fact I have tried to translate into language and have not managed to: the sensation of copper wire under tension, the slight heat of the metal, the way the machine's rhythm created a responsive rhythm in your body that was not quite its own but that accommodated the machine's. After an hour, two hours, you were no longer thinking about the wire. The thinking moved below the threshold of monitoring. The hands did their work. The mind went somewhere that was almost rest.

This is, I think, what people mean when they say they miss physical work.

Gennady paused his machine at ten-thirty, as he did most nights, to check the tension gauge on a coil he was finishing. I was between specifications on my own machine — the current winding complete, the new specification sheet on the stand, two minutes to read and reset before starting. He looked over.

"How long have you been here?" he asked.

"Since October." About six weeks at that point.

He nodded. "Before this?"

"Tashkent. Hotel work."

He made a sound that was not quite acknowledgment and not quite dismissal but something in between.

"You'll go back," he said. It was not a question.

I said I had something else in process.

"The Embassy." He said it flatly. Not hostile. The way you say a word that everyone knows.

I said I had applied.

"My son's friend worked there," Gennady said. He reset his tension gauge. "Before the business with Andijan. Then they cut some of the local positions."

I said I had heard about the cuts.

"They're hiring again," he said. "Or so I heard."

He started his machine. The copper wire began moving again.

This was the whole conversation. In it there was, compressed into sixty seconds of exchange in a noisy winding bay at ten-thirty on a weeknight, the complete geometry of the situation: the fifty-three-year-old Russian-speaker who had been at this plant since before Uzbekistan was a country, who knew about the Embassy because his son's friend had worked there, who mentioned it without pressure or agenda because that was the kind of man he was, whose life would continue in exactly the same pattern whether I left or stayed, and who had said *you'll go back* not as prophecy but as observation, the observation of a man who had watched enough people come through the late shift to know the difference between the ones who were here and the ones who were passing through.

I was passing through.

He was here.

---

We ate in the canteen at nine. The canteen served *lagman* from a pot that had been on the stove since afternoon, which meant the noodles had absorbed the broth and the broth had concentrated into something rich and slightly too thick. There was bread. There was tea in large mugs rather than the tea glasses of the chaikhana, because the canteen was not a chaikhana, it was a factory canteen, and the distinction was total — same liquid, different institution, different meaning. The men who ate in the factory canteen at nine PM were the late-shift men, and the late-shift men were the men I have described: the newest and the oldest, with not much in between.

I ate with Gennady most evenings and occasionally with two younger men from the assembly section whose names I have changed and whose faces I still remember clearly, which is how I know that what I witnessed at the plant was not an abstract condition but specific men in specific bodies in a specific room that smelled of *lagman* and copper and the transformer varnish that clung to everything within a hundred meters of the varnishing bay.

The younger men were twenty-four and twenty-one, both Uzbek, both from Chirchiq families, and both in a situation that was the Uzbek version of mine: working at the plant because the plant paid and the alternatives were not obviously better, not planning to stay forever but not having a plan for the other option. One of them had a brother in Almaty. The other had an uncle in Tashkent who worked for a telecom company. We did not discuss these connections at length — not from privacy, but from the particular reticence of people who are in the same room for a reason they'd rather not examine.

Gennady ate his *lagman* and read a newspaper. *Narodnoe slovo* `[CONFIRM-NEWSPAPER: or Pravda Vostoka, or another publication]` — the Russian-language paper, which by 2006 had thinned considerably in terms of content, most of what might once have been real reporting having been replaced by official statements and cultural announcements. He read it the way people read newspapers in authoritarian states: for the spaces between the words, for what was not said, for the news contained in the particular angle of the official statement. This was not a sophisticated political practice. It was a survival habit.

He folded the newspaper when he finished. He looked up at the canteen ceiling with the expression of a man who has looked at this ceiling many times and who has not yet resolved his relationship to it.

"The plant used to have fifteen hundred workers," he said, once, in the middle of November. We were eating, not in the middle of a conversation about anything. He said it the way you say something that has been in your head for a while and that you are releasing into the air because the air might as well have it. "In the eighties. Maybe more. They brought people from Leningrad in forty-two with the equipment from the Electropult plant." He paused. "Now I don't know. Four hundred maybe. Three-fifty."

I said I had heard the original equipment was from Leningrad.

"My father came with it," Gennady said. He looked back at his newspaper. "Well. Not literally with it. A few years after."

His father had come with the Leningrad evacuation or its immediate aftermath. He was, in this sense, the third generation of a decision made under extreme duress in 1942, a decision that had brought a family from a besieged European city to an industrial Soviet city in the Uzbek SSR and that had proved, in the way that decisions made under extreme duress often prove, to be permanent without ever having been chosen. Gennady's father had not chosen Chirchiq. Gennady had not chosen to stay. But here he was at fifty-three, at a winding machine on the late shift, eating canteen lagman with a man twenty years younger who had applied for an Embassy job.

I did not say any of this in the canteen. It was not something that could be said in a canteen. It was something that existed in the air of the canteen in the way that certain things exist in rooms: present to anyone attending, never the subject of the conversation.

---

The plant was Soviet in its bones — which is not only a comment on the machinery, though the machinery was genuinely Soviet, maintained over decades by the improvisation that is the true skill of Soviet-legacy industrial operations. When a specific part fails on a 1968 winding machine, you do not order a replacement. You modify another part. You machine something from stock. You call the man two cities away who has had the same problem and compare solutions. This is not romanticized — it is simply what you do, because the supply chain that made those parts no longer exists and the budget for replacement equipment was cut in 1994 and not restored. The machines worked. They worked in the way that things work when the people operating them have made their personal reputations responsible for the operation.

Gennady could fix anything on the winding machines in his bay. Not because he was trained on every component — he was trained on his specific machine, Winding Station 7, and had been since 1984 — but because twenty-two years on the same machine means you know the machine the way you know a person whose habits you have watched long enough to predict. He could hear a tension misalignment before the gauge showed it. He could tell from the sound of the wire moving whether the spool feed was running slightly fast or slightly slow. This was not supernatural. It was attention deployed over a long time to a specific object. The specific object was a winding machine. The long time was a Soviet career in an industry that had, since 1991, been running on the fumes of that career.

I watched him work on the late shift and I thought: this is what expertise looks like when the world that required the expertise has collapsed. Not the expertise — the expertise was intact. Gennady's hands knew exactly what they knew. What had collapsed was the institutional structure that converted expertise into salary, into social position, into the sense that the work mattered beyond the immediate product. The transformer exited the plant and went somewhere into the grid and somewhere a substation ran that would not have run without it. This was real. It was simply invisible, and invisibility has a long-term effect on the person doing the invisible work that I was only beginning to understand.

My father had understood it. He had understood it since 1991, when his salary became ceremonial. He was at the technical institute now, doing something with consulting that kept him at a desk with colleagues, and whether this was adaptation or defeat depended on the day and the angle from which you looked at it. I looked at Gennady at the winding machine and I looked at my father at the technical institute and I saw the same structure, expressed in different vocabularies, across a twenty-year gap.

I did not formulate this at the time. I am formulating it now, from the distance that makes formulation possible and that also, inevitably, tidies and falsifies. At the time I was on the late shift at the Chirchiq plant watching Gennady wind transformer coils and thinking about whether the Embassy letter would come before the winter or in the spring.

---

The letter came in February.

I was at the winding machine. The foreman brought it to the bay — I had given the plant address because the application had been filed before I left the hotel and the address update had not registered, or I had not updated it `[CONFIRM-LETTER-DELIVERY-DETAIL]`. The envelope was white and official, with the American seal in the upper left corner. The foreman looked at it and looked at me and handed it to me without saying anything.

I read it at the machine. The position was confirmed. I was to report on a date three weeks away for the initial orientation.

Gennady was running his machine. He had his back to me. I stood at the winding station with the letter in my hand and the copper smell in the air and the machine noise that was always there, and I thought of nothing in particular, which is what I think when something has been expected for long enough that when it arrives it lands as confirmation rather than surprise.

At the break I told Gennady.

He looked up from his newspaper. He looked at the letter I was holding. He nodded once, slowly, the way he nodded when he had expected a thing.

"Good," he said.

He poured himself tea. He poured me tea. He went back to his newspaper. At the end of the canteen period he folded the newspaper and stood up.

"The morning shift supervisor is named Azizov," he said. "He can adjust your last days. Give you the full last week."

I said I hadn't thought about the last week yet.

"You should tell the supervisor by Monday," Gennady said. "He'll want time to adjust the shift roster."

This was the entire farewell. I mean this seriously — Gennady's instruction about the supervisor was the complete expression of his response to my news, which was the response of a man who operated in the practical register by disposition and had learned, over fifty years, that the practical register served as well as any other and caused less damage when used incorrectly. He was not cold. He was precise. The precision contained whatever the warmth was that he had.

I gave notice Monday. I worked the last week. On the last night Gennady and I ate *lagman* in the canteen as usual and he did not say anything about it being the last night and I did not either. When the shift ended he shook my hand at the gate. His hand was the hand of a man who had worked with copper wire since 1978 — hard in a way that had nothing to do with how hard it was to shake but that you registered anyway.

"Work well there," he said.

I said I would.

He walked to the bus stop. I walked to the marshrutka.

---

I want to say one more thing about the plant, and it is this: I was twenty-two, and I saw in Gennady a future I was not going to have, and I did not see this with the contempt that some people bring to seeing a future they are not going to have. The contempt is the easy version — the version where the narrator's escape is evidence of his superiority and the man who stays is evidence of limitation. That version is not available to me, partly because of what I know about Gennady and partly because of what I know about my father.

The truth is flatter and harder: I saw in Gennady a specific kind of integrity that was costing him something real, which was the cost of staying in the place where you belong to, in the role you are suited for, in the city your father helped build, in an economy that has decided your expertise is worth approximately three hundred and fifty workers instead of fifteen hundred. He was paying that cost every night on the late shift. I was not going to pay it. Whether I was right not to pay it is a question the book does not answer, because the book knows better than to answer questions of that shape.

What I can say is this: I left the plant in February and I carried the copper smell for a week after, no matter how many times I washed.
