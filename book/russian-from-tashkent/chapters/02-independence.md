# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 2: Independence

*Chirchiq / Yunusabad, Tashkent — August–December 1991* `[CONFIRM-YEAR: Ch1 establishes summer 1989 as ground state; this chapter opens late summer 1991 when protagonist is 6]`

---

The first thing that changed was the television.

Not what it showed — that changed too, but slowly, in ways I would only understand later. What changed first was how long my father sat in front of it. Through the summer of 1991 he sat in front of the television the way men sit in front of things that are happening but cannot yet be explained. He sat the way you sit when the problem is larger than sitting solves but sitting is the available position.

I was six years old. I had my wooden bus, now missing one wheel, and a collection of bottle caps I had been smoothing on the courtyard asphalt for most of the summer. I had a friend named Dimka who lived two floors up and whose father, I had recently learned, worked at the same factory as mine in some capacity that involved a different set of tools. `[CONFIRM-DIMKA: Ch1 doesn't establish him yet. Plant this now.]` I had the usual summer inventory: a scraped knee still healing, a piece of blue chalk I had been protecting for two weeks. I had the courtyard and the chinar tree and the heat that came off the asphalt in waves you could see if you looked at the pavement from the right angle.

What I did not have was any understanding of what was happening on the television.

---

In August the television showed tanks. I know this now as a fact. I did not know it then as anything except the shape of a machine that was not a regular machine, going somewhere it was not supposed to go. The face of the man with the birthmark appeared and then disappeared. The face of a different man appeared — a larger man, standing on or near something, his voice different from the way voices on the television usually sounded. My father watched without blinking. My mother stood in the doorway to the kitchen with her hands folded, and she had been about to do something in the kitchen when the thing on the television started, and she had not yet returned to do it.

I asked my father what the tanks were.

He said they were going somewhere.

I asked where.

He said Moscow.

I knew Moscow as the place where Vremya came from, and as the place my father's sister had gone when she married — `[CONFIRM-PATERNAL-AUNT-MOSCOW: placeholder for family network in Russia]` — and as the place that was very far away and cold and had a building on the television sometimes shaped like a cake. I could not picture a tank going there. I pictured the street in front of our building and I tried to picture a tank on it, but I could only picture the trolleybus.

"Why?" I said.

My father said something I did not understand. Not because the words were hard. The words were simple. What he said was: "They're trying to keep things the way they are." The reason I did not understand was that it had not yet occurred to me that things could be any different way.

---

Three days and then it was over, whatever it was. The television went back to its regular face. My father still sat in front of it in the evenings but differently — not watching, exactly, more checking. My mother began a series of quiet conversations with women from our floor, conversations conducted in the stairwell and on the landing with the door to our apartment left open so she could hear if something happened inside.

The something that happened inside was me, reading my books, or pushing the one-wheeled bus across the carpet. Nothing happened.

What happened outside: on September first, Uzbekistan declared itself a country.

I will tell you how I found this out. I found it out because the morning of September first I went down to the courtyard and Dimka was there already and Dimka said, "We're independent." He said it the way a seven-year-old says something he has overheard and not fully processed — with confidence about the word and uncertainty about its meaning. "We're independent," he said, and bounced his tennis ball once against the wall of block fourteen.

I said what does that mean.

He said it means we're our own country now.

I thought about this. "Were we not our own country before?"

Dimka shrugged. He was two years older than me and the fact that he did not know troubled me more than the news itself.

We bounced the ball for a while. The day was the same as the day before. The chinar was the same chinar. Someone on the third floor of block twelve had their window open and the radio was playing, and the radio sounded like the radio always sounded except that at one point a voice came on and said something formal that included words I recognized: Uzbekistan, and *suverenitet*, and a date.

I did not know what *suverenitet* meant. I would not know for another year and then only because a teacher would write it on the board.

---

The days that followed were the same days but at a slight angle. My mother started doing something at the table with papers — envelopes and stamps and her careful handwriting in the blue ink she used for important things. My father came home later. Not by much: fifteen minutes, twenty. But he had always come home at the same time, with the precision of a man whose day was structured by shift changes, and the fifteen minutes was a new thing.

I asked my mother once what she was writing.

She said letters to some people.

I asked which people.

She said relatives.

I had relatives in Russia and relatives in Tashkent and relatives I had never met whose names were spoken at holidays and whose photographs were on the shelf in the hallway. `[CONFIRM-FAMILY-GEOGRAPHY: father's family in Russia? mother's in Tashkent?]` The letters, I understood, were going to the Russia ones. I understood this because she sealed them carefully and wrote addresses that were long and had numbers I associated with train tickets.

---

Our neighbor Galina Ivanovna Sorokina `[CONFIRM-NEIGHBOR: a placeholder composite Russian-speaking neighbor — female, approximately 50, alone or with husband]` knocked on our door in the second week of September. She had a cake, a proper cake with icing, which was not the season for cakes but which she brought anyway, and she sat at our kitchen table and ate a slice and my mother poured tea and they talked for a long time in the way adults talk when a child is present and they are saying certain things and not saying others.

I sat on my stool by the window and listened, which was something I had learned to do without appearing to do it.

What Galina Ivanovna was not saying: that she was leaving. What she was saying, instead, was that her nephew in Novosibirsk had a good apartment, a two-room, and that the factory where he worked was taking people with her husband's profile `[CONFIRM-HUSBAND-CONFIRM: does she have a husband? keep flexible]`, and that it was getting cold earlier this year, she felt, didn't my mother feel it, the cold was coming earlier.

My mother agreed that the cold was coming earlier.

They ate the cake. Galina Ivanovna left. My mother sat for a moment with her hands flat on the table. Then she got up and began doing the dishes, and the sound of dishes was the sound of the afternoon resuming.

Three weeks later I passed Galina Ivanovna's door and it had a different smell. Not absence exactly — a kind of settling, the smell of a space where the daily habits have stopped.

I did not understand what I was smelling. I know now it was the first departure. Not the last. But the first is the one you remember longest, because it is the first time you understand that what is here can become what is not here.

---

In October my father brought home a newspaper — not Pravda Vostoka but something I had not seen before, folded differently, with a different color at the top. He read it at the kitchen table with a quality of attention I had never seen him give a newspaper. Not the way he read the regular paper, which was methodical, from front to back, the way you eat a meal. This was the way you read something you don't want to put down but also can't quite believe.

My mother looked at it once over his shoulder and went back to the stove.

I asked what the new newspaper was.

My father said it was from Moscow. He said it was a different kind of newspaper.

I asked if it said things the other newspaper didn't say.

He looked at me for a moment. "Sometimes," he said.

This, I now understand, was the first time I had an inkling that information was something that came from somewhere. That the Pravda was Pravda because someone made it Pravda. That there was such a thing as a different newspaper. I could not have articulated any of this. I felt it as the particular alertness a six-year-old feels when an adult answers a simple question as though it is a complicated one.

---

The flag changed. I did not see it happen — it happened at a public building I passed infrequently with my father, and one afternoon the flag on the building was different: lighter, with a crescent. I looked at it for a long time.

The previous flag was also something I had looked at without understanding. A red rectangle with a mark in the corner. I had seen it so many times it had stopped being information. The new flag was new, which made it briefly very visible.

I asked my father who changed the flag.

He said the government.

I asked why.

He said because the country was different now.

I turned this over. I said: "Is our house different?"

He looked down at me from the height fathers have over six-year-olds. Something moved in his face that I would spend years learning to read and never fully learn. "No," he said. "Our house is the same."

I believed him. I was six and I had no reason not to believe him.

---

What did change at our house: the shelf with the Soviet encyclopedias. My father had a set — twelve volumes, dark green spines, the kind that came with a subscription and arrived over several years in brown cardboard. He moved them from the main room to the storage space off the hallway, not all at once but one by one, over the course of several weeks, and replaced them with different books from a box I had not seen before, books with thinner spines and different cover colors. I watched this without asking about it. Some understanding had already settled in me by November: there were questions worth asking and questions better left as observations.

My mother's approach to the changed country was more practical. She had, at some point in October, begun a notebook — a regular school notebook, blue cover, the kind I used for my letters — in which she wrote things in her careful hand. I saw it once when she had left it on the table and I read some of it before she came back. It was not a diary. It was more like a list of possibilities, with numbers next to each one. The numbers might have been money. I did not know enough about money yet to be sure.

She put the notebook away when she came back and we both pretended I had not been reading it.

---

School began in September, as it always had. The school was the same school, the same walk, the same smell of chalk and the boiled-cabbage lunch and the linoleum floors that had been waxed all summer. My teacher that year was a woman named Irina Mikhailovna `[CONFIRM-TEACHER: placeholder, fine to leave composite for now]`, small, quick, with a sharpness about her that I associated with competence. She had been my teacher the year before and would be again the year after.

What was different: in the first week of school, Irina Mikhailovna introduced us to a subject called Uzbek language, which would now be part of our week. She announced it without editorial. She wrote the Uzbek alphabet on the board.

I had seen Uzbek writing before, on street signs and on the packages of certain foods, but always alongside Russian, and it had always seemed to me like decoration — not information, since information was the Russian line underneath. Now it was on the schoolboard, alone, being taught.

Irina Mikhailovna pronounced the Uzbek sounds carefully, as though she herself had practiced them. We repeated after her. The sounds were not entirely foreign — some were Russian sounds, or nearly — but some were sounds the Russian alphabet didn't have, made in parts of the mouth I had not previously used for language.

I looked at Dimka, who was in my class, across the room. He made a small face that was not quite a grimace: the face of a person encountering something unexpected and declining, for now, to have an opinion about it.

---

By December the cold had settled in for the year. The central heating in the building was working — this was not always certain, and the winter of the year before had had two weeks without it — and our apartment was warm, which made it feel, in the evenings at least, as though nothing had changed.

My father's sister in Moscow had written back. I know this because my father read the letter twice, and then read it again, and then folded it and put it in the drawer where he kept things he wasn't ready to discard. My mother, who had been watching, went to the kitchen and began making tea. The tea was her way of giving him a moment he could have alone even though she was there.

What the letter said I did not know. I know only that when my father came to dinner that evening he ate less than usual and said almost nothing. When my mother asked what his sister had written he said, "She's fine. She says come visit." He said it the way people say something that is simultaneously true and insufficient.

My mother said maybe in summer.

My father said yes, maybe in summer.

Summer was far away. The winter was what was available.

---

Late December. A neighbor from two buildings over — a man named Vitaly Konstantinovich, `[CONFIRM-NEIGHBOR-VITALY: composite placeholder, Russian-speaker, roughly the father's age, with family]` who my father sometimes played dominos with on Saturday evenings, whose wife sometimes gave my mother pickled tomatoes in the autumn from a dacha they had somewhere outside the city — came to say goodbye. Not for a visit. For good.

He and his wife and their daughter, who was maybe twelve, stood in our hallway in their coats. They were already in their coats, which meant they were going somewhere after this. His wife had the particular expression of a woman who has made the decision and is now executing it, and who has put the grief somewhere to be retrieved later. Their daughter was looking at the floor.

I was standing in the doorway to the main room, watching.

The adults shook hands. My mother had baked something — when had she baked? She had found the time to bake — and she pressed it on Vitaly Konstantinovich's wife, who received it with a care that was slightly too formal, the care you extend to a gift when you are already calculating what you can carry.

"Kuzbass," my father said. It was not a question. He knew where they were going.

Vitaly Konstantinovich said yes. An uncle, he said. Work.

My father nodded.

They stood for a moment. Two men who had played dominos on Saturday evenings, in the courtyard in summer and in someone's apartment in winter. A whole decade of Saturday evenings. The something that passes between men who have been neighbors and will not be neighbors anymore was in the hallway with us but I didn't have a name for it. I had never seen it before.

They left. The door closed. My father stood looking at the door for a moment. Then he went to the kitchen and I heard him take down the bottle of vodka he kept on the high shelf, the one he only reached for particular occasions, and I heard him pour one glass and drink it standing at the counter, and then I heard the bottle go back.

My mother did not come out of the kitchen. She knew the sound.

---

I was six, nearly seven. I understood the following: that some families were leaving and some were not. That the families who were leaving were mostly the families who looked like us, spoke like us, had names like ours. That the families who were not leaving were us. That there was a reason for this distinction, which the adults had but which they had not given me yet.

I did not understand the following: why. What we were staying for, what we were hoping, what my father calculated at the kitchen table after my mother was asleep. What my mother wrote in the notebook with the blue cover. What the word *natsionalnost* was doing in the conversations I overheard — what it meant that it had suddenly become a word in use, when before it had simply been the answer to a question on a form.

What I understood was the texture: the specific quality of the stairwell after Galina Ivanovna left. The way Vitaly Konstantinovich had taken off his hat and then put it back on again in the hallway, not from cold but from habit, the motion of a man who was already on his way. The daughter looking at the floor.

I went back to my room. I found my one-wheeled bus and set it on the carpet, but it listed when I pushed it and I had to hold it upright with my finger. I had been meaning to fix the wheel for months. I had the wheel — I knew where I had put it, in the box under the bed. I just needed a moment when fixing it seemed worth the attention.

That night I got the wheel and the bus and tried to fit the wheel back on. It wouldn't hold. The metal pin that was supposed to keep it in place had bent and I didn't have a way to straighten it. I put the bus back and went to sleep.

The wheel was still in my hand when I woke up.

---

On New Year's Eve my parents stayed up and I fell asleep in the middle of the television counting-down and woke to the sound of them clinking glasses in the kitchen, their voices low, careful, the particular volume of people who are celebrating in the presence of something they are not celebrating. The radio played, faintly, the New Year song that it always played, the one about the blue fire and the frost — `[CONFIRM-NEW-YEAR-SONG: *V lesu rodilas yolochka* or the Zemfira-era variant — for 1991/92, *V lesu* is correct, or the famous New Year *Goluboi Ogonek* program]` — and I lay in the dark and listened to my parents in the kitchen and thought nothing in particular except that it was the new year and that it was the year of something, but I didn't know what.

It was 1992. The new country was three months old. The rubles in my father's wallet were still rubles but they were lighter ones, somehow — not in weight, which is the same in paper, but in the way he held them, which had changed.

Outside the chinar tree was bare. The ants from the summer before were somewhere I couldn't locate. The crack in the asphalt between the buildings was the same crack, but covered now with a thin skin of ice that by morning would have thickened.

Happy New Year, said the television, and my parents in the kitchen said it back.

I said it to no one, in the dark.
