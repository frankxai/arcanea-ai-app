# THE RUSSIAN-SPEAKER

## Chapter 4: Hotel Work and the World

*Yunusabad, Tashkent — 1996–1998* `[CONFIRM-DISTRICT: by mid-90s, protagonist's family is still in Yunusabad/Chirchiq area; if they moved before 1996, adjust]`

---

The foreigner arrived the way foreigners arrived in Tashkent in the middle of the decade: through the Hotel Uzbekistan's revolving door, with a single good bag, and the particular quality of unawareness that money produces in a person who has always had it.

I was not there for this. I am telling you how it happened from the account I was given by my uncle, `[CONFIRM-UNCLE: my father's brother or mother's brother — composite; works at or near Hotel Uzbekistan in administrative capacity, not front-desk]` who at that time was working in some administrative capacity at the hotel — not front desk, something behind front desk, something with keys and schedules and the management of things that guests required and did not see. My uncle was not a romantic man. He described the foreigner the way you describe a weather pattern: as a fact of the environment that had arrived and would eventually leave.

What I remember of that account, filtered through twelve years of age: the man was German, or possibly Dutch `[CONFIRM-FOREIGNER-NATIONALITY: uncle may have specified; this is a placeholder]`, and he was there on business, which in 1996 meant something in the construction or commodity sector, because those were the sectors that made business sense in Tashkent in 1996. He had shoes that my uncle had felt compelled to mention, which told me something about the shoes. He had a briefcase that opened across the top rather than at the side, which my uncle had also mentioned, which told me something about what impressed my uncle.

I could not have told you then why this account lodged in me the way it did. The foreigner was real to me in the way that things glimpsed through the door of another room are real: entirely, with the force of something withheld.

---

The Hotel Uzbekistan was not the first large building I had been inside. The school was large. The factory district had large buildings. My city had Khrushchyovkas stacked nine stories high. But the hotel was large in a different register: the lobby ceiling had a height that was not about function but about statement, and the statement was that the world outside had different ceilings than the world I lived in, and that whoever had built this building knew it.

I had been inside the lobby once before this, briefly, when my uncle had taken me along on an errand — something involving papers, a delivery or a collection. I had stood near the entrance while he went to an office and I had looked at the chandelier, which was a chandelier of the specific Soviet grandeur that announces itself without apology, and at the floor, which was a pattern I recognized as intended to be looked at, and at the man behind the reception desk, who was wearing a jacket in a shade of dark blue that was not the shade of any jacket I had seen anyone's father wear.

The man behind the reception desk was speaking Russian. On the phone. But his Russian had something in it that I registered as different — a crispness, a particular formality — that made it feel like a version of Russian I did not know yet. I would later understand that this was the Russian of people trained to say things precisely, trained to put the sentence in a form that would not require adjustment. Hotel Russian. Service Russian. The Russian that exists to remove inconvenience from the person being addressed.

I did not know any of this at eleven. I only knew that the jacket was the right shade of blue and the Russian was a different shape.

---

English was at this point a subject we had recently started in school. It was taught by a woman named Valentina Prokopivna `[CONFIRM-ENGLISH-TEACHER: composite; could be named differently]` who had learned her English from Russian textbooks and Soviet-era pedagogical methods, which meant that she taught us a language that was functional and also slightly wrong in ways that would take years to identify. Her English was technically correct and inflected with the particular accent of someone who had learned pronunciation from transcription rather than from hearing, and who had never been required to use the language in a situation where the other person could not wait.

I was good at it immediately. Not because I had a gift for languages — I do not think I had a gift, exactly, so much as I had the habit of listening to the register of things. The habit of knowing when someone's Russian was hotel Russian and when it was kitchen Russian. The habit of sorting languages into their textures and fitting myself into the available gaps. English had textures too. I could hear them even through Valentina Prokopivna's careful transcription.

I brought my first English vocabulary list home and showed it to my father. He looked at it for a moment. He said he had studied German `[CONFIRM-FATHER-GERMAN: per character bible, father likely had some language; German is a common Soviet-era technical language choice]` in school and the university, and that languages were useful. He said it without enthusiasm, but also without irony. Language was useful: this was a fact of the world, like the fact that tools had to be maintained.

My mother asked what the words meant. I read them to her in Russian. She listened. She said: "Good. Keep going."

---

My uncle had been at the Hotel Uzbekistan for several years by this point. `[CONFIRM-UNCLE-HOTEL-TENURE: how long? author to confirm family connection to hotel sector — this is a key structural element]` He had begun, as near as I could work out from family conversation, as something minor — a position that involved logistics or maintenance or both, the position that involves being reliable with things that break or go missing, which is the position that in a Soviet organization led to nothing and in a post-Soviet international hotel could lead somewhere. He had, apparently, led somewhere. He was now part of the staff that interacted with the operations team — Uzbek managers working with foreign hotel consultants, some form of joint management structure that I didn't understand and wasn't told directly.

I heard about this through my mother, who heard about it from my uncle's wife, who told it with a particular tone: pride mixed with the specific caution that comes from admiring something slightly out of reach. My uncle made more money than my father. This was not stated, but it was understood in the arithmetic of who had what in the family, in who could afford what at which occasion.

I did not resent this. I was twelve and resentment of that kind requires a political education I hadn't yet had. I observed it the way I observed everything: as a datum in the landscape.

What I felt, if I am being precise, was the particular form of wanting that is also a map. I wanted to know what it felt like inside that jacket the shade of dark blue. I wanted to know what language hotel Russian was in English. I wanted to know what the foreigner with the good shoes had been there to do, and whether the place he was coming from was a place that could be reached from where I was standing.

My father took me to the hotel once more in 1997, this time to meet my uncle for a family dinner at a restaurant nearby `[CONFIRM-OCCASION: birthday? end of year? keep flexible]`. We walked through the lobby on the way out. The lobby had the same chandelier, the same floor pattern, the same dark blue jacket at the desk. A different man wearing it — or possibly the same man, I couldn't be sure.

Near the entrance, waiting — for a taxi, probably, or for someone arriving — there were two men who were not Uzbek and were not Russian-speaking. They were speaking to each other in something I knew was English because I recognized the shape of it from school, though at this speed and this register I understood approximately nothing. One of them was older, with a briefcase; one was younger, with a laptop bag — `[CONFIRM-LAPTOP-1997: laptops existed in Tashkent in 1997 among international business travelers; the ThinkPad era; this is historically plausible]` — and they were talking in the unhurried manner of people waiting for something that would arrive on their schedule rather than anyone else's.

I watched them.

The older one said something I caught partially: *not what we agreed* — I was nearly sure of those words — *not what we agreed* — and the younger one made a face and said something I couldn't follow and the older one laughed once, a short laugh, and that was all before my father moved me toward the exit.

I replayed the four words I had caught for weeks afterward. *Not what we agreed.* Formal, specific, the words of someone who had an agreement with someone and was discussing its status. The words of someone in the middle of something that mattered and was happening right here, at the same carpet I was standing on.

I had not understood the hotel as a place where things happened. I had understood it as a lobby. But it was also a place where things happened, and the things that happened there were not the things that happened in the Chirchiq district, and you could learn English and be the person who was in the room when those things happened, or you could not learn English and not be.

I was twelve. I understood very little. But I understood that.

---

At school, by 1997, English was the subject I looked forward to. This required some management of the situation, because looking forward to a subject was not entirely acceptable as a social position among boys of twelve. What you did instead was be good at it without appearing to try, which I managed moderately well, and find ways to do additional work without it being noticed.

The additional work involved the dictionary that my father had bought, not a new dictionary — new English dictionaries were expensive and unavailable at the state bookshop, which had mostly Soviet-era remnants — but a secondhand one that had belonged to a family now in Moscow and which my father had acquired through the informal book exchange that operated at Alaysky bazaar. The dictionary was from 1978. This was fine. The words had not changed.

I also acquired, through a school friend whose older sister had been to an English-language course at some institute in Tashkent `[CONFIRM-ENGLISH-INSTITUTE: in late 1990s Tashkent, British Council-affiliated institutes and American-backed language centers were opening; this is historically plausible]`, a cassette tape with English dialogues. The dialogues were from a Soviet-era textbook and they were formally correct and also staged in a way that bore no resemblance to how I imagined English people actually talked. But I listened to them on our tape player anyway, in the evenings, with the volume low, repeating the sentences into the air of my room.

*This is the market.* *Where is the post office?* *I would like to buy a newspaper.*

Sentences for a world of clear objectives and achievable transactions. I listened to them and thought about *not what we agreed* and tried to hear the distance between the two.

---

My father's wage had, by 1997, reached a plateau that was not a comfortable plateau. The word I heard most often in the kitchen when they thought I was asleep was *stavka* — the rate, the salary rate — and the rate was the rate, and the rate was not moving. He worked. He came home. He fixed things that needed fixing in the apartment, which was his way of directing competence toward a problem he could solve. A hinge on the kitchen cabinet. The bathroom tile that had lifted. The tap in the hallway sink. `[CONFIRM-APARTMENT-MAINTENANCE: period detail; correct for a competent Soviet-trained engineer maintaining aging Khrushchyovka infrastructure]`

The things he was fixing were getting older. He was getting older. I was getting older. My sister was five, six, seven — small and specific, with opinions about things that made my mother laugh in the way people laugh when something manages to be both an inconvenience and a joy.

I do not want to make this into hardship it wasn't. But I was aware, in the way a twelve-year-old who has watched two years of departure and reduction becomes aware, that the horizon my father had been trained for — the factory, the rate, the Soviet logic of stable competence — was not the horizon I was going to get. Not because of anything he had done. Because the factory had been built for a different country.

The man at the front desk of the Hotel Uzbekistan was also from a Tashkent family. Possibly a Russian-speaking family. He had a jacket the right shade of blue and a rate that was a different kind of rate.

---

My uncle invited me to come to the hotel one afternoon in the summer of 1997. Not for an occasion — just to see it. He said he had time; he said it might be useful. `[CONFIRM-UNCLE-INVITATION: did this happen? was there a relative who gave access to the hotel world? This is the chapter's structural anchor — author to confirm or replace with actual circumstances of first hotel exposure]`

He walked me through the service entrance, which was not the revolving door but a side entrance for staff, and this was its own kind of introduction: the back of the hotel was nothing like the front. The hallway was linoleum, not the patterned stone of the lobby; the walls were painted the green of utility; the smell was laundry chemicals and something savory from the kitchen below. Staff in different versions of the uniform moved through with the particular economy of people who know where everything is.

My uncle moved through it the same way. He showed me the linen storage, briefly, and the schedule board, and the room where the keys were kept when rooms were being serviced. He showed me these things with the practicality of a man showing you a machine: here is how the pieces relate.

Then he walked me through the door from the staff side into the lobby, and the lobby was the lobby again — the chandelier, the floor, the desk — but now I had seen the seam. I knew what was on the other side of the wall the guests never saw. I knew where the linen came from and who cleaned the keys. The lobby had a system, and the system was work, and the work was done by people from Tashkent who had learned how to make the gap between the two sides invisible.

I thought: that is a skill. Making the gap invisible is a skill.

---

In the autumn of 1997 my uncle told me, at a Sunday family meal, that the hotel would be hiring for the holiday season. He did not say this to me specifically. He said it to the table. My father nodded. My mother passed the bread. I filed it.

In December a cousin of mine `[CONFIRM-COUSIN: older cousin, female or male, who gets holiday work at hotel — author to confirm or adjust]` — older than me by five years, with enough English to manage — began working at the hotel for the holiday month. She came back from her first week with stories that had the quality of dispatches from a neighboring country: what the foreign guests asked for, what they tipped, what things they found strange that were not strange, what things were invisible to them that were not invisible. She talked about the English — not the grammar, which was classroom English — but the way guests used language: short, expectation-laden, polite on the surface and precise underneath.

I listened the way I listened to everything.

What she said, at one point, that I have thought about since: "They don't see us the way we see them." She said it without resentment — with a kind of professional clarity. She said: "They see a function. If you do the function well, you're invisible. That's the job."

I asked her if that bothered her.

She looked at me. She was seventeen `[CONFIRM-COUSIN-AGE: five years older than narrator's 12 = 17; placeholder]`, and she had the expression of someone explaining the obvious to someone too young to have had the experience. "It's how it works," she said. "You figure out what they need before they ask for it. Then they're happy and you're doing your job."

I thought about this for a long time. I did not think: this is sad. I thought: this is a skill too. Knowing what someone needs before they ask for it. Reading the room. Managing the gap.

---

By 1998 I was thirteen and the city had changed in the specific way that cities change when you grow old enough to walk through them alone. I walked to school alone. I walked home from friends' houses alone. I walked to the bazaar on errands for my mother. Tashkent had a texture at the level of walking that I had not had access to at the level of being carried or supervised.

What I walked through: the wide Soviet-planned streets of Yunusabad, lined with trees that were green in summer and bare in winter and never quite as tall as they would have been if anyone had watered them properly. The newer construction on certain blocks — the white marble facades of government buildings that Karimov had ordered resurfaced, so that walking into the administrative center of Tashkent was walking into a city that had decided to look different from the inside out. The kiosks at every corner — metal boxes selling cigarettes and beer and phone cards and candy from Turkey, and sometimes a Chinese product in unfamiliar packaging, and sometimes a pirated cassette tape. The Gazelle minibuses at the main road, white, overcrowded, heading toward different districts in a system that you learned by riding it and made no sense on a map.

I walked through this city and I knew it. I knew its rhythms: what time the bread was freshest from which bakery, which policeman at which intersection was more likely to ask for your papers and which one never bothered children, which streets flooded in March and were passable by April, which shortcut cut twenty minutes off the walk to school and which one added fifteen. This knowledge was not the knowledge I was building in English class, or in mathematics, or in the school history that was now a different history than it had been — a new Uzbek history, with Tamerlane at the center, built differently from the Soviet history we had started with. `[CONFIRM-SCHOOL-HISTORY: curriculum changes in Uzbek schools in the 1990s — author may remember which historical figures were newly prominent]`

It was the knowledge of a person who belonged here. I belonged here the way you belong to a place that has shaped you before you could choose it.

I also knew, by 1998, that belonging was not the same as being claimed.

---

In late 1998 something changed in the city's air. I do not know how to say this with more precision than that, because what I remember is not an event but an atmosphere. My father, over the autumn and early winter, became quieter. Not quieter at home than outside — he had always been quiet, it was his nature — but quieter in a different direction. The quietness of a man listening for something.

I noticed that certain conversations in the kitchen stopped when I came in. Not conversations about money — those had their own code by now, which I had half-learned and which involved the names of things rather than their actual subjects. Different conversations. About people in the neighborhood, or about something my father had heard at the factory. The conversations had names in them that I didn't know, and the names were followed by pauses.

My mother, when I asked her once what was going on — not the specific conversations but the general atmospheric shift — said: "Things are a little complicated now." She said it in the middle of doing something else, so that the sentence was distributed across an action, which was her way of giving an answer that was technically complete.

I had learned by thirteen that *complicated* in my mother's language meant something real was happening that she was protecting me from knowing, and that the protection was not because she thought I couldn't handle it but because there was nothing I could do with the information except worry.

I did not ask again.

---

My English class had moved to a new textbook that autumn — a British one, actually printed in England, which had arrived through some channel connected to the new teaching materials program `[CONFIRM-TEXTBOOK: in late 1990s Tashkent, British Council influence on English education was real; placeholder is historically plausible]`. It was a different textbook from the Soviet ones. The dialogues were set in England, not in a generic modern city, and the England of the dialogues had a pub and a bus and a football match and a park where people ate their lunch and talked about holidays.

I read through the dialogue sections with a attention I was careful not to display. The England of the textbook was not a real place, or not a complete one — it was a collection of vocabulary-building scenarios, full of useful phrases and stripped of complication. But the England behind the textbook England was real, I understood: a country where people ate lunch in parks and took buses and talked about holidays in the ordinary way, the way we talked about things in the ordinary way here, and the ordinary way was different, and the difference was the gap I had been trying to measure since the lobby.

My English teacher that year — Valentina Prokopivna had been replaced, I did not know why, by a younger woman named Aisha Rakhmatullayevna `[CONFIRM-REPLACEMENT-TEACHER: placeholder; Uzbek name, younger, represents the generation more comfortable with post-Soviet curricula]` — asked us one class to write a paragraph in English about what we wanted to do when we grew up.

I wrote: I want to work with foreign people. I want to help them to understand Tashkent and Uzbekistan. I want to use English every day.

I wrote this without particular boldness. It was a school exercise. But when I read it back I recognized something in it that was not just the exercise — a direction, stated in a foreign language, in a foreign syntax, imperfect and honest.

Aisha Rakhmatullayevna gave me a 5. She wrote in the margin, in Russian: *Interesting goal.*

By December of 1998 the city felt different in a way that I could not have explained to anyone who wasn't living in it. The policeman at the intersection near our school who had never bothered anyone had started asking for papers. Not mine — I was thirteen, a student, what papers — but men who walked past. Men of a certain age, a certain appearance. Men who looked like they might be the kind of person the new vocabulary in the newspaper was describing, a vocabulary I had begun to pick up from my father's occasional reading and from the fragments of adult conversation that reached me.

I did not understand the full shape of what was coming. I would not understand it until February. But I understood this much: the city was tightening in a way that you felt in the way people moved through it, in who was present at which corner and who was absent, in the specific atmospheric quality of a city that has decided to be afraid of something and has not yet decided what.

I was thirteen. I was working on my English. I was watching my uncle's hotel from the distance of someone who had stood in its lobby and seen the seam. I was thinking about what my cousin had said — *they don't see us the way we see them* — and whether that was a limitation or, in some way I couldn't articulate yet, a skill.

My father's factory was still there. The rate was the rate. The machines were the same machines he had worked for twelve years and they would work for twelve more, diminished, maintained past the point where maintenance was sensible, running because stopping was not yet a decision anyone had made.

My mother was in the kitchen, and the tutoring had become real enough to have a schedule, and the red notebook had more pages filled in it than the blue one. Her adaptability was the family's actual engine; I was only beginning to understand this. `[CONFIRM-MOTHER-WORK: by 1998, if tutoring was real, it would be established; adjust if mother's actual work was different]`

My sister was six. She had started school. She had Uzbek language classes, and apparently, based on what she told me at dinner, she had a Uzbek language teacher who waited when you gave the wrong answer. She said this with the tone of someone filing a bureaucratic complaint.

I told her to just try again. She said she knew. She said the teacher wasn't mean about it.

I said: good.

Outside the city was tightening. The winter was coming. The new year was coming, and the century was almost done, and something in the air of Tashkent was not what it had been in the spring, though it would take until February to have a name.

I did not know what was coming. No one in our apartment did. We ate dinner, the four of us, under the kitchen light. My father read his newspaper. My mother washed the dishes. My sister told me about the Uzbek words she had learned that day, ticking them off on her fingers: *kitob*, book; *maktab*, school; *do'st*, friend.

I listened. They were not hard words. I knew them all.
