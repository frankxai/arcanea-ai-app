## Chapter 8: Moscow

Moscow in early 2001 was having a conversation with itself about what it was going to be. The conversation had been going on since 1991 and it was not finished, but in the spring of that year it had taken on a different tone: more certain of some things, less ashamed of others. The new president had been in office for a year. His face was on the television in a way that was not quite the old way — not the Soviet way, not Karimov's way, which had a different grammar, the grammar of a man who needed to remind you that he was there — but something its own, which I did not have a word for until later. Competence performed publicly. The performance of someone who had decided, on behalf of the country, what the answer was.

I noticed this on the television in the apartment of Ruslan's friend `[CONFIRM-MOSCOW-CONNECTION: Who received the protagonist in Moscow? A friend from Petersburg? `[CONFIRM-MOSCOW-MOVE]` What follows is my best reconstruction of what I knew when I arrived in Moscow and what I learned after, in the order of learning. The order matters because in retrospect the knowledge has a different shape than the learning had. In retrospect: Moscow was where I developed the specific skill of being legible to multiple parties at once, which would later be useful in ways I could not have predicted. At the time: Moscow was where I was cold and broke and learning, with the particular alertness of a person who has run out of room for mistakes.

The city hit you in the first week. Not metaphorically. The metro at rush hour is physical. Eight in the morning at Komsomolskaya station — where the Kazansky Vokzal and Yaroslavsky Vokzal terminals feed into the circle line, where the trains from Tashkent and the trains from Yaroslavl and the trains from Kazan and all their human cargo pour into a single interchange — the press of bodies is something you manage with your whole body or you don't manage at all. Petersburg's metro was orderly, purposeful, cold. Moscow's metro was all of those things at twice the amplitude.

I learned the Moscow metro the way I had learned Petersburg's: by errors that were educational. The wrong direction. The missed transfer. The station closed for repairs at an hour that was not posted. But Moscow's metro was larger — 150 stations, the maps plastered in every car — and learning it was not a week's work but a month's work, and the month was also the month of finding work and finding a longer-term room and finding, as one always has to find in a new city, the particular radius within which daily life becomes sustainable.

---

I found a room by mid-May in the Tekstilshchiki district, south-east Moscow, near the ring road. The room was in an apartment that had been divided into four separate rentable rooms by a landlord who had, in the late nineties, converted his two-bedroom apartment into a revenue source and left the city for somewhere quieter. The managing of the apartment had been handed to his niece, a woman of about thirty named Irina Arkadyevna, who collected rent in cash on the first of each month and brought her own problems to the arrangement.

The chief problem was that Irina Arkadyevna had opinions about who should rent rooms in her uncle's apartment, and her opinions were calibrated against a standard she did not explicitly state but demonstrated clearly. She looked at my Uzbek passport for a long time, longer than necessary. She asked about my propiska, which I had arranged through a different channel in Moscow — a different woman in a different district, different price, same rubber stamp, same understanding. She asked where I worked.

I told her I was looking for work.

She looked at me in the way of a woman who is deciding whether a problem is worth the money.

"Two hundred rubles a month," she said. The rate for the room was, I had been told by the man who'd referred me, a hundred and fifty.

I said I had been told a hundred and fifty.

She said she had recalculated.

I paid two hundred.

The room was seven square meters. It had a window that looked at the wall of the adjacent building, a single bed, a shelf bolted to the wall, and a radiator that worked. The bathroom was shared with the other three rooms. One of the other tenants was a man from Krasnodar who worked in construction and was never there. One was a young woman from Voronezh whose hours were irregular in a way that Irina Arkadyevna seemed to accept without comment. One was an older man named Afanasyev who had lived in the apartment since before the division and retained — by what legal right was unclear — the larger of the two original bedrooms and a specific arrangement regarding the refrigerator that was not negotiable.

Afanasyev and I coexisted without difficulty. He was retired and spent his mornings watching television programs about animals and his afternoons doing something in his room that involved low radio and occasional coughing. He addressed me as *molodoy chelovek* — young man — and the title was neither warm nor cold, simply accurate.

Once, early in the summer, he stopped me in the hallway and said: "You're from Central Asia."

I said I was from Tashkent.

He nodded. He was from Sverdlovsk originally, he said, and had been in Moscow for thirty years, and it was a different city now than it had been but some things were the same.

"The same things," he said, "are the things you can't change."

I asked what those were.

He considered it. "The weather," he said. "The distance." He went into his room.

The conversation was not wise in the way of the great Russian literary tradition of old men who are unexpectedly wise. It was the conversation of a retired man from Sverdlovsk who had not talked to many people that day. I have included it because Afanasyev was the person in Moscow who talked to me most simply, without agenda, without the specific management of expectation that most conversations in Moscow involved. This was its own kind of warmth.

The documents check.

I will describe it plainly.

I was at Kazansky Vokzal, which is the Moscow terminus for trains from Tashkent and Samarkand and Dushanbe and Almaty. I was there not because I was traveling but because a man in Dima's circle who knew someone who knew someone had told me that day work was available at the station — loading freight, no registration required, pay in cash by the hour.

I had been there an hour when two officers stopped me at the main hall entrance.

They were militsiya — the regular police, not the special forces, not the OMON in their riot gear. Young men. One had a mustache in the early stages of itself. They were doing what militsiya at transport hubs did in 2001: looking for faces that fell into the category of *kavkazskaya vneshnost* — Caucasian appearance — which in practice meant any face that was not ethnic Slavic, any face from the arc of territories that had recently become troubled: Chechnya, Dagestan, the Central Asian republics, Azerbaijan. The category was not scientific. It was approximately the category of faces that looked like they came from the places that were currently appearing in the news about terrorism, about drugs, about illegal migration.

The first officer asked for my documents.

I gave them. Uzbek passport. Moscow registration stamp.

He looked at the stamp. He looked at the passport. He looked at my face, not rudely, the way a man looks at a thing he is trying to categorize.

He said something to his partner. I heard the word *uzbek* and after that a phrase I will not write because the word in the middle of it is the one I mentioned in the previous chapter, the word for a wooden stump, the word applied to people from where I was from.

The second officer took the passport. He opened it to the data page. He looked at my photograph. He looked at my face. He handed the passport to the first officer.

There was a standard procedure here. I had been told about it by Dima and by the man who'd suggested the station work. The procedure was: the officer holds your passport, asks where you are going, asks where you are coming from, finds an irregularity — this takes approximately as long as the officer wants it to take — and then suggests that the irregularity could be resolved by a payment of some sum, paid now, in cash, without a receipt.

The first officer began working through the procedure. He found an irregularity in the registration stamp: the date, he said. The date on the registration stamp needed to match the date of arrival in Moscow, and my date of arrival in Moscow, as per my passport, was —

I said the date on the stamp was the date of arrival. I said this calmly.

He looked at the stamp again. He was not reading the stamp. He was deciding whether the performance of reading the stamp needed to go further.

The procedure has a resolution that is either payment or departure. I had been told this. I had been told to have money in a specific pocket for this purpose. I was also sixteen years old `[CONFIRM-AGE]` and I was standing at Kazansky Vokzal with my Uzbek passport and a registration stamp from a woman in a district I had never visited and the work I had come for had not happened yet and might not happen, and the arithmetic running in the back of my mind was: how much, whether I had it, what happened if I did not.

I paid. The amount was not large. The officer pocketed it with the practiced movement of a man for whom this is a standard unit of interaction. He handed back the passport.

"Everything's in order," he said.

"Yes," I said.

I left the station. The day work at the freight bay was still theoretically available but I did not go back in. I took the metro to Tekstilshchiki. I sat on the seven-square-meter bed and ate the bread I had in my bag and looked at the wall opposite the window.

What I felt was not humiliation in the simple sense. It was something with more components. There was anger, yes, and I knew the anger had nowhere to go that would be useful. There was also a recognition: I had been assessed and found to be in a particular category, and the assessment was conducted without reference to what I spoke or where I had grown up or what I knew or what I did not do. The category was a face and a passport origin. My Russian was irrelevant in the encounter with those two officers. I could have quoted Pushkin and it would not have changed the arithmetic.

This is not a discovery that I am claiming as original. Millions of people make this discovery in millions of places about millions of categories. I am simply recording that I made it in October 2001, at Kazansky Vokzal, at sixteen years old, and that I ate bread on a seven-square-meter bed afterward and looked at the wall and did not go back to the station.

I found different work.

---

The work I found was at a storage warehouse near the Avtozavodskaya metro station, owned by a man named Leonid who ran it with his two brothers and one non-brother who did the accounts. The warehouse stored restaurant supplies — commercial-grade kitchen equipment, packaged food, cleaning goods — for the cluster of Georgian restaurants and Uzbek chaikhanas that had opened in the south of the city over the previous decade.

Leonid was from Georgia. He had been in Moscow since 1993, his brothers since the mid-nineties. He spoke Russian with the softened consonants of a Georgian speaker of Russian, which is a distinctive music, and he spoke what he called *po nashem* when I mentioned I was from Tashkent — "in our way," meaning the way of the former Soviet non-Slavic republics, which was not one language but one set of having-been-through-something.

He hired me for stock work and deliveries. The stock work was physical. The deliveries required knowing the city — which streets closed when, which restaurants took deliveries at which entrances, how to navigate the Sadovoye Koltso at midday when the traffic turned the ring road into a parking facility. I learned this. I had the habit, by then, of learning the practical geography of cities as a matter of self-preservation, and Moscow's geography was learnable at the cost of enough wrong turns.

What I did not expect was that the deliveries would teach me Georgian.

Not Georgian as in Georgian the language. Leonid and his brothers spoke Georgian to each other when they were not speaking Russian, and I worked with them for eight months and acquired approximately sixty words of Georgian, which is sixty words more than I had before and is a linguistic curiosity without practical application. What I mean by Georgian as a form of learning is: the deliveries went to Georgian restaurants. The restaurants were run by Georgians and staffed by a mix of Georgians, Russians, Uzbeks, Tajiks, one Vietnamese man named Vinh who was the most competent chef in any kitchen I visited and who communicated primarily through the language of what he was cooking. In these kitchens I was the delivery person from the warehouse, which is a specific role with specific permissions: you are in the kitchen, you are not of the kitchen, you can be talked to or not talked to as the kitchen sees fit.

The kitchens saw fit, often, to talk to me. This surprised me. In the warehouses and construction sites the social geometry was organized around shared labor — you talked to the man carrying the other end of the thing you were carrying, or not at all. In the restaurant kitchens the social geometry was faster, more layered: the hierarchy of the kitchen had its own architecture, the sous-chef's relationship to the prep cook was different from the prep cook's relationship to the dishwasher, and the delivery person from the warehouse existed outside all of it and was therefore, paradoxically, a person to whom anyone could speak.

I was spoken to, over eight months of deliveries, about: marriage difficulties, the price of beef short ribs from the market, Georgian politics, the best route around the Zamoskvorechye traffic, Uzbekistan as remembered and Uzbekistan as imagined by people who had never been, the taste of real lavash versus what could be obtained in Moscow, the taste of real khachapuri versus what could be obtained anywhere outside Georgia, the economics of running a small restaurant in Moscow in 2001 and how this differed from what had been expected.

I listened. I asked occasional questions. I gave the invoices to the owner or the manager. I collected the signatures. I left.

What I was doing, without knowing I was doing it, was learning to inhabit the periphery of many different worlds simultaneously. The warehouse had its world. Each restaurant had its world. The streets between them were their own world. I moved between these worlds carrying invoices and boxes and, increasingly, information — not anything secret, just the ordinary information of a person who is present in many places: the Georgian restaurant on Ordynka was short of the large serving plates it had been ordering; the Uzbek chaikhana in Zamoskvorechye was having a dispute with its landlord about the lease; the Russian kitchen supply depot on the Leninsky Prospekt had changed its billing procedure.

I did not exploit this information. I was not positioned to exploit it. But I noticed it, and I noticed that I was noticing it, and I noticed that the noticing was useful in keeping me employed.

This is one of the origins of what would later be called, by the people at the Embassy who described it to other people as a qualification for hiring me, as *cultural and linguistic literacy*.

There were days when my face was the category and days when it was just a face on a metro at the end of a shift. The category was real. The individuals administering it were people, which is to say they were variable.

By the second winter in Moscow I had established something that I can recognize in retrospect as a position, though at the time it felt like improvisation maintained by practice.

I knew the warehouse work and I knew the delivery routes. I was paid regularly and in cash, which was both more stable than the Petersburg situation and more fragile, because a warehouse owner's decision to reduce staff requires no notice. I had learned to navigate the document check situation through a combination of adequate paperwork, appropriate behavior, and the statistical reality that most checks ended with the passport handed back.

I had also developed, from the deliveries, a set of relationships with the kitchens and their managers that were not friendships but were something between acquaintance and professional connection. When the Georgian restaurant on Ordynka had a busy week and needed someone to help in the back for two days, they asked Leonid if his delivery person was available. Leonid asked me. I was available. I helped in the back for two days and was paid for two days' work.

This happened several times. I was not a kitchen worker. I was not able to cook at the level required. What I was able to do was: follow instructions in Russian, understand quickly what was needed, not make costly mistakes, and be neither obtrusive nor invisible in a working kitchen. These are skills that are difficult to name but that kitchen managers, who work by feel and by the specific cognitive load of a service environment, recognize immediately.

After the second time this happened, the manager of the Ordynka restaurant — a man named Giorgi, careful and exact, who wore his hair in a way that was five years out of date in Moscow and completely appropriate for him — asked me how my English was.

I said it was adequate.

He said adequate how.

I said I had studied at school and could read it well.

He said that was something.

He had an American client he wanted to impress — a businessman who came once a month and brought colleagues. The businessman spoke no Russian. Giorgi's English was functional but not confident, and the presence of a young man who could assist would be, as he put it, *solidnee* — more solid.

I said I could try.

The businessman was named Mr. Collins `[CONFIRM — composite name]`. He was from somewhere in the American Midwest, by the vowels. He was large and affable and ate with the enthusiasm of a man who has recently discovered Georgian cuisine and intends to continue discovering it. He asked questions about the food, about Georgia, about Moscow. Giorgi answered through me.

My English was not, as I had said, more than adequate. But adequate was enough. Mr. Collins asked what the sauce was called; I knew the word *tkemali* from the delivery work and I translated it as "sour plum sauce" and explained roughly where it came from. Mr. Collins was satisfied. He told me my English was excellent.

I thanked him.

He asked where I had learned it.

I said I had studied it in Tashkent.

He looked at me with the particular look of an American encountering a fact about the world that does not fit his map of the world. Not unkind. Just recalibrating.

"Tashkent," he said. "In Uzbekistan."

I said yes.

"And you speak Russian."

I said yes.

"And now Georgian food," he said, gesturing at the menu.

"And now Georgian food," I said.

He laughed. He left a generous tip and told Giorgi I was very useful.

Giorgi told me afterward. He was pleased. He looked at me with the professional appraisal of a man who has found a useful tool and is deciding what other uses it might have.

"Your English will get better," he said. It was not encouragement. It was a prediction based on available data.

He was right. Over the following months I worked occasionally at Ordynka for extra income and for the practice of the American businessmen, the occasional English tourist, the NGO workers who came in a group on Thursdays. My English improved in the specific way that a language improves when it is used under pressure: fast in the domains needed and slow everywhere else. I was good at: menus, directions, reassurances about quality. I was poor at: anything personal, anything abstract, anything requiring the kind of vocabulary that comes from reading in the language rather than speaking it.

I started reading in the language. There was an English-language newspaper available at the kiosk near the metro — it was not cheap, but I bought one a week and read it with a dictionary. The dictionary was from a second-hand bookstall on the Arbat, a Soviet-era English-Russian dictionary with the particular authority of Soviet lexicography, which is to say it was precise and dense and occasionally wrong about connotations. I read what I could and looked up what I could not. This is not an impressive educational practice. It worked.

Inner: by spring 2002, I had been in Russia for two years. I had learned what a city teaches you and what it does not. I had a pocket of savings that was larger than when I had arrived and smaller than I had imagined possible in two years. I had not started university, which was the path I had not taken at every fork, because the arithmetic never permitted it. I had improved at English and at navigating cities and at being useful in commercial environments without being any particular thing — not chef, not waiter, not translator, not administrator, but the lateral element that holds those categories together.

I had learned that in Moscow my being Russian-speaking and my being from Tashkent were two facts that did not produce a sum anyone could locate on a social map. The language I spoke was the language of the city. The city did not claim me by language. The categories it had for me were geographic: I was from outside. From the southeast. From the warm republics, as people sometimes said with a mixture of condescension and something that was not quite envy but was adjacent to it.

I had been in Russia for two years and Russia had not asked me to stay.

This is not a complaint either. A city does not owe you inclusion on the basis of your having arrived in it speaking its language. I understood this intellectually. The understanding did not change the geometry.

The geometry was: I was going back. The place I was going back to was not the place I had left from, which I had known before leaving. You cannot un-know a city the way you have known Moscow and then re-know Tashkent as you knew it at fourteen. What I would find in Tashkent was the place after the bombings, the tightened city, the checkpoints that had become permanent, and whatever had happened to my father's health in two years.

I would also find a city where my face was not the wrong face. In the hierarchy of belonging, there is a stage below not-quite-belonging, and Moscow had shown it to me. In Tashkent I was not fully claimed either, but the specific form of not-being-claimed was one I knew the dimensions of, had grown up inside. It had a different texture from the Moscow version.

---

I worked the last two weeks in May and I told Leonid I was leaving at the end of the month. He said he was sorry to lose me and he said it in the way of a man who means it factually: he was sorry in the sense that it was inconvenient, not in the sense that he was grieved. I respected this. It was the honest register.

Giorgi at the Ordynka restaurant said: "You should stay. Your English is improving."

I said I had family.

He looked at me with the look of a man who understands that family is a larger fact than English.

"Come back," he said. "If you come back."

I said I might.

I did not know whether I would. As it turned out, I did not come back to Moscow. But the things I had learned there came with me, and they were the things that would, several years later, make my application for a different job in a different city more legible to the people reading it.

The skills that travel: languages, the habit of sitting at the periphery of rooms and understanding what they contain, the specific cognitive practice of translating between parties whose interests are not identical and whose understanding of each other is incomplete. These are not glamorous skills. They are the skills of someone who has spent years in the margins of many worlds, finding what can be carried.

The night train from Kazansky Vokzal to Tashkent takes three and a half days in the other direction. `[CONFIRM-RETURN-ROUTE]`

I booked a third-class berth — *platzkart* — because it was what I had booked on the way in and because the money in my pocket was for arriving in Tashkent with something, not for comfort on the way. The berth was an upper shelf in an open car, six bodies to a section, the kind of accommodation that has no privacy except the privacy of strangers choosing not to look at one another.

The man below me was a Tashkentsi too, a Russian-speaker in his fifties, returning from what he described as work in Moscow, in an industry he did not specify. He had a thermos of tea and a paper bag of food and the bearing of a man who had made this journey several times and had organized the making of it into an efficient system. He offered me tea. I accepted.

We talked through the first evening and then intermittently across the steppe, as people do on long train journeys, with the gaps that are not unfriendly but are simply the natural rhythm of two people who do not need to fill all the hours.

He asked what I had been doing in Moscow. I said warehouse work, deliveries. He nodded as though this were a coherent account. I asked what he had been doing. He gave the same vague answer as before and I did not press it.

At some point in the second day, crossing what I thought was the Kazakh steppe, he said: "You're going back."

I said I was.

"Permanently?"

I said I didn't know.

He thought about this for a moment with the look of a man who has thought about it himself and arrived at the answer that applies to his own life, which is a different answer.

"It doesn't matter," he said finally. "Whether you stay or you go, Tashkent stays the same. That's what it does."

I asked if that was good or bad.

He poured more tea from his thermos. "It's Tashkent," he said.

This seemed, to him, to be the answer. I filed it with the other answers I had collected in two years of asking questions that had not quite resolved. Yelchin's *Moscow takes you.* Afanasyev's *the things you can't change.* Giorgi's *come back if you come back.*

The steppe passed. The light changed as we moved south and east. Somewhere in the second night the air through the cracked window began to smell different — drier, warmer, with the particular quality of air that had been heated by a different sun over a different landscape. I had grown up in that air and I did not think about it until it was present again in my face, and then I recognized it the way you recognize something you have been told you would recognize.

The train arrived in Tashkent on the third morning.

I collected my bag. I stood on the platform. I was seventeen years old `[CONFIRM-AGE]` and I had been in Russia for two years and I was standing in the city I had grown up in, which was the same city and was not the same city, depending on which version of myself was doing the measuring.

The air smelled like dust and diesel and, from somewhere, the early apricot stalls of late May.

My mother was at the gate.
