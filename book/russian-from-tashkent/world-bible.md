# World & Period Bible — Russian from Tashkent (1985–2010)

This document is a research-anchored period and place reference for a memoir-fiction novel following a Russian-speaking man born in Uzbekistan in 1985, tracing his life through Soviet collapse, post-independence turmoil, a formative period in Russia, and his return to a changed Tashkent. It is not a plot outline. It is the sensory, material, and institutional reality behind the story — the texture the prose must pass through to feel true. Where specific facts could not be confirmed to archival certainty, they are marked `[CONFIRM]`. The author, as protagonist, is the final arbiter of biographical truth.

---

## 1. Era-by-Era Sensory Swatches

### Soviet UZ 1985–1991: The Last Ordinary Years

The apartment block at the edge of Tashkent or Chirchiq is a five-story Khrushchyovka (хрущёвка), cream plaster gone the color of old paper, balconies stacked with bicycles, drying laundry, and mason jars of pickled cucumbers. The courtyard (двор, dvor) is the actual neighborhood: domino tables under a plane tree (чинара, chinara), grandmothers on benches, a rusted swing set, and the smell of ashphalt baking in July sun that hits 40°C. Bread comes from the государственный магазин (gosmagazin, state shop) — white brick loaves stacked behind a glass counter, two for 18 kopecks. Kefir arrives in glass bottles with foil caps, left by the door in the morning; the empty bottles go back. On Saturday evenings the family gathers for Vremya (Время), the state news broadcast at 9 PM on the single clear channel. Soviet Central Asia still gets Uzbek SSR television as well — "Yoshlar" (youth programming) in Uzbek, which the Russian-speaking household watches without comprehension. The radio plays Alla Pugacheva and, later, the first tentative sounds of perestroika-era softening: Kino, Nautilus Pompilius. Cotton is everywhere in the economy but invisible to children — it is something adults go away to pick in autumn. Winters are mild but genuinely cold, -10°C or below in January, and the central heating (централизованное отопление) either works or it doesn't. When it doesn't, the family burns what it has. The streets are clean in the Soviet way: kept, gray, official. The neighborhood bakery makes non (нон), the round Uzbek flatbread with a stamped center, which the Russian family buys alongside rye. Currency is Soviet rubles, entirely notional — you cannot buy much, but nothing costs much either. The scarcity is in supply, not price. Everything that matters — an apartment, a hospital, a school place — flows through the state, not the market.

### Independence & Collapse 1991–1995: The Floor Drops

August 1991. The coup in Moscow lasts three days and then collapses. The family watches it on television — the tanks around the Russian parliament, Yeltsin on a tank, Gorbachev's pressed face. On September 1, Uzbekistan declares independence. Islam Karimov reads the declaration. For a child aged six, nothing changes visibly that week. The changes come slower and then all at once. The gosmagazin empties. Shelves that held kefir and macaroni now hold air or a single brand of canned goods from a republic no one has heard of. Sugar disappears for months at a time. The introduced coupon system — талоны (talony), rationing coupons — means standing in a queue for two hours to redeem your family's entitlement to one kilogram of butter. By 1992 hyperinflation has eaten the ruble. Then a transitional currency, the Uzbek sum-coupon (сум-купон, sum-kupon), a paper rectangle printed in low colors that looks improvised because it is. The permanent Uzbek som (сўм) arrives in 1994 on July 1, replacing the sum-coupon at a 1:1000 rate that instantly makes everyone feel rich and poor at once. Neighbors begin leaving. Russian-speaking families — engineers, teachers, factory foremen — sell what they can and take the train to Russia. The dvor empties apartment by apartment. Those who leave take their household goods in cardboard boxes tied with rope; those who stay watch from their balconies. The market (bazaar) fills the vacuum the state left: entrepreneurs, returning shuttle traders (челноки, chelnoki) who carry suitcases of Turkish goods back from Istanbul on Uzbek Airlines, men who have somehow acquired electronic goods — cassette players, early VCRs — and sell them on cloth laid on the pavement. The smell of the new market economy is meat in open air, sweet melon, and diesel from the Gazelle minibuses replacing the old Soviet routes.

### The 1990s Middle 1995–1999: Karimov's Managed Calm

The som stabilizes, more or less. Karimov billboards appear — a particular civic style, the president in a blue suit looking into the middle distance, occasionally with a slogan about independence (Mustaqillik — Мустақиллик). State television runs programs about Uzbekistan's ancient heritage: Samarkand, Tamerlane, the Silk Road. There is a story being told about what Uzbekistan is, and it is not Soviet, but it is also not democratic. For a teenager in the mid-1990s, the more immediate texture is: bootleg cassettes sold at Chorsu market, Turkish soaps (soap operas from Turkey are enormously popular — "Yüz Yıllık Destan" [CONFIRM era accuracy], dubbed into Russian), early satellite dishes appearing on the buildings of the well-connected. By 1997 or 1998 the first internet cafes open in central Tashkent — dim rooms with slow machines, $1 per hour, boys playing Doom and writing letters to addresses abroad. The MMM pyramid scheme (МММ) that devastated Russia in 1994 has Uzbek equivalents, informal credit circles and chain schemes that drain savings from families already damaged by the early-decade inflation. Currency exchange is conducted at two rates: the official bank rate and the real rate available from men standing near every major hotel and at Alaysky bazaar. The gap between them is a daily fact of life. A dollar is something you hoard. Education is still nominally free but the informal payments system — cash gifts to teachers for passing grades — is now institutionalized. Russian-speaking teens navigate a school system that has made Uzbek the language of instruction for many subjects; Russian-language schools still exist but their future feels uncertain.

### Post-1999 Crackdown: The Tightening

On February 16, 1999, a series of car bombs detonates in Tashkent — the targets appear to be government buildings, possibly aimed at Karimov himself. The official death toll is sixteen. The true atmosphere in the days after is: fear and then policy. Mosques that had been tolerated or semi-tolerated are closed or put under direct state administration. Young men with beards are stopped at checkpoints. The Hizb ut-Tahrir crackdown intensifies — arrests, prison, disappearances of men the neighborhood knows as unremarkable and devout. Propiska (прописка, the Soviet-era internal registration system that Uzbekistan kept) tightens further: you need your registration in order to hold a job, use a hospital, put a child in school. Leaving the country becomes harder. The exit visa (выездная виза) is not abolished until 2019 [CONFIRM current research]; in this era it is active. Getting one requires paperwork, a reason, and the goodwill of an OVIR (Отдел виз и регистраций) official. For Russian-speakers contemplating leaving for Russia, this bureaucratic weight is one more confirmation that the moment to go is now. The streetscape shows: more police at key intersections, fewer informal vendors near religious sites, occasional checks of men's ID on the metro. The surveillance is not invisible — it is designed to be seen.

### St. Petersburg ~2000: White Nights and Migrant Precarity

A young man arriving in St. Petersburg around 1999–2000 arrives in a city that is simultaneously gorgeous and in decay. The historic center along the Neva and Nevsky Prospekt (Невский проспект) is being renovated selectively — facades painted for the approaching 300th anniversary celebrations (2003); scaffolding everywhere. The city beneath the scaffolding is kommunalki (коммуналки, communal apartments), multiple families sharing a kitchen and bathroom on the same floor, a single telephone in the hallway. A new arrival from Uzbekistan rents a corner of a room from someone in such an apartment — the economics are: a bed, shared kitchen use, $50–80 per month in cash. Marshrutki (маршрутки, fixed-route minibus taxis) have proliferated to fill the gaps in metro coverage. The metro itself — five lines by 2000, deep stations with elaborate Soviet-era mosaics — is the real circulatory system of the city. White nights (белые ночи) in June are real: it does not get dark. Teenagers drink on embankments until 2 AM in daylight. The city is in the first flush of oligarch-era money in its expensive restaurants and new casinos on Nevsky, but that money is not for a Central Asian migrant working construction, hospitality, or markets. The militsiya (милиция, police) conduct document checks on Metro and buses; dark-skinned people, including those from Central Asia, are stopped disproportionately. This is not rumored — it is a daily operational reality for anyone who looks non-Slavic. The city's Russian sounds slightly different from Uzbekistan's Russian: faster, colder in register, using slang he doesn't know yet.

### Moscow ~2001–2002: The New Capital's Machinery

Moscow in 2001 is louder, faster, and richer than anything before it. The MKAD (Московская кольцевая автомобильная дорога, Moscow Ring Road) loops a city that has sprawled into suburbs of new construction. The metro (Московский метрополитен), over 150 stations by this point, runs at 90-second intervals during rush hour — an engineering fact that remains astonishing. A Central Asian man moving through Moscow carries a specific anxiety: the Chechen wars have made "Caucasian appearance" (кавказская внешность) the category police use for spot checks, and Central Asians fall into it by phenotype in the eyes of Russian militsiya. At Казанский вокзал (Kazansky Vokzal, Kazan Station) — the rail terminal for trains from Central Asia — there are specific document check points before and after arrival. The rynki (рынки, outdoor markets) — Cherkizovsky rynok, Luzhniki — are where Central Asian migrant labor is concentrated: stall-keeping, loading, restaurant kitchens. Putin consolidated his presidency after the Kursk submarine disaster (August 2000) and 9/11 has reshuffled the geopolitical deck in ways that will matter enormously for Uzbekistan (the K2 airbase deal is signed in October 2001). Moscow in late 2001 is briefly in a moment of American-Russian warmth that feels strange and temporary. The street food of Moscow: shawarma (шаурма) everywhere now, sold from converted Gazelle vans, Georgian хинкали (khinkali) in stolovaya canteens, cheap Chinese noodle places near markets. A young Uzbekistani man would eat well and cheaply if he found the right stalls.

### Tashkent 2000s Return: The Cotton Capital Reconfigured

Returning to Tashkent after several years in Russia means arriving in a city that has changed its surface while keeping its bones. The Karimov renovation program has produced a Tashkent of new white marble facades on government buildings, broad ceremonial plazas, and monuments to Tamerlane. Behind those facades: the chaikhana (чайхона, teahouse) culture persists entirely unchanged. A proper chaikhana — low wooden platforms (tapchans, тапчаны) beside a water channel under a mulberry tree, green tea in ceramic pots, plov arriving in a kazan (казан, iron cauldron) at noon — is the most stable institution in Uzbekistan. The plov ritual matters: Fergana-style versus Tashkent-style is a real argument, and being able to identify which you are eating signals where you belong. Chorsu bazaar (Чорсу, literally "four waters") in the old city remains one of the largest markets in Central Asia — covered sections for spice, meat, fabric; open sections for electronics, secondhand clothes. Alaysky bazaar (Алайский базар) on the city's edge serves a slightly different clientele, more wholesale, more Russians. The Tashkent metro — opened 1977, Soviet-era stations with Uzbek geometric tilework, one of the best-preserved Soviet metro systems in the world — still runs. Photography on the metro is illegal, for security reasons. Taxis are negotiated on the street: you hold out your hand, a private driver (gyspy cab, частник, chastnik) stops, you negotiate a fare before getting in. There are no meters. Hiring a yellow official taxi is unusual and more expensive. Mobile phones have arrived: by 2003 the GSM networks (Beeline Uzbekistan, UCell) are operating and cheap Nokia handsets are everywhere. Salary reality: an ordinary white-collar job in Tashkent in 2003–2005 pays $100–200 per month at official exchange rates, but wages are sometimes paid in cash at rates that reflect parallel-market dollar values.

## 2. Place-as-Character Entries

### Chirchiq (Чирчиқ) / Chirchiq Transformer Plant (Чирчикский трансформаторный завод)

Chirchiq sits on the Chirchiq River about 28 kilometers northeast of Tashkent, connected by a road that runs through the edge of the industrial belt and then into flat cotton country. The city was built as a Soviet industrial project in the late 1930s and its bones show it: long grid streets, factory-adjacent housing (завод-жилой квартал), a Khrushchyovka skyline. The population in the Soviet era was heavily Russian and Ukrainian — skilled workers and engineers brought in for the electrochemical and metallurgical plants. By the early 2000s many of those families have left; the Russian-speaking community is reduced but still present, particularly among older workers and those too embedded to easily extract.

The Chirchiq Transformer Plant (ЧТЗ, ChTZ) is a Soviet-era heavy industrial facility producing power transformers — large units for electricity grids. In its Soviet peak it employed thousands. By the early 2000s it operates at reduced but real capacity, still producing transformers for the Central Asian grid market and for export. The plant floor reality: transformer winding bays are the core of the work — enormous copper windings assembled on horizontal mandrels, the winding machines (обмоточные станки) running slowly and loudly. The smell is copper, cutting oil, and transformer varnish (пропитка, propitka) — a pungent lacquer smell that coats everything within fifty meters of the varnishing bays. Workers wear cotton overalls (спецовки, spetsovki), thick-soled boots, and in the winding bays often a light glove on the winding hand. The machinery is Soviet-vintage: built in the 1960s and 1970s, maintained rather than replaced. A young man on the floor in the early 2000s would earn approximately 30,000–60,000 som per month [CONFIRM], equivalent to $80–150 USD at the parallel market rate, paid partly in cash and partly with chronic delays. The workforce is mixed Russian-Uzbek, with Uzbek workers increasingly dominant in proportion; Russian-speaking workers occupy some of the older technical roles (senior winding operators, maintenance foremen) by tenure rather than formal policy. Shift culture: two shifts, 7 AM–4 PM and 4 PM–midnight, with a half-hour lunch brought from home or bought from the factory canteen (столовая). The canteen serves lagman (лагман, noodle soup), rice, and tea. There is a trade union committee building that no longer does anything a trade union does. The plant director's office has a portrait of Karimov and a portrait of the founding Soviet director. Both are expected.

### A Tashkent International Hotel, circa 2003 [CONFIRM which hotel — likely Sheraton Tashkent or Intercontinental]

The Sheraton Tashkent Hotel (now Le Meridien) on Buyuk Ipak Yoli [CONFIRM address] or the Intercontinental Tashkent on Amir Timur Square is the most plausible setting for a young Russian-speaker's first formal hospitality job. These are the international-brand hotels serving the diplomatic community, international business travelers (oil and gas sector, construction), and NGO workers in the early post-1999 period. The lobby is the first genuine first-world interior the protagonist may have entered: marble floors, a consistent temperature (air conditioning that actually works), Western goods in the gift shop, a lobby bar where beer costs $6 and a man on a $120/month local salary does not order beer.

The front desk is staffed by bilingual young Uzbeks and Russians — the hiring criterion is explicit: Russian fluency, English competency, Uzbek ability, and presentable appearance (the latter a hotel industry code with racial and class dimensions). For a young Russian-speaker from Chirchiq or a Tashkent Russian family with some English education, this desk represents a kind of class leap: the salary ($150–250/month for a junior agent, more for senior desk [CONFIRM]), the Western training materials, the contact with foreigners. The backstage of the hotel — staff locker rooms, the service corridor, the staff canteen (different from the guest restaurant) — is the real workplace. The foreign general manager operates on an entirely separate planet of expatriate salary and housing allowance; local staff do not discuss this, or if they do, it is with a practiced neutrality. The underground circulation of gossip: which diplomat tips well, which NGO worker is demanding, which guest leaves clothing for staff. Hotel work in Tashkent in this era is one of the few paths to dollar-denominated income for educated Russian-speakers without connections to the government apparatus.

### US Embassy Tashkent, Moyqorghon Street / Nukus Street area [CONFIRM current compound address]

The US Embassy Tashkent is located in the Yunusabad district on Moyqorghon Street 3 [CONFIRM — address has changed across years with compound expansions]. The compound is a walled fortress in the American embassy style post-Nairobi/Dar es Salaam: set-back from the street, concrete barriers, a Vehicle Inspection Point before the gate, local guard contractor staff (from a Uzbek security firm) at the outer perimeter. American diplomatic security officers (RSO staff) operate the inner perimeter. A Foreign Service National (FSN) employee — the local hire category — crosses through the outer checkpoint daily using a blue badge and goes no further into the compound than their section requires. The moat is real and architectural: FSN employees work in buildings within the compound but do not have access to the classified sections, the Marine guard post, or, typically, the ambassador's residence.

Post-Andijan (May 2005): the Andijan massacre fundamentally ruptures US-Uzbek relations. Karimov expels US forces from K2 airbase in July 2005, and the embassy shrinks accordingly — USAID programs are cut, public diplomacy budgets fall, and some FSN positions are eliminated or remain unfilled. For an FSN employee hired in the 2003–2005 window and retaining their position through the post-Andijan chill, the atmosphere shifts from cautious optimism (the K2 partnership years) to managed distance. The work: consular section FSN employees process visa applications, interview prep, document handling. Public affairs FSN employees support American Corners (reading rooms), cultural programs, press events. Economic section FSN employees conduct reporting on Uzbek economic conditions — collecting data the American diplomat cannot easily gather without a local interlocutor. The salary for an FSN in Tashkent in this era ranges from approximately $400–800/month [CONFIRM, highly variable by grade and section], which in the local economy is substantial — several times an ordinary professional salary — and comes with USGOV health benefits for the employee. The psychological position: you are trusted enough to be hired, not trusted enough to be cleared. You translate for people who hold your country's fate in their policy cables. You are instructed in American values programs. You watch visa applicants — people like your neighbors and family — be refused entry to a country whose compound you enter every morning.

### Ligovsky Prospekt area, St. Petersburg: A Specific Street, circa 2000

Ligovsky Prospekt (Лиговский проспект) runs south from Moskovsky Vokzal (the train station where trains from Moscow arrive) through a neighborhood that is simultaneously central and unglamorous — not the imperial showcase of Nevsky, not a suburb, but the working connective tissue of the city. A young man arriving from Tashkent might well enter St. Petersburg through Moskovsky Vokzal and find a room in the Ligovsky zone: the kommunalki here are dense, the residents a mix of elderly Leningraders who never left their allocated rooms and new arrivals from across the former USSR paying cash for a corner.

The building at a typical Ligovsky address: a late-19th century apartment block, six stories, entrance (парадная, paradnaya in Petersburg speech — not подъезд as Muscovites say) with its door code scratched into the plaster by a previous resident, stairwells that smell of cats and damp concrete. The kitchen shared by three or four tenants: a gas stove with independent burners claimed by household, a single refrigerator partitioned by shelf. The bathroom: a separate WC, a separate bathing room with a geyser water heater (колонка, kolonka) that requires lighting with a match. The street outside: a 24-hour produkty (продукты, food shop) in a former ground-floor apartment, selling beer, black bread, processed cheese, and a narrow selection of alcohol at any hour. Nearby: a shawarma stand open until 3 AM. The Ligovsky metro station on Nevsko-Vasileostrovskaya Line puts the city within reach. Young Petersburgers in 2000 are drinking Baltika beer (Балтика, numbered by strength: Baltika 3 is light, Baltika 9 is the strong one), wearing adidas and counterfeit branded jackets from the Avtovo market, listening to Zemfira and the early Russian hip-hop (Kasta, Ligalize). For a young man from Uzbekistan, this city is simultaneously free and exposing — no one knows you, which is liberation and loneliness in the same fact.

## 3. Period Transitions — What Changed Visibly

### August–September 1991: Independence

The red flags with the hammer and sickle come down from public buildings across Tashkent. In their place: a new Uzbek flag (light blue, white, and green with a crescent) appears on the same flagpoles. The physical flag change happens fast — within days. The Lenin statue in central Tashkent remains for now [CONFIRM removal date — believed 1992]. Soviet-branded shop signage begins changing slowly, over months. The Uzbek language intrudes more aggressively into public signage, which had been bilingual Russian/Uzbek. Streets are renamed — Soviet street names (Lenin, Marx, Soviet) give way to Uzbek heroes and dates. Post offices stop selling Soviet postage stamps. The money does not change yet; that horror comes later.

### 1994: Som Introduction

On July 1, 1994, the som (сўм) enters circulation. The immediate streetscape change: new paper money, smaller denominations than people are accustomed to, portraits of the new national architecture (the Uzbek coat of arms, historical sites). The sum-coupon is demonetized rapidly. At currency exchange points (обменные пункты) — which now operate both officially and informally — the new som trades at a rate that reveals immediately how distressed the economy is. The street-level result: prices stated in dollars, calculated in som, and everyone doing rapid mental arithmetic.

### February 1999: Tashkent Bombings

Checkpoints that existed in theory now exist in practice: police barriers at major intersections, document checks, a new pattern of random stops on pedestrians near government buildings. Within weeks: several prominent mosques reduced to supervised operation or closed for "renovation." Men known in their neighborhoods as devout — not extremist, simply observant — begin disappearing from social life, either arrested or sufficiently frightened to become invisible. A new caution enters everyday conversation about religion, politics, anything adjacent to criticism of the government. People lower their voices differently.

### October 2001: 9/11 and the K2 Alliance

The US-Uzbekistan basing deal (Karshi-Khanabad airbase, K2) is signed in the weeks after 9/11. For Tashkent's visible economy: an influx of American military and contractor personnel, more US diplomatic traffic, a brief improvement in the USAID-funded NGO sector. English becomes slightly more commercially viable as a skill. International hotel occupancy rises. This is noticed from inside the hotels and inside the embassies; it is felt less directly at the level of the street.

### May 2005: Andijan Rupture

The Andijan massacre of May 13, 2005 — government forces fire on a crowd in the Fergana Valley, killing hundreds (official figures disputed; credible estimates range from 500 to 1,500 [CONFIRM current scholarly consensus]) — produces an immediate chill in Tashkent that is felt in specific institutional ways. The K2 eviction in July follows. American faces become less common on the streets of central Tashkent. NGO offices close. For an FSN at the US Embassy: colleagues are let go, programs are cancelled, the daily calculus of being employed by the Americans shifts from advantage to complication. Uzbek state media runs a counter-narrative about foreign interference; this is the water in which FSN employees swim every day on their commute home.

1. **City of birth and childhood neighborhood** — This bible assumes Tashkent city proper or Chirchiq as the primary childhood location. If the family was in a different city (Fergana, Samarkand, Namangan, Bukhara) or a specific Tashkent district (Chilanzar, Yunusabad, Sobir Rahimov), the local texture shifts significantly. Which district? Which type of apartment block?

2. **The exact hotel** — The Tashkent international hotel career anchor is assumed to be Sheraton Tashkent or Intercontinental (the two dominant international-brand hotels of the early 2000s) [CONFIRM]. If the hotel was a different property (Dedeman, Radisson, Lotte City Hotel), the brand culture, management nationality, and guest mix differ.

3. **The Russia years itinerary** — The bible places St. Petersburg first (~1999–2001) then Moscow (~2001–2002). If the sequence was reversed, or if only one city was visited, or if there was a third location (Yekaterinburg, Novosibirsk, a smaller city where a relative lived), the place-as-character entries need reorientation.

4. **The Chirchiq connection** — The Chirchiq Transformer Plant is included because it is a historically real industrial employer and Chirchiq has a Russian-speaking heritage. But the protagonist's actual connection to it — was this a job he held, a place his father worked, a place he visited as a child, a place he returned to? — determines whether it is a chapter location or background detail.

5. **The Embassy role** — FSN roles vary enormously: consular (visa processing), public affairs, economic, administrative, security. The psychological and operational texture differs. Which section, and roughly what years?

6. **The route out** — Exit from Uzbekistan in 1999–2000 required an exit visa. How this was obtained (family connection, legitimate stated reason, bribe, or using an existing student/work visa) is a story beat worth preserving accurately.

7. **Language of home** — Was Russian the sole home language, or was there any Uzbek spoken? This affects the protagonist's relationship to Uzbek — fluent with accent, functional, or genuinely limited — which shapes his legibility in every Uzbek-language situation in the memoir.
