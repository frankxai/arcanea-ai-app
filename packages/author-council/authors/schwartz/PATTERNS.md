# Schwartz — Structural Patterns

## Reluctant-veteran protagonist

The Schwartz hero is almost always a man with history. Havald (Das Erste Horn), Zornblum in the Götterkriege — veterans who remember, who hesitate, who pay emotional price for killing. Their age and weight is structural.

## Three-faction-minimum politics

Never two-sided. The central conflict always has at least three factions, often five or more — each with internally legitimate reasons. The hero chooses among contested legitimacies, not between good and evil.

## Named-artifact constraint

Weapons carry histories. The history constrains what the wielder can ask of the weapon. The artifact remembers; this memory becomes plot.

## Institutional theology

Temples have bureaucracy. Priesthoods have factions. Orders have politics. A church in Schwartz is not a symbolic concept; it is a legal entity with budgets, hierarchies, and schisms. Faith is lived through institutions, not in spite of them.

## Götterkriege convergence

At the largest scale: human faction wars are shadows of god faction wars. The political is the mythic rendered at human scale. Gods do not descend and fight; their agents do.

## Legalistic clarity in dialogue

When characters argue, they argue like lawyers — stating claims, adducing evidence, acknowledging counter-arguments. This is not cold; it is how Germanic honor operates. Emotion underneath, precision on top.

## Combat-as-cost

Combat scenes are rarely satisfying. They are necessary, survived. The aftermath (grief, wound-care, reckoning) takes as many words as the fight. Violence without trauma-cost is rejected.
