# The second seat

Proposed scene in the creator-owned Tideglass world. All people, events, boat details and obligations below are new proposals; the three supplied world facts are preserved.

Mira heard the bridge go before she saw it. A thin hiss, like water poured over hot iron, reached the ferry landing. The tide had turned. Where the glass had joined the far bank, daylight showed through. Then the crossing was gone.

Three people waited on the other side. A man in a soaked wedding coat held a girl's hand. The third person had dragged a wooden chest to the steps and was trying to wave without letting go of its handle.

Mira pushed her boat out. The empty seats knocked against the thwarts. Two passengers, she reminded herself. Two, with her at the oars. She had painted the number on the gunwale because people always thought a small boat could carry one more person if the reason was good enough.

The man was calling before she reached them.

“My daughter first.”

The girl stood behind his coat, looking at the place the bridge had been. She was old enough to understand a missing thing, Mira thought, and young enough to keep looking for it.

The courier lifted the chest's lid. Inside, small bottles lay wrapped in wool.

“Fever medicine. East village. Before dark.”

Mira brought the bow against the lowest step and looped her rope around an iron ring. The water pulled the knot tight.

“Two seats,” she said. “And that chest stays.”

“I paid for passage.” The courier held out a damp receipt.

“For a parcel. That's freight.”

“It's what I could get.”

Mira looked at the girl's father. Across the channel, his wife stood beside the landing bell. She could see him. She could also see two empty seats.

There was a dry road behind the steps on this bank. The father could wait there; so could the courier. Neither waiting place made the argument easier.

“Take us,” the father said. “Come back for the box.”

The courier took a bottle out of its wrapping.

“There won't be another cart after sunset. If I miss this one, coming back for me tomorrow is no use.”

“Then go,” the father said. “Leave the box.”

The courier set the bottle down carefully. “Go with what?”

Mira could take the father and child together. The courier would lose the cart. Or she could take the child and courier, leaving the father to wait. The bottles could fit beneath a seat if the courier carried only what was needed tonight. The chest and its extra stock would have to stay.

She did not touch the receipt again.

“Wrap tonight's bottles in your coat,” she told the courier. “Your fare is returned. Put it toward getting someone back here for the chest.”

She counted the coins into the courier's palm. That was the money she had kept for a new oar collar. The old one slipped every time the channel pulled sideways.

The father tightened his hand around the girl's.

“I can wait with you,” she said.

He looked across at his wife, then at the courier kneeling beside the open chest.

“No. You go.” He crouched and buttoned the girl's coat wrong, one button too high. She let him.

Mira held the boat while they boarded. The courier tucked the wrapped bottles beneath the forward seat. The girl sat behind, facing her father.

Mira counted the two of them aloud, untied the rope and pushed off.

At the first pull, the worn collar slipped. She caught the oar before its blade struck the courier's knee and settled it back into place. The girl had not looked away from the bank.

“Will you go back?” she asked.

Mira turned the boat toward the landing bell. “After I put you ashore.”

She pulled again, keeping her thumb over the collar this time.
