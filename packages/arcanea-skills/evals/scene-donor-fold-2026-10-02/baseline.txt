Mira saw the bridge from mid-channel and watched it begin to go.

It ran from Gannet Stair out to the black rock called the Pell, a pale span with the dusk showing through it. The seaward end had already clouded like breath on a window. Three figures stood at the rock end, not crossing. She swung the skiff round and pulled.

By the time she reached the lee ledge, the bridge was glittering grit on the water and sinking, and the flood had started to lean on her hull. Odo Venn, the grower, sat with both hands wrapped in his shirt, the cloth gone dark. Hesk, who sold salt on the Stair, stood with her bad hip cocked against the wind. The boy, Tam, held Odo's sleeve.

"I misjudged the turn," Odo said. "Take Hesk and the boy. Come back for me. I can hold the ladder."

"With those?" Hesk said.

"I'm not going without him," said Tam.

"Nobody's asking you to be brave," Mira said. "I'm asking you to be light."

She took inventory. The skiff carried four on paper, but the stern was stacked with four casks of lamp oil for the Marrow Quay warden, half paid already. With them aboard, she could take two passengers and no more. That meant two crossings, twenty minutes each, while the Narrows quickened. The Pell's crown would be shin-deep by the second trip, and a man with ruined hands would be waiting on an iron ladder in the dark.

"Can't you grow another span?" Hesk snapped at Odo.

"Not before low water. Past midnight."

Tam looked at Mira. "Could you?"

"I keep a ferry," she said. "I couldn't grow a bead of it."

A swell lifted the skiff and set it down hard. The landing slab, the only flat footing on the ledge, slid under a sheet of green water. Hesk's foot went with it. Mira caught her wrist, took the old woman's weight against the gunwale, and felt the hull grind on stone. When Hesk was in the boat, shaking, the ledge was a wet slope with no step left in it. Fetching Odo off it a second time, in the dark, with the flood running, was no longer a plan she believed in.

"Leave the oil," Hesk said, settling on the thwart as if she'd been consulted.

"It isn't yours to tell me to leave."

"It isn't yours either. You'll owe him half." Hesk's voice was flat. "Better owing than rowing back for a corpse."

"Go, Mira," Odo said. "Boy first. I'll not be the reason you're out here twice."

"You're the reason I'm out here once." She stepped onto the ledge, braced a boot against the rock, and rolled the first cask off the stern. It thudded onto the stone and sat there, black and heavy and not hers. She rolled the second, then the third. The fourth she held a moment longer than she needed to, then let it go. The flood would find them within the hour. They might float to the Spit. They might not.

"Odo, stern, hands in your lap. Don't use them for anything." She hauled him in by the belt. "Tam, the tin under your seat. Bail when I say, not before."

The skiff sat low, a hand's width of freeboard and the chop slapping at it. Mira set the oars and looked across at the Stair, where one lantern had been lit.

"Hesk," she said, "when we land, send your grandson along the Spit at first light. Four casks, oak, Quay brand. If he finds them, I'll split the warden's finder fee with him."

"Half," Hesk said.

"A third."

"Done." Hesk shut her eyes.

Mira pulled.
